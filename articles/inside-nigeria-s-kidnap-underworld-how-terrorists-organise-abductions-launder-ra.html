Inside Nigeria’s kidnap underworld: How terrorists organise abductions, launder ransom

By The Sun Nigeria The Sun Nigeria 3d ago 11 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: The Sun Nigeria

• Ex-NIA DG exposes network of terrorists, bandits, informants, financial facilitators

• Recounts how recovered Chibok girl sought to return to terrorist husband

• N7.78bn ransom paid from July 2025 to June 2026

By Beifoh Osewele

Former Director-General of the National Intelligence Agency (NIA), Ahmed Rufai Abubakar, has blown the lid off the sprawling criminal machinery behind Nigeria’s multibillion-naira kidnapping industry, exposing how terrorists, bandit militias, informants and financial facilitators operate an intricate and elaborate network that identifies victims, tracks security operatives, coordinates mass abductions and launders huge ransom payments through cryptocurrencies and small financial outfits.

Abubakar revealed that behind the gunmen who storm communities, abduct schoolchildren and seize travellers lies a sophisticated web of collaborators whose activities have turned kidnapping into a major source of financing for terrorist operations, recruitment, propaganda and prisoner exchanges, with criminal gangs and extremist organisations increasingly pooling resources to sustain the deadly enterprise.

He disclosed that the scale of the illicit business extends to millions of dollars, citing the March 2022 Abuja–Kaduna train attack, during which kidnappers allegedly collected between $1 million and $2 million in ransom payments that he said were channelled to the terrorist organisation, Ansaru.

In another startling revelation, the former intelligence chief recounted how a Chibok schoolgirl rescued after years in terrorist captivity, during which she was married successively to three fighters, expressed a desire to return to her captors despite being brought to Abuja for medical examinations and debriefing.

Abubakar made the disclosures during a panel discussion at the eighth Global Security Forum in Doha, Qatar, held from October 19 to 21, 2026, under the theme: “The Future of Alliances,” where security and intelligence experts examined the growing convergence of terrorism, organised crime and hostage-taking.

Organised by The Soufan Centre and the Qatar International Academy for Security Studies, the forum brought together senior officials and security experts from more than 100 countries to examine international cooperation, emerging threats and the growing influence of non-state actors.

Informants identify victims, collaborators track security agents

Abubakar said the kidnapping network extended beyond the armed men who seized victims, with informants and other collaborators providing information essential to their operations.

“You have the perpetrators in three different categories. You have the motive behind, but you have a very strong network of logistics providers, and that is the informants.

“They use a lot of informants that tell them about their targets, their potential targets.”

Other collaborators, he said, provided logistical support, including intelligence on the movements of law enforcement personnel, enabling the perpetrators to evade security operations and sustain their activities.

“There are also other criminal support logistics that actually help them by informing them about the movement of law enforcement agents. So, this is what makes the ecosystem of kidnapping, hostage taking, mass abduction in Nigeria.”

How ransom is laundered

The former intelligence chief said the collection of substantial ransom payments had created a need for financial channels through which the perpetrators could conceal and move their proceeds.

“You collect ransom, huge ransoms, and you have to now launder the money,” he said, identifying cryptocurrencies and small financial outfits among the channels used. “Small financial outfits are used to launder this money.”

He placed the financial facilitators within the broader support structure sustaining terrorist organisations, bandit militias and other criminal gangs, whose objectives increasingly overlapped.

Abubakar cited the March 28, 2022, attack on the Abuja–Kaduna passenger train as an example of the financial benefits terrorist organisations derive from mass abductions.

He estimated that the kidnappers collected between $1 million and $2 million in ransom payments, which he said were channelled to Ansaru, a terrorist organisation with links to al-Qaeda.

Gunmen had attacked the Kaduna-bound passenger train after explosives were detonated on the railway track, killing passengers, injuring others and abducting dozens.

The hostages were subsequently released in batches, with the remaining 23 regaining their freedom on October 5, 2022, following efforts by security agencies.

The attack forced the suspension of passenger services on the route until December 5, 2022, when operations resumed under additional security arrangements.

Terrorists, bandits join forces

Abubakar identified terrorist organisations, bandit militias and other criminal gangs as the principal categories of perpetrators, explaining that their motives and methods had evolved over time.

According to him, terrorist groups such as Boko Haram abduct people for several purposes, including fundraising, recruiting young men as fighters and taking girls and women as spouses.

“They do targeted kidnappings individually. Initially, they did it to fundraise, and then, of course, also to recruit from the young men to fill their ranks of fighters, and then also recruit from young girls and women for purposes of having partners or spouses.

“And they go directly to communities and villages and then take what they want and then they go.”

Bandit militias, he explained, were principally driven by money, carrying out both targeted kidnappings and mass abductions. “They also do targeted kidnappings. They go directly into houses, to communities, and so on, and take victims.”

He added that the militias also engaged in cattle rustling and received support from gunrunners, distinguishing their financial motivations from the ideological objectives of terrorist organisations.

“They are not ideologically driven. They have no ideology. They want to enjoy life,” he said. “Their paradise is here.”

Despite their different motivations, Abubakar said terrorist groups and bandit militias increasingly collaborate to carry out mass kidnappings.

“Then we graduated to a situation where we now see alignment of, for instance, terrorist groups working with militia groups to conduct especially mass kidnapping.

“They do that for dual purposes: first of all to fundraise, to get money, and then, of course, also to serve as propaganda.

“So there is a political motive over there for them to be known, for the government to know that they are active, especially when the pushback from the government forces was very strong.”

JNIM, Ansaru and the Sahel connection

Abubakar identified Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin (JNIM) and Ansaru among the terrorist organisations linked to kidnapping operations involving bandit militias.

JNIM, commonly translated as the Group for the Support of Islam and Muslims, is an al-Qaeda-affiliated terrorist organisation operating across parts of the Sahel, including Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger.

The organisation emerged in March 2017 following the merger of four extremist groups: Ansar al-Din, al-Murabitun, the Macina Liberation Front and the Sahara branch of al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb (AQIM).

The United Nations placed JNIM on its sanctions list on October 4, 2018, following its involvement in terrorist attacks against military formations, international forces and civilian targets.

Ansaru, a separate Nigeria-based terrorist organisation, announced its existence in January 2012 after breaking away from Boko Haram. The United Nations identifies the group as having operational connections with AQIM, including training and attack planning, and placed it on its sanctions list on June 26, 2014.

A 2022 United Nations monitoring report recorded that Ansaru pledged allegiance to JNIM on December 31, 2021, although subsequent UN reporting indicated that the group had struggled to provide operational support to the Sahel-based organisation.

The report also raised concerns about possible operational connections involving terrorist organisations, criminal networks and former Boko Haram operatives in Kaduna, Katsina, Niger and Zamfara states.

Kidnapping for ransom has also been identified as a source of financing for JNIM. In April 2024, the United States Department of State announced sanctions against individuals linked to the organisation’s hostage-taking activities, identifying ransom payments, extortion and proceeds from smugglers and traffickers among its funding sources.

Abubakar further disclosed that terrorist organisations maintained links with sponsors outside Nigeria, enabling them to mobilise funds for their operations and expansion.

He identified Nigeria’s large population and economy as factors making the country attractive to terrorist organisations seeking financial resources.

N7.78bn ransom payments in one year

The scale of Nigeria’s kidnapping economy was further illustrated by findings from security consultancy, SBM Intelligence, which reported that at least N7.78 billion was paid in ransom between July 2025 and June 2026, compared with N2.56 billion in the preceding reporting period.

In its report, ‘The Economics of Nigeria’s Kidnap Industry, 2026’, the consultancy said 7,825 people were abducted in 1,713 kidnapping and related incidents during the period, while at least 1,142 people were killed.

According to the report, the Jama’atu Ahlis Sunna Lidda’awati Wal-Jihad faction of Boko Haram accounted for approximately 90 per cent of recorded ransom payments.

The consultancy called for stronger action against the financial networks sustaining insurgency and the economic conditions that continued to supply recruits to criminal organisations.

Schoolchildren, worshippers seized for prisoner exchanges

Abubakar said the objectives of mass abductions had increasingly extended beyond financial gain to securing the release of detained members of terrorist and criminal organisations.

“Now, currently, what we have is mass kidnapping of schoolchildren that is going on. Mass kidnapping of worshippers, either Muslims or Christians, is also going on.

“And in most cases perpetrated by bandits or terrorist groups, and the purpose now has evolved to prisoner exchange.

“So they do it because they have their friends, their comrades that have been arrested, and they want them freed.”

He explained that such demands often complicated negotiations, particularly where the abductors insisted on the release of associates who had already been killed during attacks or security operations.

“So that makes negotiation, hostage diplomacy very difficult,” he said.

Why negotiations go through back channels

Asked how diplomatic, legal and law enforcement tools could be applied to different hostage situations, Abubakar said the appropriate response depended on the identity of the abductors, their objectives and the circumstances surrounding each incident. “Different diagnosis, different medication,” he said.

He explained that while some terrorist abductions were principally intended to make political statements, others involved financial demands or attempts to secure prisoner exchanges, requiring different approaches to negotiations.

Where the captors communicated financial demands, he said, negotiations could require discreet engagement through trusted intermediaries.

“You have to use the hostage diplomacy. And it has to be through the back channel.

“There are groups, some of them foreign groups like the HD and others, and then internal groups, and some contact persons that the terrorists trust.”

He said such engagements were conducted quietly, without public disclosure of the details of negotiations or any ransom payments.

“This is a high level of hostage diplomacy negotiations,” he said.

Recovered Chibok girl wanted to return

Recounting the Chibok girl’s experience, Abubakar said security officials had continued tracking the abducted girls over the years, recovering them in small groups through liaison channels and security operations.

“There are also situations where they abduct people, intending to keep them, as happened with the Chibok girls, who were taken in 2014, relatively early in the group’s activities.

“They abduct people for several reasons, but in that case, a major objective was to make a statement. They entered a girls’ school and took the students. There was public outrage, but they kept the girls and remained silent.

“Some of the girls escaped, while some died. Many were retained and distributed among the groups, and they were married off to fighters.”

On efforts to recover the girls, he said: “We kept on all these years actually keeping track. And we got them in batches of two, three, four, depending on circumstances and situations, police, military raid and so on.

“But this particular case, we got across this lady. We brought her to Abuja, kept her for about two weeks, exposing her to medical checks and debriefing and so on. And what we found was very interesting.

“She went through three different husbands. The first two were killed in combat.”

He said the young woman was married to a third fighter when security officials recovered her.

According to him, a zonal commander within the terrorist organisation had adopted the young woman as a daughter and arranged successive marriages for her whenever her husband was killed.

“Now, the interesting thing is she had very good things to say about her godfather, that he was a very kind man.,” Abubakar said.

He said she wanted to see her parents but also expressed a desire to return to where she had been held.

He cited the experience to illustrate the complexities security officials encountered in recovering people who had spent prolonged periods in the custody of terrorist organisations.
