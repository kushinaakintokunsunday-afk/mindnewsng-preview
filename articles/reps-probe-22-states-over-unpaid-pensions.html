Reps probe 22 states over unpaid pensions

By Punch Punch 6d ago 8 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The House of Representatives has called on the Federal Government to prevail on state governments owing pensioners to clear their outstanding retirement benefit

The House also resolved to investigate the failure of 22 states to pay retirement benefits regularly, following concerns that thousands of elderly retirees are being subjected to hunger, poverty and preventable deaths as a result of unpaid pensions.

The resolutions followed the adoption of a motion of urgent public importance sponsored by the member representing Apapa Federal Constituency of Lagos State, Adesola Adebayo, at plenary on Tuesday.

Moving the motion, the All Progressives Congress lawmaker cited a newspaper report indicating that only 14 of Nigeria’s 36 states pay pensions regularly, while the remaining 22 owe retirees several months or, in some cases, years of entitlements.

He described pensions as an earned right rather than a privilege or charity, insisting that retirees who had devoted between 30 and 35 years to public service deserved to enjoy their benefits without unnecessary hardship.

The lawmaker expressed concern that many pensioners aged between 70 and 90 were dying in poverty because they could not afford food, medication, rent and other basic necessities.

He also raised concerns over the practice of subjecting elderly retirees to prolonged verification exercises, forcing some to queue for days under harsh weather conditions, with reports of pensioners collapsing and dying in the process.

Adebayo argued that the non-payment of pensions violated constitutional provisions and undermined human dignity, while creating anxiety among serving public workers who could begin to regard retirement as a death sentence.

He said, “The House notes, with grave concern, a scandalous report published by a public newspaper revealing that only 14 out of the 36 states of the federation, excluding the Federal Capital Territory, pay pensions regularly to their retired workers, while the remaining 22 states owe pensions several months and, in some cases, several years.

“The House also notes that the pension is not a privilege, favour, or charity, but a defined wage, a constitutional right, and a moral debt owed to citizens who spent their productive lives, 30 to 35 years, serving their fatherland as teachers, nurses, civil servants, police officers, soldiers, and other public servants.

“The House further notes that the 1999 Constitution (as amended) explicitly provides that a person who has held office in the public service of the federation or a state shall be entitled to pensions and gratuity as prescribed by the National Assembly or the House of Assembly of the state, and that such pensions shall not be delayed or altered to the disadvantage of the pensioner.”

The lawmaker lamented that “many states have failed to remit both employer and employee contributions, failed to fund accrued rights, failed to domesticate the Act, and have left thousands of retirees without access to their retirement savings accounts.”

He added that “many elderly pensioners, some aged 70, 80, and 90, are now dying in penury, unable to afford food, medication, rent, and medical care, while others are forced to queue for days under the sun and rain at pension verification centres with some collapsing and dying in the process.”

He further argued that the non-payment of pensions constituted a breach of the Constitution, “a violation of human dignity, a threat to national security, and a discouragement to serving workers, who now see retirement as a death sentence, and all other than blood of rest.”

Lawmakers divided

The motion, however, generated disagreements among lawmakers over the constitutional powers of the National Assembly to intervene in pension matters involving state governments.

Contributing to the debate, APC lawmaker representing a constituency in Akwa Ibom State, Mark Esset, opposed the motion, arguing that pension obligations at the state level fell within the jurisdiction of state governments and their respective Houses of Assembly.

He cautioned against any suggestion that the Federal Government could deduct money from states’ allocations to settle outstanding pension liabilities, describing such an approach as unconstitutional.

Esset urged the House to concentrate on pension challenges facing military personnel, who, he said, had repeatedly protested over their entitlements.

“I understand the patriotism of the mover of the motion but these are purely state matters. Emotions are not enough for this motion to be heard by the House. The issue of pensions at the state level are purely for the State Houses of Assembly to handle,” he said.

Similarly, Dominic Okafor (APGA, Anambra) cautioned against generalising the allegations against all states, noting that some governors had fulfilled their pension obligations.

He cited Anambra State, which he said had cleared its pension liabilities, arguing that the motion should distinguish between states that had failed to pay and those meeting their responsibilities.

On his part, Billy Osawaru (APC, Edo) urged the House not to abandon the motion, proposing amendments to remove provisions that fell outside its constitutional jurisdiction.

“We know what our fathers are going through. We know what it means for a man to work for 35 years only to be called to stand in the sun, under the sun, to review, to keep reviewing until he can’t stand it no more.

“So, Mr Speaker, instead of killing this beautiful motion, I would like to go in for amendments. I would like to take out areas our mandates don’t cover and go with those areas that our mandates cover.”

The Deputy Speaker, Benjamin Kalu, who presided over the plenary, subsequently directed lawmakers to examine the House Standing Orders to establish the jurisdiction of the Committee on Pensions.

Reading the committee’s mandate, Kalu noted that it was empowered to examine pension-related issues across the public service, recommend measures to ensure the timely payment of retirement benefits and investigate employers’ failure to meet their pension obligations.

Its responsibilities, he said, included oversight of the National Pension Commission (PenCom), the Military Pension Board and other relevant institutions.

Also speaking, Terseer Ugbor (APC, Benue) urged the House to allow the motion to proceed, arguing that the suffering of pensioners across the country warranted urgent legislative attention.

Another lawmaker, Zainab Gimba, also supported the motion, citing Section 210 of the 1999 Constitution, as amended, which protects the pension rights of persons in the public service of a state.

She particularly drew attention to Section 210(2), which provides that pension benefits due to a person under the law shall not be withheld or altered to the person’s disadvantage, except as permitted by law.

Gimba argued that although state pension liabilities must be distinguished from federal obligations, the National Assembly had the constitutional authority to debate the issue, make recommendations and encourage state legislators to exercise the necessary oversight.

She warned that continued failure to pay pensions could trigger humanitarian challenges, undermine the dignity of retirees and negatively affect their families and society.

“The law says that pensioners should be paid both at the national and state level. It is an entitlement protected by the Constitution. We must not forget that the condition of pensioners today can cause a humanitarian crisis,” she said.

The House Leader, Prof Julius Ihonvbere, also defended the motion, insisting that lawmakers should not shy away from addressing the plight of Nigerians simply because some of the affected matters fell within the jurisdiction of state governments.

Ihonvbere argued that members of the National Assembly represented Nigerians across the country and had a responsibility to speak out when citizens were subjected to hardship.

He maintained that the House could intervene through motions, investigations and recommendations without necessarily usurping the constitutional powers of state governments.

Recalling previous interventions by the Ninth Assembly in matters involving state governments, he said the parliament had a responsibility to engage relevant authorities where circumstances demanded it.

“We need to be very careful not to diminish our powers and responsibilities by hiding behind some of the local sections, under the Standing Order of the Constitution, not to perform our duties.

“Technically, there is nothing in which this House or the National Assembly cannot engage in, whether they are in the local government, the state, or anywhere, because we represent the entire country,” he maintained.

He further called on governors who had yet to clear outstanding pension liabilities to fulfil their obligations, insisting that the availability of public funds should not be an excuse for abandoning retired workers.

He also commended governors who had maintained regular pension payments, urging others to follow their example.

Following the deliberations, the House unanimously adopted the motion and resolved to call on the Federal Government, through the Office of the Secretary to the Government of the Federation, to prevail on state governments to prioritise the payment of pensions.

It also mandated its Committee on Pensions to interface with PenCom and engage state governments to ascertain the challenges responsible for irregular pension payments, identify possible solutions and report its findings to the House.

The investigation is expected to provide the House with a clearer picture of pension arrears across the affected states and the institutional and financial challenges responsible for the delays.

Nigeria operates a contributory pension system under the Pension Reform Act, with PenCom regulating and supervising the pension industry. However, pension arrangements and obligations at the state level involve state-specific legal and administrative frameworks, making the question of federal legislative intervention a subject of debate.

Section 210 of the 1999 Constitution guarantees pension and gratuity entitlements for persons who have served in the public service of a state, while Section 173 provides similar protection for federal public servants. Both provisions underscore the constitutional recognition of retirement benefits as an entitlement rather than a discretionary payment.
