‘Stablecoins ease cross-border payment burden’

By Punch Punch 1d ago 12 min read

Timon CEO Oluwatomi Ayorinde tells JUSTICE OKAMGBA how stablecoins and local partnerships are streamlining African cross-border payments and reducing dollar dependence through local settlements and PAPSS

What key challenges in Africa’s cross-border payment systems is Timon addressing through stablecoins, and what gaps remain in existing platforms such as PAPSS?

The core problem in African cross-border payments is convertibility. Too often, a business or individual holding one African currency can’t easily exchange it for another, so the payment has to go through the US dollar first. That extra step adds cost and delay, and for many people the dollars are hard to access in the first place. Today, USD stablecoins are the most practical fix. They give people a way to hold value and move it across borders without waiting on scarce dollar liquidity, and Timon uses them to help customers access and spend their money across borders, including through card payments. However, this still leaves African currencies dependent on the dollar as the bridge between them. Local-currency stablecoins could reduce that dependence by enabling direct exchange between African currencies. PAPSS, created by Afreximbank, is already laying the groundwork, with its payment rail connecting African markets and its African Currency Marketplace enabling direct exchange between their currencies. Timon was part of the first cohort of the Afreximbank Accelerator Programme, and we actually plan to build with PAPSS as this infrastructure develops, bringing those benefits to individuals and businesses through the cards, accounts and payment methods they already use. We see our work as complementary to the broader infrastructure being developed across Africa, with partnerships helping to make cross-border financial services more accessible and practical for customers.

Can you illustrate with an actual transaction how Timon’s stablecoin infrastructure has improved the speed, cost and efficiency of cross-border payments compared with conventional channels?

I travel to Kenya often for business, and my own experience is the simplest example. Most businesses there prefer M-Pesa, so before Timon, I had to exchange my naira for physical US dollars, get a Kenyan SIM, and set up an M-Pesa account. Topping it up meant finding an agent each time and took about 30 minutes because of the physical interaction. When I used Kenyan ATMs to withdraw cash with my international card, each transaction could cost as much as KSh 1,000, depending on the bank. Today, I top up from my Nigerian account and send directly to M-Pesa accounts whenever I need to. It takes less than three minutes and costs far less than those card fees. Behind the scenes, Timon uses USD stablecoins to move the value between the two markets, so I no longer have to carry cash, withdraw from ATMs or rely on an agent. The next step is to remove the dollar from the middle of that journey. USD stablecoins still have to be converted into Kenyan shillings, but a shilling-backed stablecoin could allow settlement into M-Pesa without that extra conversion, which is where we see this heading.

How does the settlement process work when a business in one African country pays another across the continent? What role does the stablecoin play, and at what stage are local currencies introduced?

Timon is currently focused primarily on consumers rather than operating as a business-to-business settlement network, so it is important to distinguish our existing product from the broader possibilities for cross-border payments. Conceptually, a stablecoin can serve as an intermediate settlement asset in a cross-border transaction.

A sender could initiate a payment using an available local currency or payment method, value could move between providers using a stablecoin, and the recipient could receive funds in their preferred local currency. Whether this approach is suitable depends on the markets involved, available liquidity, the participating providers, and applicable regulatory requirements. We see potential for different payment and settlement mechanisms to work together, but the specific arrangements depend on the infrastructure and partnerships supporting each transaction. Our current focus remains on making cross-border financial services more accessible and convenient for individual users.

You have highlighted stablecoins as a solution to liquidity challenges among African currencies. What are the major constraints in the current system, and how does Timon’s model address them?

The specific problem is that there is very little direct liquidity between most pairs of African currencies.

 For example, not many people actively trade naira for Kenyan shillings at any given moment, so providers usually convert through the US dollar. That adds cost and exposes the payment to dollar shortages, and providers often have to keep money pre-funded in each market, which ties up capital. Timon uses what’s often called a stablecoin sandwich, which means we don’t have to hold balances in each market ourselves. When I send money from Nigeria to M-Pesa, the naira is converted into USD stablecoins, which move across the border at any hour. A local liquidity provider in Kenya then accepts those stablecoins and pays out in shillings. Because we work with multiple providers on an ongoing basis, we can get the best available rate and avoid relying on any single one, and connecting each market to the same stablecoin bridge means we don’t need a deep direct market for every currency pair. That structure makes the liquidity problem far easier to manage. Without pre-funded balances to maintain, we can open new corridors faster and avoid locking up capital in every market we serve. Rates still depend on how deep local liquidity provider markets are, and regulatory requirements still apply in each market, but that is a much lighter constraint than funding each market ourselves. The next stage is direct exchange between African currencies, which is why we’re interested in local-currency stablecoins and in PAPSS’s African Currency Marketplace. As those markets deepen, more transactions can settle without going through the dollar.

How does Timon intend to complement PAPSS rather than compete with it? Could stablecoins serve as an underlying settlement mechanism for local-currency transactions processed through platforms such as PAPSS?

PAPSS and Timon work at different layers, so there’s little to compete over. PAPSS, created by Afreximbank, is building the rails and the currency marketplace that let African markets pay and exchange directly with each other. Timon builds the customer-facing products on top, the cards, accounts, and transfers that let individuals and businesses actually use their money across borders. Our approach is to plug into infrastructure like PAPSS rather than duplicate it, just as we work with local liquidity providers in each market instead of holding balances ourselves. On the second question, yes, we believe stablecoins could become that settlement layer. Today, we use USD stablecoins to bridge markets, which works but still keeps the dollar in the middle of every transaction. Local-currency stablecoins, such as naira- or shilling-backed ones, could let value settle directly between African currencies, and PAPSS’s African Currency Marketplace is already moving in that direction with stablecoin-based exchange. If those two develop together, a payment from Lagos to Nairobi could settle end to end in local currencies, with PAPSS providing the rails and Timon delivering it to the customer. Our time in the first cohort of the Afreximbank Accelerator Programme reinforced that view, and we plan to build alongside PAPSS as this infrastructure matures.

Which African currencies and markets does Timon currently cover, and what volume of cross-border transactions has the platform handled to date?

Timon has more than 100,000 users across 16 African countries, with Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana and South Africa among our strongest markets. Our platform offers multi-currency settlement, cross-border transfers, virtual and physical cards, and other services designed to help customers access and use their money across markets. To date, we have processed approximately $50m in transaction volume, with monthly transaction volume currently exceeding $4m. We are focused on making our services useful to customers whose financial needs extend beyond a single market, while continuing to develop the product and infrastructure needed to support them.

What are the major regulatory challenges facing stablecoin-based cross-border payments in Africa, particularly regarding licensing, foreign exchange regulations, consumer protection and anti-money laundering compliance?

Honestly, the hardest part is that every market plays by different rules. Some countries have clear stablecoin regulation, some are still figuring it out, and one payment from Lagos to Nairobi can touch both. So you can’t build one model and copy it across the continent. What works in Nigeria might need a different licence or a different partner in Kenya. FX controls are the other big one. Many African countries keep a close eye on money going in and out, and stablecoins don’t get a pass, so every conversion and payout has to fit each country’s rules. KYC and consumer protection are similar. Customers want the same experience wherever they are, but the requirements change from market to market, which is why we do dedicated local KYC for local top-ups. If you’re funding your account in a market, you’re verified to that market’s standard. We handle this by working with licensed merchants of record and local partners in each market, including the liquidity providers who pay out in local currency. Every part of the transaction sits with someone properly licensed to do it. It’s slower than pretending one set of rules applies everywhere, but it’s the only way to build something that lasts. I do think the rules will get clearer and more aligned over time, and that’s what local-currency stablecoins and systems like PAPSS will need to really take off.

Given that stablecoins are largely dollar-backed and may deepen dollar dependence, how do you address concerns that their adoption could weaken African currencies instead of strengthening local currency markets?

It’s a fair concern, and I don’t think anyone building in this space should dismiss it. But it helps to separate the demand for dollars from the tools people use to get them. That demand was here long before stablecoins. When a currency loses value quickly, people look for ways to protect what they’ve earned, and they’ll find one whether it’s cash under the mattress, a domiciliary account or a stablecoin. Stablecoins didn’t create that need, and they can’t fix the conditions behind it. Where I think we can genuinely help African currencies is in how they relate to each other. Right now, many intra-African payments go through the dollar simply because there’s no better bridge, even when neither side wants dollars. We use USD stablecoins for that today because they’re the most practical option available, but we see them as the bridge, not the destination. Local-currency stablecoins, alongside systems like PAPSS, could let a naira settle directly into the West African CFA franc without the dollar in the middle, which would reduce reliance on the dollar for trade within Africa. So, the effect really depends on how these tools are used and how each market’s policy develops. We’d rather be part of that conversation with regulators than build around it, because the version that lasts supports African currencies rather than replacing them.

What key changes are needed in Africa’s financial infrastructure to create a unified payments market across the continent, and which major challenge should be addressed first?

There’s an old economic idea that explains a lot of this. The economist Williams Stanley Jevons called it the “double coincidence of wants”: the problem that trade happens only when both sides want what the other has at the same moment. Very few people want to swap naira for Malawian kwacha directly at any given time, so the market is thin, and everyone routes through the dollar. Krugman showed in 1980 that this is how a vehicle currency locks in, because the more people trade through it, the cheaper it gets. So the bottleneck to solve first is convertibility, and Asia offers a useful lesson. After the 1997 crisis, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand appointed specific banks to quote direct rates between their currencies, deliberately creating market makers for pairs the market wasn’t serving. They also built local-currency bond markets, which gave investors a reason to hold those currencies. Connecting their payment systems came later. What might work in Africa is a similar approach, with designated liquidity providers quoting direct rates on corridors like naira to kwacha, supported by central banks and institutions like Afreximbank. PAPSS pairing its payment system with the African Currency Marketplace is an important step in that direction, and local-currency stablecoins could make that exchange cheaper still. None of it works without stable macro policy, but once direct pairs beat the detour, the same loop that locked in the dollar can start working for African currencies.

Since joining Afreximbank’s Flagship Accelerator Programme, what progress has Timon made in securing partnerships, engaging regulators, attracting funding and advancing its pan-African expansion plans?

The biggest shift has been in who we’re talking to. Before the programme, most of our conversations were with card networks, liquidity providers and fintech partners. Afreximbank opened doors to a different tier, including trade finance institutions, central banks and regulatory contacts across several markets, and the wider Afreximbank ecosystem, including PAPSS. Those relationships take years to build on your own, and the programme compressed that. It also changed how we think about expansion. We support payments in 16+ African countries today and were planning to reach 40 soon, but the programme pushed us to focus on trade corridors rather than just adding countries. That’s a big part of why we’re looking to build more for businesses soon, aimed at SMEs moving money between African markets every day. Afreximbank is also investing in Timon, which matters beyond the capital itself. Having the continent’s trade bank behind us gives partners and regulators more confidence in what we’re building. Our priority now is turning these relationships into live products and growing market by market as customer demand and each regulator’s requirements allow.
