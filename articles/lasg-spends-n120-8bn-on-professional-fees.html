LASG spends N120.8bn on ‘professional fees’

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Lagos State Government recorded N120.8bn in expenditure on a single budget item classified as “Professional Fees Expenses” between January and September 202

According to the state’s third-quarter budget performance report, released by the Ministry of Economic Planning and Budget and obtained and reviewed by The PUNCH, “Professional Fees Expenses” is listed as a distinct expenditure item with an annual allocation of N227.3bn.

The report’s cumulative expenditure of approximately N120.8bn represents about 53.1 per cent of the allocation, accounting for almost a quarter of the state’s total overhead spending of N498.9bn during the nine-month period.

The scale of this spending is more than 15 times the reported primary healthcare expenditure and over 11 times the basic education figure. Primary healthcare expenditure was approximately N7.8bn against an annual allocation of N19.2bn, while basic education recorded expenditure of about N10.5bn against a budget of N14.1bn.

The Lagos State Government’s third-quarter budget performance report does not provide a breakdown of the N120.8bn by recipient, assignment, contract, procurement process, or approving authority, leaving the specific services covered by the expenditure unclear and raising concerns about transparency and accountability.

The figures reveal a sharp acceleration in the third quarter, when expenditure on professional fees rose to N56.5bn from N32.2bn in the preceding quarter, an increase of approximately 75.5 per cent based on the reported figures.

The third-quarter figure represents nearly half of the total professional fees expenditure for the first nine months of 2026. The increase came at a time of heightened political activity ahead of the next electoral cycle, raising questions about the nature and timing of the expenditure, particularly as overall overhead spending declined during the same period.

According to the documents reviewed, Lagos recorded overhead expenditure of approximately N191bn in the second quarter, compared with N173.4bn in the third quarter, representing a decline of about 9.2 per cent.

The disparity becomes more pronounced when the professional fees item is excluded. Overhead expenditure on all other items fell from approximately N158.8bn in the second quarter to N116.9bn in the third quarter, a decline of about 26.4 per cent.

The figures show that the professional fees item moved sharply against the broader spending trend, accounting for a substantially larger proportion of the state’s recurrent expenditure in the third quarter.

The scale of the expenditure, its sharp quarterly increase and the absence of a corresponding breakdown raise questions about the nature of the services procured, the agencies responsible for the commitments, and the approval and accountability mechanisms governing the expenditure.

The PUNCH made repeated attempts over several days to obtain clarification from the relevant government offices on the expenditure, its beneficiaries and the circumstances surrounding the sharp increase in the third quarter.

After reviewing the newspaper’s enquiry, the Public Affairs Officer at the Ministry of Economic Planning and Budget, Busola Fatinukun-Aroloye, directed The PUNCH to the Office of the Accountant-General at the State Treasury Office, suggesting that the office was better placed to provide the requested information.

“Please reach out to the Accountant-General at the State Treasury Office (STO) or get in touch with their PAO,” Fatinukun-Aroloye said on Monday morning.

However, when contacted, the Public Affairs Officer at the Office of the Accountant-General, Olawunmi Ajala, redirected the newspaper to the Ministry of Economic Planning and Budget, saying the office was not in a position to respond to the questions.

“We are not in a position to respond. Please, you need to reach out to them at the Ministry of Economic Planning and Budget for responses to your questions. They are in charge,” Ajala said.

The exchange left The PUNCH without a substantive response from either office on the specific engagements covered by the N120.8bn expenditure, the identities of the recipients or the approvals authorising the spending.

Additional requests for comments sent to the Governor’s Special Adviser on Media and Publicity, Gboyega Akosile, also remained unanswered as of the time this report was filed.

The N120.8bn expenditure represents almost one in every four naira recorded as overhead spending by the Babajide Sanwo-Olu administration in the first nine months of 2026, although the report does not provide details of the recipients or establish the specific beneficiaries of the payments.

The amount is also substantially higher than spending on any other individual overhead category identified in the report. The next largest item was “Welfare Package and Special Services to the Public”, which recorded expenditure of approximately N44.1bn.

This means the professional fees expenditure was about 2.7 times the amount recorded for providing welfare packages and special services to the public.

“The government cannot establish the legitimacy of this expenditure merely by pointing to its inclusion in the approved budget. Budgetary approval is only one part of public financial accountability.

“Lagos should publish a detailed breakdown showing which ministries and agencies incurred the expenditure, the consultants and firms engaged, the procurement processes followed, the approving authorities and the specific deliverables for which payments were made,” Senior Researcher at Good Governance Africa, Kabir Adejumo, told The PUNCH.

“The most significant governance concern is not simply that Lagos State spent such billions of naira on professional fees, but that such a substantial amount of public money is recorded under a broad expenditure heading without sufficient information about the services procured, the beneficiaries, the contracts involved and the outcomes delivered,” he added.

A further examination of the state’s consulting and professional services classification reveals the extent to which expenditure is concentrated in the broadly named professional fees category. The report records approximately N162.8bn under consulting and professional services during the period. Of this amount, the generic professional fees item accounted for approximately N120.8bn, representing about 74 per cent of the total.

All the other consulting and professional services categories combined accounted for approximately N42bn. The disparity is particularly pronounced when the professional fees expenditure is compared with four identifiable professional service categories.

The state recorded approximately N1.2bn for legal services, N2.3bn for engineering consultancy, N2bn for architectural services and N1.4bn for financial consulting. Together, these four categories amounted to about N6.9bn. The expenditure on the generic professional fees item was therefore approximately 17.5 times the combined amount recorded for the four categories.

Other consulting and professional services categories collectively recorded expenditure of approximately N20.4bn in the second quarter, compared with about N12.4bn in the third quarter, representing a decline of roughly 39 per cent. Within that period, management consultancy fees fell from approximately N8bn to N2bn. Professional fees, by contrast, increased by about N24.3bn.

Beyond the size of the allocation, the absence of a transaction-level breakdown weakens public scrutiny of how the money was committed and what value the state received in return, raising concerns about accountability.

For a state with extensive responsibilities in healthcare, education, transport, infrastructure and social protection, the accountability standard for an expenditure of this magnitude cannot end with its appearance in a budget performance table.

“The issue is about the public’s right to know how its money is being spent. A government that seeks public trust must make its expenditure traceable, verifiable and open to independent scrutiny. Transparency should not depend on investigative reporting uncovering unusual spending patterns; it should be built into the way public finances are managed and reported,” Adejumo said.
