Tinubu’s frog and the 36 scorpions

By Punch Punch 2d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

​You likely know the old fable of the frog who agreed to ferry a scorpion across a flooded river. Hesitant at first, the frog relented only after the scorpion s

Halfway through the churning water, the scorpion struck anyway. As they sank into the depths, the dying frog gasped out a single question: Why? “Because,” the scorpion replied, “it is simply in my nature.”

​Today, President Bola Tinubu is that frog. The scorpions riding his back are Nigeria’s 36 state governors.

​For 15 years, I breathed the serene, orderly air of Abuja. Walking down broad avenues beneath working streetlights, insulated by relative security and federal infrastructure, it was easy to indulge a comforting illusion – that Nigeria was steadily finding its footing.

Step across the borders of the Federal Capital Territory into the states and 774 local government areas, however, and that illusion shatters. Out there, hunger is no abstract metric debated on television. It is a daily, grinding battle for survival.

​Like millions of my compatriots, my initial fury rushed straight toward the most visible target in the land: Aso Rock. When a bag of rice outstrips a civil servant’s monthly wage and petrol prices choke small businesses, public rage lands squarely at the presidential gates. Firm in the belief that the centre was starving the nation, I held the President personally to account.

​Then I stopped brooding and started reading – poring over the cold, unassailable ledgers of the National Bureau of Statistics, the Federation Account Allocation Committee, and BudgIT’s State of States report.

​What they reveal turns the popular narrative on its head. Blaming Abuja exclusively for grassroots misery is an elaborate alibi shielding the true architects of local poverty: our governors. When this administration scrapped the haemorrhaging petrol subsidy and floated the naira, it shut down decades of institutionalised leakage, tripling monthly distributable FAAC revenue.

The pool that once hovered between N700 billion and N900 billion now routinely breaches N2 trillion. States that scraped by on N5 billion to N8 billion a month suddenly saw N15 billion, N20 billion, even N30 billion land in their accounts every 30 days.

​We do not need forensic auditors to confirm this windfall; the governors have indicted themselves out of their own mouths.

During the Nigerian Governors’ Forum meeting in February 2024, Governor Dapo Abiodun of Ogun State candidly admitted that federal allocations had dramatically swelled, remarking that states were now receiving “almost double” what they got prior to subsidy removal, and adding pointedly that “we have no excuse not to perform.”

In Delta State, Governor Sheriff Oborevwori went further, telling residents openly that “we are receiving more money from Abuja now, so we cannot complain of lack of funds,” even boasting that his administration had avoided borrowing because the federal inflow was so substantial.

Across the country, from Bauchi’s Bala Mohammed to Anambra’s Chukwuma Soludo, governors have publicly acknowledged that Tinubu’s reforms rescued subnational treasuries from imminent insolvency.

​Yet if the coffers are overflowing by their own confession, why are hunger and decay worse at the state level than ever before? It’s simply because our governors have turned subnational administration into a fiscal black hole, sustained by the pathology of “feeding-bottle federalism.”

According to World Bank analyses, state reliance on federal transfers averages roughly 73 per cent of aggregate revenues outside Lagos. The feeding bottle has tripled in size, yet the appetite for economic self-reliance has flatly refused to grow.

​BudgIT’s State of States report lays this failure bare: an extraordinary surge in state revenues paired with deteriorating service delivery. Across the federation, states burned through nearly N2 trillion servicing debt while piling up massive contractor liabilities and choking arrears in pensions and gratuities.

Behind every delayed pension stands a retired public servant who gave their prime decades to the state, now reduced to begging for earned entitlements.

​Nowhere is this betrayal sharper than in the stranglehold on local governments. Constitutionally charged with primary healthcare, rural roads, and basic education, local councils now routinely attract upwards of N1 billion a month – more than enough to remake rural Nigeria.

Instead, governors have reduced council chairmen to errand boys who surrender their capital surplus to the governor’s office. Even after the landmark Supreme Court judgment of July 11, 2024, outlawed state tampering with joint accounts, governors engineered legal workarounds and captured state electoral commissions to keep local chairmen on a tight leash.

​There is a bitter historical irony here. As Governor of Lagos from 1999 to 2007, when President Olusegun Obasanjo withheld Lagos’s local council allocations, Tinubu did not wring his hands. He overhauled internal revenue collection, defended subnational autonomy, and proved a state could prosper without Abuja’s feeding bottle. Why, then, is the former Governor of Lagos allowing 36 governors to turn his painful reforms into a political tragedy?

​I suspect the answer lies in the cold arithmetic of 2027. Governors are regional barons who command party structures, convention delegates, and ballot machinery. Auditing their windfalls publicly and enforcing the Supreme Court ruling with real teeth would mean declaring war on the cartel required for re-election.

That silent bargain has turned the ruling coalition into a sanctuary for impunity, triggering a stampede of opposition defectors buying instant insulation from anti-graft scrutiny: pledge loyalty to the centre, and your local sins are washed clean.

​By shielding them, the President is ferrying scorpions across the river of 2027. None of this absolves Aso Rock – monetary policy, national security, and macroeconomic stability remains federal duties. Yet accusing the President of stalling the vehicle while governors slash the tyres and siphon the fuel is an intellectual failure.

​An ancient African proverb warns that a cat that cares about tomorrow does not eat a pregnant rat – the seed of continuity and future abundance. Driven by immediate appetite, our state capitals are devouring that pregnant rat today.

Through an obsession with ribbon-cutting infrastructure, governors pour billions into showpiece flyovers, airports, and imposing monuments because concrete is easy to photograph for campaign billboards, while education and healthcare budgets stagnate.

An eight-lane asphalt corridor cannot rescue a malnourished child or staff a dilapidated primary health clinic. Concrete is a vehicle for development, not development itself.

​It is time for the President to abandon quiet electoral appeasement and confront this sabotage head-on. Enforce direct local government disbursements without compromise. Publish itemised monthly breakdowns of every naira sent to every state and council. Unleash anti-corruption agencies on subnational diversions regardless of party badges.

And to our 36 governors – who have already confessed on record that Abuja has given them every naira they need to succeed: you cannot pocket triple what your predecessors received, suffocate local councils, splurge on vanity projects, and expect Aso Rock to serve forever as your human shield.

​The hunger is in the states. The money is in the states. Nigerians must stop gazing exclusively toward Aso Rock for salvation and march – intellectually, intentionally, and civically – to our state capitals to demand an accounting.

True wisdom in leadership is knowing what not to consume today so that tomorrow may live. It is time to stop measuring governance by the size of monthly federal cheques and start measuring it by the dignity of ordinary lives.
