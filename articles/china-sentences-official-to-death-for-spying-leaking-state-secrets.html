China sentences official to death for spying, leaking state secrets

By Punch Punch 5h ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The official reportedly signed an agreement on “confidentiality” and “loyalty”, handed over 35 pieces of information classified as state secrets, and received payments worth more than 1 million yuan ($150,000), The Guardian reported on Tuesday.

According to Gold Wall Press, a publishing company associated with China’s national administration of state secrets protection, Ma was approached and recruited by an unnamed foreign intelligence service while working abroad.

The report stated that an intelligence operative identified as Kailin “frequently arranged to meet with Ma, hoping to engage with him more,” citing an interest in learning Chinese.

It added that Kailin later introduced Ma to a colleague who threatened to report his earlier disclosures to Chinese authorities if he refused to cooperate.

After returning to China, Ma continued to gather intelligence for the foreign agency, which provided him with a laptop, the statement said. He used his position to collect material from classified documents during the day and transmitted it overseas at night via an espionage website.

The statement did not disclose the countries involved, when or where Ma was sentenced, or whether he had already been executed.

The report further stated that recently, the Ministry of State Security and Gold Wall Press have increasingly publicised arrests, as well as using social media, movies and comics to promote warnings about espionage.

Chinese courts typically do not publish information about state security cases, it added.

Gold Wall Press said “this shocking case serves as yet another wake-up call,” as it warned that foreign intelligence agencies were “cunning” and used emotional ties, financial interests and threats to recruit people in key positions.

The statement also urged officials to remain vigilant, strengthen their loyalty to the Communist Party, protect state secrets and guard against foreign recruitment.

The Guardian reported that the case followed publications by US intelligence of its efforts to recruit sources in China.

It referenced the YouTube channel of the Central Intelligence Agency, saying its five most-viewed videos on the channel are recruiting advertisements in Mandarin.

A check on the CIA’s YouTube channel by PUNCH Online on Tuesday revealed that the five most-viewed videos had a total of 135.7 million views.

The oldest of the five videos, titled ‘Safely Contacting the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA),’ was uploaded on October 2, 2024, with over 17m views.

Screenshots taken from the video and translated to English urged anyone willing to contact the CIA to take some precautions, including the type of computer, internet network, and browser to use.

Some of the instructions read, “The preferred way to contact us is to use a trusted non-local VPN service or the Tor network to strengthen your security. If you don’t take these steps, any website you visit may be monitored by security or intelligence agencies or other third parties. Although the content of our website is encrypted, if you don’t take these steps, others may be able to see which websites you have visited.

“If you choose to contact us through https://cia.gov, you must use a VPN. Please consider the following: Use a VPN provider whose headquarters are not in Russia, Iran, China, or any other country considered unfriendly to the United States. Choose a VPN server (country/region of origin) that is not in Russia, Iran, China, or a country considered unfriendly to the United States.”

It added that if an individual wants to be contacted by the CIA via email, they must “provide an email address whose domain host is not in China, Iran, Russia, or any other country considered unfriendly to the United States.

“This email address must not include any personally identifying information, and must not be linked to any account or device you use personally,” among other instructions.

The Guardian reported that in June 2023, China expanded the definition of espionage and strengthened the investigative powers of state security authorities.

The Guardian reported that under the revised law, “relying on espionage organisations and their agents” as well as the unauthorised obtaining of “documents, data, materials, and items related to national security and interests” can constitute a spying offence.
