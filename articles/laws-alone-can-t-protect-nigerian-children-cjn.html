Laws alone can’t protect Nigerian children – CJN

By Punch Punch 4d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Chief Justice of Nigeria, Justice Kudirat Kekere-Ekun, has warned that legislation alone cannot protect Nigerian children from abuse, exploitation, neglect

She said the effectiveness of the Child Rights Act 2003 depended on how consistently its provisions were implemented to protect children in their daily lives.

The CJN spoke on Friday at the inaugural conference of the Nigerian Association of Family Court Judicial Officers, Magistrates and Other Practitioners, organised to commemorate the 2026 United Nations World Children’s Day in Abuja.

Represented by a Supreme Court Justice, Jummai Sanke, SAN, Kekere-Ekun said Nigeria had established an important legal framework for protecting children’s rights but needed coordinated action by relevant institutions to translate the provisions into practical safeguards.

“In Nigeria, the Child Rights Act 2003 provides an important legal framework for the protection and advancement of children’s rights. Its recognition of the best interests of the child as a primary consideration in all actions concerning children establishes a principle that must guide our laws, policies, institutions and decisions.

“However, the existence of legislation, important as it is, does not by itself guarantee the protection of children. The real measure of our commitment lies in how effectively these rights are respected, protected and enforced in the daily lives of children,” the CJN said.

She identified child abuse, neglect, sexual exploitation, trafficking, child labour, early and forced marriage, abandonment and denial of access to education and healthcare as persistent threats to children’s welfare.

The CJN said the judiciary had a significant responsibility to ensure that children appearing before courts as victims, witnesses or children in conflict with the law were treated with dignity, sensitivity and understanding.

She called for child-friendly judicial proceedings that protected children’s privacy, encouraged their participation and took account of their age, maturity and emotional circumstances.

“Where a child has come into conflict with the law, the emphasis should, where appropriate, be on rehabilitation and reintegration rather than measures that permanently undermine the child’s prospects,” she said.

Kekere-Ekun also called for more training for judicial officers in trauma-informed approaches, handling child witnesses, emerging forms of exploitation and the implications of digital technology.

She said child protection required collaboration among government institutions, law enforcement agencies, social welfare services, schools, healthcare providers, legislators, families and civil society organisations.

“A child who has suffered abuse should not have to navigate a fragmented system in search of help,” she said.

The CJN urged participants to identify practical solutions to child protection challenges and strengthen institutions to ensure that children’s rights guaranteed by law translated into meaningful protection.

She said the true measure of commitment to children’s rights would be seen in children protected from abuse, girls allowed to remain in school, children whose voices were heard in custody proceedings and young people given opportunities for rehabilitation.

The Attorney-General of the Federation and Minister of Justice, Lateef Fagbemi, SAN, also acknowledged gaps in the implementation of child protection laws, disclosing that the Federal Government was reviewing the Child Rights Act 2003 to address emerging challenges.

He said the review had culminated in a draft amendment bill scheduled for stakeholder validation on November 24, ahead of further steps towards its finalisation and enactment.

Fagbemi said the challenge confronting Nigeria was not the absence of laws but the failure to effectively implement them.

“The pressing challenge before us is not the absence of laws, but the gap in their implementation. Across the country, too many children still face realities that threaten their safety and stifle their potential.

“Bridging this gap demands sustained political will, coordinated institutional action, and a readiness to adapt our systems to the evolving vulnerabilities of our children,” he said.

The minister said the Federal Ministry of Justice was driving reforms to strengthen the country’s child justice system, adding that it had supported the development of Legal Aid Guidelines and Prosecutors’ Guidelines for Handling Cases of Children in Contact with the Law.

He said the guidelines were designed to guarantee quality legal representation and enforce child-sensitive prosecution standards nationwide.

Fagbemi also called for stronger safeguards against cyberbullying, digital exploitation, harmful online content and breaches of children’s privacy.

He urged stakeholders to ensure that the outcome of the conference went beyond communiqués and produced measurable improvements in courtrooms, policies and frontline services.

Earlier, the President of NAFCJOMP, Justice Eberechi Suzette Nyesom-Wike, said the association was committed to advancing children’s rights, protection and welfare through stronger family court practice and a child-sensitive administration of justice.

She said the association, registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission in January 2026, brought together judicial officers, magistrates, prosecutors, social welfare officers, advocates, law enforcement personnel and other child protection practitioners across the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory.

Nyesom-Wike said the association’s mission was to promote children’s best interests by strengthening family court practice and supporting the effective administration of justice.

“Among other things, our objectives are to promote compliance with child protection laws by advocating for the implementation of policies related to child welfare, including education, healthcare, and social security.

“We seek to pursue the successful implementation of the Child Rights Act and laws of the sub-nationals, in ensuring that the Nigerian child suffers no detriment in any circumstance,” she said.

She said the association also sought to promote professional development and collaboration among family justice and child protection practitioners to ensure consistent standards in family and juvenile court practice.

Nyesom-Wike said NAFCJOMP, in collaboration with its partners, facilitated access to counselling, legal aid and medical support through a referral system.

“This reflects our conviction that protecting children requires not only sound laws and effective courts, but also coordinated support that meets children’s needs in practice,” she said.

She said the conference would provide an opportunity for stakeholders to strengthen their professional community, deepen collaboration across disciplines and develop practical approaches to challenges confronting children and families.

The Speaker of the Children’s Parliament, Miss Progress Umoh, also made a case for Nigerian children, while participants drawn from secondary schools across the Federal Capital Territory spoke on issues affecting their rights and welfare.

The conference, themed “My Day. My Rights. My Future,” brought together judicial officers, legal practitioners, policymakers, child protection professionals, civil society organisations and children to examine child protection, access to justice, rehabilitation and children’s participation in matters affecting them.
