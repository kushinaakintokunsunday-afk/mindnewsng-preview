When love meets genetics: The importance of genotype testing (2)

By Punch Punch 4d ago 5 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Young people should be encouraged to establish their genotype early, instead of waiting until they are considering marriage or starting a family. Parents, schools, healthcare professionals and community organisations all have a role to play in improving awareness.

Genotype education should form part of wider conversations about health and inheritance, helping young people understand their genetic background before they reach the stage of making important family decisions.

In Nigeria, where family expectations, cultural traditions and religious beliefs can influence marriage decisions, conversations about genotype need to become more open.

Churches, mosques, schools and community leaders can help normalise screening without creating stigma around people living with sickle cell disease. These conversations should be approached with sensitivity and accurate information, rather than fear or condemnation.

It is equally important that genotype results are accurate. Testing should be conducted through reputable healthcare facilities. Anyone with uncertain or conflicting results should seek confirmation from an appropriate laboratory or specialist.

Genetic counselling can help individuals and couples understand their results, the way haemoglobin conditions are inherited and the possible implications for future pregnancies.

For couples who discover that they have a combination of genotypes that could result in a child being born with sickle cell disease, professional counselling is particularly important. They need an opportunity to ask questions, understand the risks and explore the reproductive options available to them.

Depending on their circumstances, these may include prenatal testing, IVF with preimplantation genetic testing, adoption or other family-building choices. Not every option will be suitable, affordable or accessible to every couple, but having accurate information allows them to consider their circumstances carefully.

These decisions can be emotionally challenging. Couples may face pressure from relatives, religious communities or society. Some may feel guilty, confused or frightened about what the future holds.

Others may struggle with the fear that a medical diagnosis could determine the direction of their relationship. This is why conversations about genotype should be handled with compassion. Couples need support and reliable advice, not condemnation.

As someone who lives with sickle cell disease, I understand that this is not simply a discussion about percentages, genetic combinations or medical terminology. Behind every statistic is a human being.

Living with SCD can involve recurrent pain, hospital admissions, fatigue and complications that disrupt education, employment, relationships and family life.

Some people experience relatively mild symptoms, while others face serious and life-changing complications. The condition can affect not only the individual but also parents, partners, children and other family members who provide support and care.

It is therefore important that discussions about genotype testing do not inadvertently suggest that people living with sickle cell disease are less deserving of love, marriage, parenthood or fulfilling lives.

People with SCD deserve the same opportunities to experience love, build families and pursue their ambitions.

They should not be defined solely by their diagnosis. The objective of genotype awareness is not to stigmatise anyone or make people feel that their worth as a potential partner depends on a laboratory result. It is to ensure that prospective parents understand the possibilities and can make informed reproductive choices.

Faith communities also have an important role to play. For many people, marriage is not simply a personal commitment; it is a spiritual and family decision.

Religious leaders can encourage couples to seek medical information and genetic counselling while reminding their congregations that people living with sickle cell disease deserve dignity, acceptance and compassion. Faith and medical knowledge do not have to compete. They can work alongside one another.

Parents should also avoid leaving these conversations until their children are old enough to contemplate marriage. Genotype awareness should be introduced as part of responsible health education.

Schools and youth organisations can help young people understand inheritance, testing and the importance of knowing their family health history. The earlier this knowledge is acquired, the less likely it is to become a source of panic or conflict later in life.

Genotype testing should not be treated as an afterthought or an administrative requirement to be completed shortly before a wedding. It should be part of responsible reproductive health education.

Couples need the opportunity to discuss their results honestly, understand the possible implications and make decisions without pressure from relatives or society.

Ultimately, love and genetic compatibility are two different things. Love may bring two people together, but knowledge helps them navigate the decisions that follow. Having a difficult conversation about genotype does not diminish love; it can demonstrate a willingness to approach the future with honesty and responsibility.

We cannot prevent every genetic condition, but we can improve awareness, access to reliable testing and opportunities for informed decision-making. We can also challenge the stigma that surrounds sickle cell disease and replace judgement with understanding.

The question, therefore, is not whether love can overcome genetics. It is whether we are prepared to combine love with knowledge, responsibility and informed choices.

Knowing your genotype does not determine whom you should love. It empowers you to understand the genetic implications of having children together. That knowledge, acquired early and used responsibly, can make a meaningful difference to the decisions we make about our future families.
