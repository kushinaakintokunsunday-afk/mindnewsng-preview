El Niño: Rising Climate Risks Deepen Economic Tensions

The El Niño weather phenomenon, expected to be exceptionally strong this year, is likely to exacerbate the disruptions to the global economy, with consequences including higher food and electricity prices.

The naturally occurring warming of surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean has already altered wind and rainfall patterns around the world, resulting in devastating droughts and wildfires.

Combined with climate change as well as soaring prices for energy and other commodities from the US war against Iran, the potential for further damage is high.

“In general, you can say it’s an extreme negative supply shock,” Paolo Zanghieri, an economist at Generali Asset Management, told AFP.

Agriculture, trade and hydroelectric production are most immediately impacted by El Niño, which is expected to peak in December and last at least until next March — with repercussions felt for months beyond.

Oxford Economics estimates that if it is as strong as many experts warn, global GDP could be cut by 0.6 percent next year and a further 0.8 percent in 2028.

Inflation Pressures

Compromised harvests from excessive heat or rain are likely to send food costs higher, which will be especially painful for millions of people in developing countries, where food represents a much bigger share of household budgets.

“An El Niño characterised by a 2C rise in Pacific Ocean temperature could lift global food prices by up to 20 percent” by the first quarter of 2028, Zanghieri and his colleague Guillaume Tresca, an emerging markets strategist, wrote in a recent report.

That could lift overall US and eurozone inflation by 0.4 percentage points, but developing countries could see an additional 3.0 points in “a non-negligible, but temporary shock”, they wrote.

“Our vulnerability index suggests that South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa face the greatest risks,” they added.

While a moderate food price shock can be offset by increased imports, existing stocks or product substitutions, Deutsche Bank analysts warn of more serious risks from a widespread crisis.

“A large shock propagating across multiple countries can’t be absorbed as easily, with second-round inflation effects likely to result,” they wrote in July.

For Simon Lacoume, an economist at the French export credit insurance group Coface, a drop in the corn harvest in Brazil, for example, would “percolate through the livestock farming and meat sectors”.

Droughts could also lower reservoir levels at hydroelectric plants, forcing countries to increase oil and gas imports at a time when prices are already high because of the Gulf conflict.

But the disruptions can also work the other way: Sri Lanka recently cancelled an electricity price hike after heavy El Nino rains helped fill reservoirs.

Hard To Measure

Droughts are also impacting global trade by forcing reduced traffic through the Panama Canal, after lower water storage forced authorities to restrict operations since September.

Recent rains have allowed increases, but the government has declared a nationwide emergency over the exceptionally strong El Niño.

Central banks are also wrestling with how to judge the fallout, worried about limiting inflation while not increasing interest rates to levels that could spark an economic downturn.

“The impact is very disparate and complicated to measure,” Tresca at Generali told AFP. “It depends on when a meteorological event hits during the planting cycle, for example.”

The Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) has urged governments to invest more in existing resilience plans for dealing with climate change.

But in most countries, the implementation of efforts to adapt agriculture and infrastructure remains insufficient.

“We don’t see the national adaptation plans actually resulting in actual mandates for different economic actors to take on these resilience investments,” said Catherine Gamper, Team Lead for climate change adaptation at the Paris-based agency.

And even when the will exists, many countries are also struggling to cut debt and deficit levels that have soared for a multitude of reasons in recent years.

“There is absolutely a public finance problem,” Gamper said.
