EXPLAINER: Things to know about Enugu’s proposed 2% rent cap

By Punch Punch 6d ago 5 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Enugu State House of Assembly has passed a bill seeking to restrict landlords from increasing rents by more than two per cent every four years, a proposal t

The proposed amendment to the state’s Landlord and Tenant Law comes amid complaints about rising accommodation costs, arbitrary rent increases, excessive agency commissions and legal fees.

Although the bill has passed its third reading, it is yet to become law, as it requires the assent of Governor Peter Mbah.

If signed, the legislation could introduce new restrictions on rent reviews, provide additional safeguards for tenants and regulate certain charges associated with securing accommodation.

Here are five things the proposed law could mean for residents of Enugu State.

1. Landlords may not increase rent every year

One of the most significant provisions of the proposed amendment is the restriction on how frequently landlords can increase rent.

Under the proposal, landlords would have to wait at least four years before increasing the rent of a property. Any subsequent increase would be limited to two per cent of the existing rent.

This means tenants could potentially enjoy greater certainty about their accommodation costs instead of facing annual or frequent rent increases.

For instance, if a tenant currently pays ₦500,000 annually, a two per cent increase would amount to ₦10,000, bringing the annual rent to ₦510,000 after the permitted review period.

Similarly, a tenant paying ₦1m annually would face a maximum two per cent increment of ₦20,000, bringing the rent to ₦1.02m.

However, the actual effect would depend on the final wording of the legislation, particularly how the four-year period and rent calculation are defined.

2. Renovated properties could attract higher rent

The proposed amendment also makes provision for landlords who undertake reasonable renovations to their properties.

In such circumstances, landlords would be permitted to increase rent by not more than 10 per cent.

This means that a property owner who carries out qualifying renovations could potentially charge a higher rent than the ordinary two per cent limit allows.

For example, if a property attracts an annual rent of ₦500,000, a 10 per cent increase would amount to ₦50,000, bringing the new rent to ₦550,000.

The provision could allow landlords to recover part of the costs of improving their properties while preventing unlimited increases.

However, the definition of reasonable renovations, the evidence required and the conditions for applying the 10 per cent increase would be important in determining how the provision works.

3. Tenants could face lower accommodation-related charges

Beyond rent increases, the proposed amendment is also intended to address complaints about excessive agency commissions and legal fees when residents seek accommodation.

Such charges can significantly increase the amount prospective tenants must pay before moving into a property.

The House committee chairman, Okey Mbah, said the legislation was designed to protect residents from exploitation by landlords and estate agents.

“This bill will subsidise over 60 per cent of what residents used to pay while trying to secure accommodation,” he said.

A previous published version of the amendment bill contained provisions requiring estate agents to obtain permits from the Enugu State Housing Development Corporation.

It also proposed limiting agency fees to 10 per cent of the rent paid by prospective tenants and legal fees for preparing tenancy agreements to 10 per cent.

However, the final version passed by the Assembly should be examined to establish whether these provisions were retained and whether additional conditions apply.

4. Landlords may face greater restrictions over eviction

The proposed reform also raises questions about the procedures landlords must follow when seeking to recover their properties from tenants.

According to a report by THISDAY, the committee chairman said the amended legislation would require landlords to properly serve tenants before initiating court proceedings.

“A tenant must be properly served before being charged to court. Without proper service, no case should be established or treated in the first instance,” Mbah said.

If retained in the final legislation, the provision could strengthen procedural protections for tenants facing eviction disputes.

It would also emphasise that landlords must follow the legally prescribed process rather than resorting to arbitrary eviction.

However, it would not mean tenants could occupy properties indefinitely without paying rent or complying with their tenancy agreements.

5. Landlords could face financial and enforcement challenges

While the proposed restrictions could offer tenants greater predictability, property owners have questioned whether the two per cent limit is sustainable in an economy where the costs of maintaining buildings, purchasing construction materials, electricity and other services continue to change.

A landlord in Enugu, Onyekwere Johnson, according to a Punch report on Tuesday, described the proposed cap as unrealistic and difficult to enforce.

“That would have been a natural law if the prices of petroleum products and electricity tariffs increased by two per cent. Otherwise, it is an unnatural law. It is neither sustainable nor can it be obeyed,” he told PUNCH.

He added, “You cannot single out landlords and regulate their business while other sectors are struggling to meet the benchmark of inflation set by the managers of the Nigerian economy. It violates landlords’ rights against discrimination.”

The disagreement raises questions about how the government would enforce the proposed restrictions, verify qualifying renovations and address disputes between landlords and tenants.

It also raises the possibility that property owners could seek other ways to adjust their charges, making effective enforcement and clear regulations important.

When will the proposed law take effect?

The Enugu State House of Assembly passed the Landlord and Tenant Amendment Bill on Tuesday, November 17, 2026, after it scaled its third reading.

The bill, which passed its second reading on March 11 and underwent a public hearing on March 26, now awaits the assent of Governor Peter Mbah.

Until the governor signs it and it commences in accordance with its provisions, the proposed restrictions should not be treated as an existing law.

If assented to, the legislation could introduce significant changes to how rents are reviewed, accommodation charges are regulated and tenancy disputes are handled across Enugu State.
