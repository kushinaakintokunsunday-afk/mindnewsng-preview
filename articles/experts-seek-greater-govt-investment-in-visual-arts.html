Experts seek greater govt investment in visual arts

By Punch Punch 6d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

They said limited institutional funding was making it difficult for galleries to sustain their operations and provide the support required by artists at different stages of their careers, despite the growing number of galleries and increasing activity in the country’s art scene.

The experts spoke at a recent press conference for the Art Masters Affairs Exhibition, organised by Fobally Art World Africa in Lagos, where they also called for greater recognition of established artists and clearer standards for distinguishing masters from other practitioners.

An art master and retired art lecturer at Yaba College of Technology, Dr Kunle Adeyemi, said government support for visual arts remained limited compared with the attention given to the performing arts.

He urged the government to extend at least half of the attention it gives to the performing arts to visual arts, arguing that galleries needed institutional support to remain sustainable.

“If they can give the same attention, 50 per cent attention they are giving to the performing arts, they can give it to visual arts. It won’t be where we are today,” Adeyemi said.

He said government assistance to visual arts was not sufficiently widespread, with only a few galleries receiving support from state authorities.

“There is a need for both governments, corporates, and international bodies’ support for Nigerian galleries to be able to continue the good work,” he said.

Adeyemi said artistic passion alone could not guarantee the survival of galleries and the wider industry, stressing that practitioners needed business structures and support systems to sustain their activities.

He noted that artists could spend decades developing their practice before gaining the recognition and commercial opportunities needed to earn a stable income.

“Passion drives you, but the business too, because as much as we are doing passion, the business is very important because it will be sustained,” he said.

He added that galleries could improve their chances of survival by developing programmes for different categories of artists and collectors, rather than concentrating on a single segment of the market.

The exhibition’s Managing Director and Founder of Fobally Art World Africa, Folasade Abiola, said the gallery was established to promote African art and artists by improving their visibility locally and internationally.

She said its programmes were designed to serve different classes of artists within the art ecosystem, including secondary school students, undergraduates, emerging artists, mid-level professionals, senior artists, masters and grandmasters.

Abiola said the gallery organised a grandmasters exhibition in the previous year and was staging the masters exhibition as part of a programme it intended to sustain in subsequent years.

She said the gallery’s approach was informed partly by the different interests of art collectors, noting that while some collectors actively sought works by established masters, others were more interested in emerging artists or mid-level professionals.

According to her, galleries needed to support different classes of artists to ensure that all levels of artists received attention and opportunities across the board.

Abiola also explained that the selection of a younger artist for the masters exhibition was not based solely on age or years of practice, but on other technical impact in the art space over the years.

She further stated that the organisers considered the impact of an artist’s work, including whether it had attracted recognition and purchases locally and internationally.

She noted that young practitioners must show that their work has made a significant impact beyond Nigeria to be considered eligible, adding that the selection process also considered professionalism, technical quality, artistic contributions and the acceptance of an artist’s work.

He said the exhibition featured approximately 27 to 28 artists whose studio work, professional achievements and contributions to the development of other practitioners had distinguished them.

He explained that the selection process aimed to celebrate artistic excellence and honour practitioners whose contributions had significantly advanced the profession: “We look at the contributions, we look at their artistic practice, we look at the excellence that they have come to play. We consider those individuals whose lives they have positively impacted.”

He said the organisers had aimed to ensure that the exhibition reflected the country’s geographical diversity, but high transportation costs prevented some artists from the far north and southernmost parts of Nigeria from taking part.

Rasheed Amodu, an artist, historian, critic and consultant, said the proliferation of galleries had made it harder for collectors and the public to distinguish established masters from less accomplished practitioners.

He said the exhibition was conceived partly to address concerns about artistic evaluation and the standards used to recognise accomplished artists.

Amodu maintained that such recognition should be based on the quality and craftsmanship of an artist’s work, years of consistent practice, depth of ideas and contributions to the profession.

He said some practitioners had spent 50 to 60 years developing their careers, adding that years of practice and the quality of work should carry weight in determining who qualified as a master.

“The class, the finesse of the work, defines who is a great artist,” Amodu noted.

He warned that the absence of clear distinctions could allow artists with limited experience or less accomplished work to claim the status of masters, creating confusion among collectors and the public.

Amodu cited a 1984 observation made by Professor David J. Gidley concerning “junk artists” and collectors buying “junk artworks” because they could not distinguish between different levels of artistic quality.

He said the exhibition was intended to establish a clearer marker of artistic accomplishment and draw attention to practitioners who had earned recognition through sustained work.

“The exhibition, per se, is just trying to show that there are masters and there are masters,” he said.

Amodu further revealed that an 81-year-old artist had been invited to the exhibition but could not attend due to personal circumstances.

The exhibition will feature works addressing Nigerian culture, social issues, philosophical themes and landscapes, with artists working across different media, including printmaking, painting, sculpture, carving, metalwork, fibreglass and tapestry.

Adeyemi said the works were intended to encourage viewers to look beyond their immediate appearance and consider the ideas and interpretations embedded in them.

He said the organisers hoped the exhibition would help strengthen public understanding of visual arts and the contributions of practitioners to the country’s cultural sector.

Abiola said Fobally Art World Africa would continue developing programmes for different categories of artists, with the aim of improving the visibility of African art in local and international markets.
