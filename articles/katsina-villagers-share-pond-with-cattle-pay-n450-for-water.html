Katsina villagers share pond with cattle, pay N450 for water

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 11 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

For the young man, it is another day at work. For the animals, it is a routine search for water. But for residents of Rimi-Muduru community in Mani Local Government Area of Katsina State, the scene represents a painful reality they have lived with for about five decades.

The water collected from the pond is not simply for watering livestock or irrigating farmland. It is also what families depend on to cook their meals, wash their clothes, bathe their children and, in some cases, satisfy their thirst.

There is little distinction between the water humans and animals consume. Both depend on the same earth pond, which residents say has served as their main water source for about 50 years.

For those who cannot afford to fetch the water themselves, there is a price to pay. Young men who have turned the community’s water shortage into a means of livelihood now sell a 25-litre jerrycan for N450.

In a country where access to clean water remains a major development challenge in many rural settlements, the experience of Rimi-Muduru raises troubling questions about communities that have waited for generations for basic infrastructure.

During our correspondent’s visit to the community on Wednesday, the scale of the problem became evident. Several young men were seen scooping water from the pond, filling jerrycans and transporting them to customers.

Women and children also gathered around the same water point, collecting what they needed for domestic use. Livestock moved around the pond, sharing the source with residents without any visible arrangement to separate human and animal consumption.

For the people of Rimi-Muduru, this is not an emergency that began yesterday. It is a way of life inherited from their parents and, in some cases, grandparents.

A resident, who identified himself simply as Aliyu, said the pond was dug by earlier generations when the community struggled to find water.

“Our community has depended on this pond for several decades because there are no alternative water facilities. I am over 40 years old, and this pond was dug by our parents because of the scarcity of water. It is the only one we have been using till today,” he said.

Another resident, Bala, describes the pond as indispensable, explaining that residents had little choice but to continue using it for their daily needs.

For Bala and other residents, the concern is not that they do not understand the importance of safe water but that they have no dependable alternative.

A commodity bought at a heavy price

The water shortage has also created an informal business around the community’s most basic necessity.

Young men who fetch water from the pond have become vendors, supplying households willing to pay for the service. What may have started as an opportunity to earn a living has gradually become part of the daily survival system of the community.

Paying N450 for a 25-litre jerrycan could be overwhelming for many households in the community.

A family that buys four jerrycans of water daily spends N1,800 in a day. They would have to cough up N54,000 to use water for 30 days.

For families requiring more water, the financial burden is considerably higher. Households with several children and other dependants must buy more for cooking, cleaning, bathing and other domestic activities.

The situation presents a difficult choice for low-income families who have to spend a substantial part of their income buying water or spend hours fetching it themselves.

Even those who get free water are not spared the burden. They must contend with the time spent walking to the pond, waiting for their turns and carrying heavy containers back home.

For women and children, who often shoulder much of the responsibility for household water collection in rural communities, the daily routine can interfere with other activities, including education, childcare and income-generating work.

The irony is that residents buy water whose quality is highly questionable, as no quality checks are carried out before they consume it.

In effect, the community is paying for a resource that should be available through reliable public infrastructure, without any guarantee that what it buys is safe to drink.

A health threat beneath the surface

Beyond the financial implications, the health risks associated with sharing an open water source with livestock are a major concern.

An infectious disease specialist, Dr Muhammad Abubakar, warned that the practice could expose residents to several infectious diseases.

Abubakar, a consultant in infectious diseases and tropical medicine at the Department of Internal Medicine, Federal Teaching Hospital, Katsina, told ArewaPUNCH that stagnant water could harbour disease-causing organisms, including bacteria, viruses and parasites.

He described the situation in Rimi-Muduru as worrisome, noting that water, although essential to human and animal survival, could become a vehicle for disease transmission when contaminated.

“Water is an integral part of life. There is virtually no aspect of life that is without water. Even our human body is mainly composed of water, likewise animals and plants,” he said.

According to him, the presence of livestock at the same water source increases the possibility of contamination through animal waste and physical contact.

“Animals are not only putting their mouths into the water; they may defecate there, pass their faeces and urinate inside. Even their hooves carry a lot of diseases from the farm and the bush, and they put their legs and hooves into the water,” Abubakar added.

His warning highlights the dangers associated with an unprotected water source where animals and humans have unrestricted access.

The specialist identified zoonotic infections, which are diseases that can spread between animals and humans, as another concern.

Among the diseases he mentioned were brucellosis and bovine tuberculosis, alongside other bacterial, viral and parasitic infections.

He explained that disease-causing organisms introduced into the water by animals could then infect humans who drink it without adequate treatment.

For residents, the warning raises an uncomfortable question: how safe is the water that has sustained their households for generations?

While the community’s dependence on the pond has continued for decades, the absence of a documented health assessment means the actual burden of water-related infections remains unclear.

Familiarity is not immunity

Some may argue that the community residents have used the water for years without experiencing a major health crisis.

However, Abubakar cautioned against interpreting decades of exposure as proof of safety or immunity.

He said residents’ continued use of the pond did not mean they were protected from diseases that could arise from contaminated water.

Responding to concerns about reported cases of diarrhoea among children in the community, the specialist said a possible connection with unsafe water could not be dismissed. However, he stressed that establishing the actual disease burden would require proper epidemiological investigations.

“We may not objectively know their health status because we don’t have the epidemiological demography, meaning the number of people in the community over so many years, how many people are alive, how many have fallen sick and how many are currently alive,” he noted.

He explained that appropriate medical investigations, including stool, blood and sputum examinations where clinically indicated, alongside a comprehensive community health survey, could help establish infection prevalence.

Abubakar also acknowledged that repeated exposure to certain organisms could trigger immune responses, a concept sometimes discussed under the hygiene hypothesis.

However, he warned that such a possibility should not be mistaken for universal protection against infectious diseases.

“The only lucky part is what we call the hygiene hypothesis, whereby people in the same community, through subsequent or continuous exposure to an antigen, may develop immunity. But this is more of a hypothesis, more of an assumption. There are some diseases; they do not respect immunity,” he said.

His explanation reminds us that survival under difficult environmental conditions does not necessarily mean those conditions are harmless.

For Rimi-Muduru, the priority should therefore be to establish the water’s quality, determine the health risks and provide residents with a safer alternative rather than rely on the assumption that generations of use have made the pond safe.

When human, animal and environmental health meet

Abubakar also advocated the adoption of the One Health approach, which recognises the interconnectedness of human, animal and environmental health.

He explained that the approach was particularly relevant in rural communities where people and livestock share the same environment and potentially contaminated water sources.

According to him, the response should not be left to health authorities alone. Relevant agricultural, environmental and water agencies must work together to address the problem.

“The One Health approach is very important. You need to understand the interface between humans and animals,” he said.

He called for public awareness campaigns, health education and practical interventions to ensure that residents understood the dangers of sharing unprotected water sources with livestock.

He also urged the community to engage primary healthcare authorities and other relevant institutions to seek intervention.

For Rimi-Muduru, such collaboration would need to go beyond sensitisation. Residents require an alternative water supply that is accessible, reliable and safe.

Five decades of waiting

The continued dependence on a single earth pond for approximately 50 years raises broader questions about rural infrastructure, public service delivery and the challenges confronting communities outside major urban centres.

Access to safe drinking water is essential to public health, sanitation, education and economic development. Yet residents of the Mani community still negotiate daily survival around a pond dug by an earlier generation.

The Katsina State Government, through the Ministry of Water Resources, the State Rural Water Supply and Sanitation Agency and relevant local government authorities, has an important role to play in addressing the situation.

The first step should be an immediate water-quality assessment to determine the extent of possible contamination and the required treatment.

This should be followed by providing emergency treated water, constructing functional boreholes and establishing separate drinking points for humans and livestock.

Any intervention must also be sustainable. A borehole that breaks down after a few months without maintenance would only provide temporary relief.

Community participation in planning and maintaining water facilities is also needed to ensure any new infrastructure serves residents over the long term.

When contacted by phone for comment, the Katsina State Commissioner for Water Resources, Manir Ayuba, did not pick up our correspondent’s calls as of the time of filing this report.

The question of dignity

For the young men who fetch and sell water, the pond represents an opportunity to earn an income. For the women who collect water for their families, it is part of an exhausting daily routine. For the children growing up in the community, it is the only water source they have known.

But behind these different experiences is one common reality: a community that has spent nearly half a century without a dependable alternative to an open pond shared with livestock.

The N450 charged for a jerrycan is more than a commercial transaction. It reflects how the absence of basic infrastructure can turn an essential human need into a recurring financial burden.

For Aliyu, who has spent more than 40 years watching his community depend on the same muddy pond, the situation is a painful reminder that the hardship inherited from one generation has become the burden of another.

His greatest concern is that children growing up in Rimi-Muduru may grow old under the same conditions, fetching water from a pond shared with cattle, while their parents continue to spend money on a resource that should be a basic public service.

After nearly five decades of waiting, residents say they want more than promises or temporary interventions. They want functional boreholes, reliable access to clean drinking water and an end to the daily struggle that has defined life in their community for generations.

For a community that has waited 50 years, access to safe water should no longer be treated as a favour from the government. It is a basic necessity and a right that should not depend on a family’s ability to pay N450 for a jerrycan.

The people of Rimi-Muduru have inherited their parents’ thirst. They should not be forced to pass it on to their children
