Seven levels to mastering your finances

Financial independence depends less on earnings than on effective money management, disciplined savings, multiple income sources and strategic investments that build wealth, strengthen financial security and secure the future, writes GODWIN AIKIGBE

Level 1: Surviving on salary

The first level is where many salary earners find themselves: depending entirely on their monthly paychecks to meet their financial obligations.

At this stage, an individual’s income comes primarily from employment, and virtually every naira earned is committed to expenses before the next payday. Rent, transportation, food, school fees, utility bills, family responsibilities, loan repayments and other recurring expenses consume most, if not all, of the monthly earnings.

Some workers begin each month with the hope of saving but discover that their salaries have disappeared before they can put anything aside. Others depend on salary advances, overdrafts or loans to survive towards the end of the month.

This situation can create a cycle in which an individual works primarily to meet immediate financial obligations without making meaningful progress towards long-term financial security.

The danger of living exclusively on a paycheck is that it leaves little room for unexpected events. A medical bill, vehicle breakdown, sudden job loss or urgent family responsibility can destabilise the individual’s finances.

However, being at this level does not necessarily mean someone is financially irresponsible. In an economy characterised by rising living costs and competing demands, some workers genuinely struggle to make their earnings cover essential needs.

The first step towards improvement is to understand the difference between earning money and making financial progress. Earning a salary is important, but the ability to retain part of that income, however small, is what begins the journey towards financial stability.

Level 2: Start budgeting

The second level is learning how to budget.

A budget is a spending plan based on an individual’s income and expenses. It provides a clear picture of how much money comes in, how much goes out and what remains after financial obligations have been met.

Without a budget, many people spend money based on immediate needs, emotions and convenience. They may know their monthly salaries but have little understanding of how much they spend on food, transportation, entertainment, subscriptions, shopping and other activities.

Budgeting introduces discipline and accountability into personal finances. It requires individuals to assign specific purposes to their income rather than spending without a clear plan.

One major advantage of budgeting is that it prepares people for unpredictable life events. Although a budget cannot prevent emergencies, it can help individuals anticipate financial demands and make provisions for unexpected expenses.

It also makes it easier to plan for major purchases. Whether someone intends to buy a car, acquire a property, pay school fees, renovate a home or finance further education, a budget provides a framework for gradually setting aside the required funds.

Another benefit is that budgeting reveals an individual’s financial position and spending patterns. By reviewing income and expenses, people can identify areas where money is being wasted and redirect those resources towards more important priorities.

The importance of monitoring seemingly insignificant expenses was captured by Benjamin Franklin, who warned that “a small leak will sink a great ship”. This observation is particularly relevant to personal finance because small, seemingly harmless expenses can accumulate into substantial amounts over time.

For instance, someone who spends a considerable amount on frequent restaurant visits, impulse purchases or unnecessary subscriptions may discover that reducing such expenses could free up money for savings and investments.

There are different budgeting methods, including zero-based budgeting, the 50/30/20 rule and the envelope system. The most appropriate method depends on an individual’s income, responsibilities and financial goals.

The 50/30/20 rule, for example, recommends allocating 50 per cent of income to needs, 30 per cent to wants and 20 per cent to savings and debt repayment. However, people facing high living costs may need to adjust these proportions.

The important thing is to establish a realistic spending plan, review it regularly and ensure that savings and financial goals are treated as priorities rather than afterthoughts.

Level 3: Debt repayment

The third level is tackling debt, particularly expensive debt that consumes a significant portion of income.

For many people, debt is one of the biggest obstacles to financial progress. Credit card balances, payday loans, high-interest personal loans and other expensive borrowing arrangements can gradually undermine an individual’s ability to save and invest.

Debt becomes particularly dangerous when borrowers repeatedly take new loans to repay existing obligations. This creates a cycle in which a growing portion of future income is committed to servicing past spending.

The consequences of excessive borrowing were highlighted by Benjamin Franklin, who observed that “he that goes a borrowing goes a sorrowing”. Although borrowing can sometimes be useful, particularly when it finances productive activities, excessive debt can become a serious financial burden.

Budgeting helps expose this problem because it shows precisely where money is going. Once individuals understand their spending patterns, they can identify how much of their income is being consumed by loan repayments and interest charges.

The next step is to develop a repayment strategy.

One approach is the debt avalanche method, which involves prioritising debts with the highest interest rates while making minimum payments on other obligations. This can reduce the total interest paid over time.

Another is the debt snowball method, which involves paying off the smallest debts first while maintaining minimum payments on others. Clearing smaller obligations can provide psychological motivation to continue tackling larger debts.

Whichever approach is adopted, the objective should be to reduce expensive borrowing and avoid accumulating new unnecessary obligations.

However, it is important to distinguish between productive borrowing and harmful debt. Not all debt is necessarily bad. A carefully structured loan used to finance a viable business, acquire an income-generating asset or fund an investment with reasonable prospects may serve a useful purpose.

Nevertheless, borrowing should be approached cautiously. Individuals must consider interest rates, repayment periods, associated charges and their ability to meet obligations even when income falls.

Paying off expensive debt frees up money that would otherwise have gone towards interest payments. It also improves financial flexibility and creates room for savings and investment.

Level 4: Saving more

Once expensive debt has been brought under control, the next level is increasing savings.

Saving is one of the most important habits in personal finance because it creates the financial resources needed to meet future obligations without depending entirely on borrowed money.

At this stage, individuals should have a clearer understanding of their income and expenses, established a budget and made progress towards reducing their debt burden.

The money previously used to service expensive loans can now be redirected towards savings.

However, saving requires consistency. It should not depend on whether an individual has money left at the end of the month. Instead, it should be incorporated into the monthly financial plan.

Benjamin Franklin’s advice on wealth creation remains relevant: “If you would be wealthy, think of saving as well as of getting.” The message is simple: earning money is only one part of building wealth; retaining some of it is equally important.

One practical approach is to save immediately after receiving a salary or other income. This is commonly described as paying yourself first.

For example, someone earning N300,000 monthly could decide to save N30,000 before attending to discretionary expenses. Maintaining this habit for a year would produce N360,000, excluding any interest or investment returns.

The amount saved will depend on income, household responsibilities and other financial commitments. People with limited earnings may begin with smaller amounts and increase their contributions as their circumstances improve.

It is also important to distinguish between short-term savings and money intended for long-term investment. Funds needed within a few months should generally be kept in accessible, relatively low-risk accounts or instruments rather than exposed to significant market fluctuations.

The objective at this level is to build a savings culture and develop the discipline to retain part of every income received.

Ov time, consistent savings can provide the foundation for emergency funds, major purchases, business opportunities and investments.

Level 5: Build emergency fund

The fifth level is establishing an emergency fund.

While ordinary savings may be intended for planned purchases and future goals, an emergency fund is specifically designed to provide financial protection when unexpected events occur.

Life is unpredictable. People can lose their jobs, experience medical emergencies, face urgent home repairs or encounter unexpected family responsibilities. Without financial reserves, such situations may force them to borrow money, sell valuable assets or abandon important financial plans.

An emergency fund serves as a financial cushion against these uncertainties.

Financial experts commonly recommend keeping between three and six months of essential living expenses in an emergency fund. However, individuals with irregular incomes, dependants or less secure employment may need to consider a larger reserve.

For instance, if someone’s essential monthly expenses amount to N200,000, a six-month emergency fund would require N1.2m.

The fund should be kept somewhere safe and accessible, such as an appropriate savings account or other suitable low-risk, liquid financial instrument.

It should not be confused with money intended for holidays, shopping, entertainment or speculative investments. Its purpose is to provide support during genuine emergencies.

Having an emergency fund reduces dependence on family members, friends, employers and financial institutions when unexpected expenses arise.

It also provides peace of mind. Individuals who know they have money available to handle temporary financial difficulties are generally better positioned to make rational decisions without the pressure of immediate financial distress.

Building such a reserve may take time, particularly for people with modest incomes. The important thing is to start, contribute regularly and avoid using the money for non-emergency purposes.

Level 6: Increase income streams

The sixth level involves moving beyond dependence on a single source of income.

For many workers, salaries provide financial stability, but relying exclusively on employment income can limit opportunities to build wealth. Job losses, salary delays, economic downturns and unexpected changes in the workplace can expose the vulnerability of depending on one income source.

Developing additional income streams can provide greater financial flexibility and accelerate progress towards financial independence.

One way to achieve this is through a side hustle. Individuals can use their professional skills, experience, interests or available time to provide services outside their regular employment.

For example, a journalist could undertake writing, editing, research or communication consultancy. An accountant could offer bookkeeping services, while a teacher could provide private lessons or develop educational materials.

Other opportunities include catering, fashion design, photography, digital marketing, graphic design, content creation, agricultural ventures, online retail and professional consultancy.

The opportunities are numerous, but individuals must carefully assess the time, resources, skills and capital required before committing to any venture.

A side business should not be started simply because someone else appears to be making money from it. Researching the market, understanding customer needs, calculating costs and identifying potential risks are essential.

It is equally important to manage additional income responsibly. Rather than immediately increasing personal consumption, individuals should consider using part of the proceeds to strengthen savings, expand the business and invest.

Some side hustles may eventually grow into full-time businesses. Others may remain supplementary sources of income. Either outcome can be valuable if the venture is profitable and sustainable.

However, people in paid employment should also consider their employment contracts, workplace policies and the need to maintain a healthy balance between their jobs and additional activities.

The ultimate objective is to develop income sources that reduce financial vulnerability and create more opportunities for wealth accumulation.

Level 7: Start investing

The seventh level is where individuals begin using money as a tool to generate more money.

Saving is essential for financial security, but keeping all one’s money in cash or ordinary savings accounts may not be sufficient to achieve long-term wealth creation. Inflation can gradually reduce the purchasing power of money, making it important to consider appropriate investment opportunities.

Investing involves committing money to assets or financial instruments with the expectation of generating income, capital appreciation or both.

Unlike saving, investing generally involves some degree of risk. The value of investments can rise or fall, and returns are not guaranteed. Therefore, individuals must understand what they are investing in before committing their money.

The importance of looking beyond the price of an investment was captured by renowned investor Warren Buffett, who said, “Price is what you pay; value is what you get.” The observation, which Buffett credited to his mentor Benjamin Graham, highlights the importance of understanding the underlying value of an investment rather than being carried away by its market price.

There are several investment options available, depending on an individual’s financial goals, risk tolerance, investment horizon and available capital.

In Nigeria, these include Federal Government bonds, Treasury bills, commercial papers, mutual funds, equities, exchange-traded funds, real estate and carefully evaluated business investments.

Government securities may appeal to investors seeking relatively predictable returns, while equities offer opportunities to participate in the growth and profitability of listed companies. However, stock prices fluctuate, and investors can lose money.

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds provide opportunities to invest in diversified portfolios without having to select every individual security.

Real estate can generate rental income and potential capital appreciation, although it requires substantial capital and involves expenses relating to maintenance, taxes, documentation and property management.

Investors must also consider liquidity, transaction costs, taxation and the credibility of investment providers.

One important principle is diversification. Rather than committing all available funds to one asset or investment opportunity, investors can spread their money across different asset classes to reduce exposure to a single risk.

It is also important to establish clear investment objectives. Some people invest to generate regular income, others seek long-term capital growth, while some prioritise preserving their capital against inflation.

The investment strategy should reflect these objectives.

Before investing, individuals should ensure that they have adequate emergency savings, manageable debt and sufficient understanding of the risks involved. They should also avoid schemes promising unrealistic or guaranteed returns, particularly those that depend on recruiting new participants.

Professional financial advice may be useful when making significant investment decisions.

Ultimately, investing is about allowing money to work alongside personal effort. Instead of depending entirely on wages or business profits, individuals can gradually build assets capable of generating income and appreciating in value.

Conclusion

The seven levels illustrate that financial independence is a gradual process rather than an achievement that happens overnight.

An individual cannot realistically expect to move from living paycheck to paycheck to becoming a successful investor without first developing the habits and financial discipline required to manage money.

Budgeting creates awareness. Debt repayment reduces financial pressure. Saving builds discipline, while an emergency fund provides protection against uncertainty. Additional income streams create opportunities to accumulate more money, and investing provides a pathway towards long-term wealth creation.

However, these levels should not be treated as rigid rules. People can work on several of them simultaneously, depending on their circumstances. Someone may begin investing small amounts while still repaying manageable debt, provided essential expenses and emergency needs are adequately addressed.

The most important lesson is that financial progress requires intentional decisions. Increasing income without controlling expenditure may not improve one’s financial position. Saving without investing may limit long-term growth, while investing without adequate knowledge can expose hard-earned money to unnecessary risks.

Money is a tool, and how effectively it serves an individual depends largely on the decisions made about earning, spending, saving, borrowing and investing.

The objective should not be to accumulate money merely for the sake of having it. Rather, it should be to achieve a level of financial security that provides choices, reduces dependence on others and creates opportunities to pursue meaningful goals.
