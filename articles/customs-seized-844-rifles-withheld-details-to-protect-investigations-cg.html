Customs seized 844 rifles, withheld details to protect investigations – CG

By Punch Punch 18 Nov 2026 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Comptroller-General of the Nigeria Customs Service, Bashir Adeniyi, on Tuesday said the NCS deliberately withheld immediate public disclosure of major arms

He disclosed this at the Global Alliance World Public Relations Forum in Abuja.

Adeniyi explained that the pressure to speak immediately after security operations could undermine investigations if officials announced unverified figures, made premature accusations or disclosed the identities of persons who had not been properly investigated.

He cited the interception of 844 rifles concealed in a consignment at Onne Port in June 2024 and the subsequent discovery of another 399 rifles in a separate operation as examples of incidents that required careful handling of information.

According to him, the Service adopted a procedure of confirming facts, coordinating with relevant security agencies and ensuring that public statements were consistent before addressing the media.

He said, “In moments like this, the pressure to speak immediately is enormous, and social media will not wait for you. But a wrong figure, a careless accusation or a premature name can damage an investigation and hurt innocent people.

“So our rule is clear. We confirm the facts, we coordinate with security agencies, and then we speak with one voice.”

The Customs chief said the Service also ensured that its public briefing went beyond displaying the recovered weapons to explaining the potential national security implications of allowing them to fall into the wrong hands.

He said the approach was intended to turn enforcement successes into public reassurance while protecting the credibility of the Service.

“When we did the public briefing, we did not only display the weapons. We explained what could have gone wrong if they had fallen into the wrong hands. This is how an enforcement success becomes public reassurance.

“Speed matters, but accuracy is what keeps your credibility for the next crisis,” Adeniyi said.

The Customs boss made the remarks while sharing communication lessons from the Service’s operations and reform initiatives, stressing that effective communication should be treated as a deliberate strategy rather than an afterthought.

He identified the need to verify information before speaking as one of the major lessons the Service learnt from its anti-smuggling operations, particularly those involving sensitive security matters.

Adeniyi also cited the introduction of the B’Odogwu Unified Management System as an example of the importance of preparing stakeholders before implementing major reforms.

He said the Service learnt that the technological aspect of replacing its old cargo clearance platform was easier than managing the transition for importers, licensed agents, terminal operators, government agencies and Customs officers accustomed to the previous system.

He explained that the NCS organised training sessions for agents, established help desks and introduced the platform in phases to minimise disruptions.

“We knew that if we simply switched it on one Monday morning, we would have queues, complaints and headlines by Wednesday. So we went for training before the switch. We held sessions with agents. We opened up the help desk and rolled out in phases, one command at a time.

“We also spoke to our officers first. This is often forgotten. If the officer at the terminal cannot explain a new process with confidence, no press release will save you,” he said.

Adeniyi disclosed that the African Continental Free Trade Area Secretariat prescribed B’Odogwu as a model for other African countries in August.

He said the experience reinforced the need to ensure that reforms were understood and trusted by the people expected to use them.

The Customs chief also recalled the Service’s decision to publish findings from its Time Release Study, which showed that clearing goods at Nigerian ports took about five days, with more than 90 per cent of the period spent on idle time.

He said the Service could have kept the findings confidential to avoid criticism but chose transparency because identifying the problem was necessary to address it.

“Some would have advised us to keep the report in the drawer. We chose to publish it because you cannot fix a problem you are afraid to name,” he said.

Adeniyi explained that Customs was not solely responsible for delays and costs at the ports, noting that terminal operators, shipping lines and other regulatory agencies also contributed to the clearance process.

He said evidence-based communication allowed the Service to acknowledge its shortcomings while holding other stakeholders accountable for their respective responsibilities.

The Customs boss identified the introduction of the Authorised Economic Operator programme as another lesson in using communication to encourage compliance.
