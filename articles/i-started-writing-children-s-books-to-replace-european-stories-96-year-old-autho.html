I started writing children’s books to replace European stories – 96-year-old author, Ade-Ajayi

By Punch Punch 3d ago 9 min read

Early childhood educator and children’s author, Chief (Mrs) Christie Ade-Ajayi, speaks with BIODUN BUSARI about her childhood, marriage, teaching career, passion for storytelling and the legacy she hopes to leave behind

What are your earliest childhood memories?

I was born in Ogbomoso, where my father was a civil servant, but my family is from Ile-Oluji, Ondo State. My mother died when I was three years old, while my brother was one. My father did not want to remarry, so he handed us over to our grandmother, who was a chief in one of the Baptist churches. She looked after us until I was six, when I moved to Osogbo, where my father was then based.

Afterwards, I attended Kudeti Girls’ School in Ibadan, now St Anne’s School. Upon completing my education there, I proceeded to United Missionary College for teacher training. I have fond memories of my British missionary teachers, including Ms Brown and Ms Holmes, who were supported by a few trained Nigerian teachers.

We called our principal ‘Iya’ because she was like a mother to us. She was a strict but loving and caring British woman. She sometimes stayed in her office until about 3am. We heard stories that evil spirits occasionally visited her office in the middle of the night. She would tell them, ‘If you are in the Lord, come with me.’ The spirits would then disappear because they were not of the Lord.

We had many experiences of being taught, trained and mentored by British teachers. At the teacher training college, we had students from different parts of the country, including Igbo, Hausa and Efik students. We had a good time together.

How did your career progress after teacher training?

Teaching was one of the most common professions for women at the time. After our training, we were posted to different schools to teach. We had made friends during our time at the college and kept in touch during short holidays.

I enjoyed the primary teaching course we undertook. Some of us preferred teaching younger children, and I was transferred to Anglican Girls’ School in Ondo in 1952. After spending a few years in my hometown, I returned to Ibadan to continue teaching.

Later, I travelled to London, United Kingdom, and California, United States, where I obtained professional diplomas in child development and elementary school administration. I also worked briefly as a supply teacher in primary schools in London to learn different approaches to teaching young children.

What inspired your passion for children’s storytelling?

After completing our training at UMC and posted to different schools, we discovered that most of the books children read were written by English authors. They learnt about British culture through stories such as Janet and John. I told myself that something had to be done to promote indigenous stories and local experiences. That was how I started writing.

I remember that when I was in London, a young boy touched my skin and asked, ‘Do you bathe?’ He asked because of my skin colour. They had never seen Black people before. I was not offended, but the incident showed how limited their exposure was. Their books reflected their own environment and experiences.

It made me realise that Nigerian children also deserved stories that reflected their culture and experiences rather than relying solely on books written by English authors. I was not the only person who felt that way, but perhaps only two of us out of a hundred people took steps towards achieving that goal.

How did you feel when you began writing children’s books?

I feel very accomplished. There were not many publishers at the time, but some people ventured into publishing and began accepting my books. I felt it was a mission I had to pursue.

I started by drawing on my experiences with my children. For example, Ade, Our Naughty Little Brother was inspired by my only son, who had four sisters. He watched cartoons so much that he would say he wanted to be Superman. He would jump from one place to another and dress like the character. There was also a time when he wanted to dress like his sisters and follow them to parties.

Children enjoyed those kinds of storybooks, and I enjoyed writing them. The knowledge I acquired in London also enriched my teaching experience and improved my understanding of primary education and teaching methods in Nigeria. It contributed to what became a fulfilling career.

What inspired the popular book, Akin Goes to School?

The story was also drawn from my family history. It was based on my father, Akinleye Martins, who was the son of a chief in Ile-Oluji. My grandfather was a warrior who participated in the Yoruba wars. He said Europeans had beaten him, so he decided that his son, my father, would not go to school.

It was only after my uncle attended a college in Oyo and returned to persuade my grandfather that he agreed to allow my father to go to school. In those days, people trekked long distances to school because there were not many vehicles. After much deliberation, my grandfather enrolled my father in school. He later attended Ondo Boys’ High School.

Why did you draw on your family’s experiences to create stories for children?

They were fascinating experiences that could be used to educate and entertain children. I wanted to tell Nigerian stories rather than leave children to read only what English authors had written. We received feedback that children enjoyed the books.

They were real-life experiences adapted into fiction for Nigerian children to enjoy and learn from. I also wanted the stories to contribute to our educational system.

How did you meet your husband?

My husband, Prof J.F. Ade-Ajayi, was one of the foundation students at the University of Ibadan. He met me through my father’s friends. However, my father initially opposed the relationship because my husband wanted to travel to London for his PhD.

He assured my father that he intended to marry me, so I later joined him in London, where we had the opportunity to get to know each other better before getting married.

That was when I attended the Froebel Educational Institute. My husband encouraged me in my career. He taught history at the university and later became Vice-Chancellor of the University of Lagos in 1972. Whenever he was on sabbatical, he encouraged me to visit schools in England and the United States to learn more about early childhood education. Those experiences further strengthened my interest in writing.

Did any of your children develop an interest in your career?

None of them chose my profession. They each followed their own interests and have done well. One studied law, another studied medicine and is a surgeon in the UK. I also have a bank executive, an architect and estate manager, and an administrator who works as an admissions manager at a university in the United States.

I am happy that all of them are successful and fulfilled in their careers.

How would you describe your contributions to teaching and children’s literature beyond Nigeria?

In Nigeria, I held several positions at the University of Ibadan Staff School before my husband became a vice-chancellor. While we were at UNILAG, I served as president of the University of Lagos Women Society, where I introduced programmes to improve conditions for young children in the nursery section.

I also participated in several organisations focused on early childhood education. I attended conferences organised by the Organisation Mondiale pour l’Éducation Préscolaire in Japan, England and the United States. OMEP translates into English as the World Organisation for Early Childhood Education. It was established after some women went to war, leaving their children behind, but never returned. It was formed to ensure that those children received proper care and education.

I was also active in the International Board on Books for Young People, UNICEF, ZONTA and other organisations. I have continued to encourage women in Nigeria to pay more attention to early childhood education and women’s rights. It has been a fulfilling journey.

What legacy do you hope to leave for younger writers and Nigerian youths?

My husband left a library where people can study free of charge. It is also part of my legacy and that of our family. Researchers, undergraduates and other students visit to read and study without paying. His books, publications and other materials from his years at the university are kept there. I have also donated copies of my books for people to read and study.

We have a librarian who manages the facility, and we hope our children will sustain it. My husband and I believed that young people who read and study would make meaningful contributions to society and improve their lives.

I pray for young people and mentor them as much as I can. Since COVID-19, I have continued to pray for Nigeria and its youths, especially after seeing many young people leave the country.

What are the secrets to your good health and longevity at 96?

I believe we must remain close to God. I hold on to Him because He has kept me alive. I pray and try to do good to others. My husband and I have helped many young people by sponsoring their education through secondary school and university.

There are blessings that come from doing good to others. Love is the most important quality anyone can show towards others. We must care for people and avoid being selfish.

What values from your generation do you think are missing in society today?

Helping others is no longer as common as it used to be. In the past, people readily showed love and extended assistance to others, but that is not happening as much today.

Over the past two years, I have written to groups of women about how we can engage the government to support children and young people. Some of them asked whether the government would actually help. Such initiatives are not forthcoming.

There is a lack of love and support in society. If we fail to help people, some may turn to crime, including kidnapping. The government, parents and people in positions of authority must take responsibility for helping children and young people build better lives.

The Bible says that the love of money is the root of all evil. Many young people are desperate for money, and this can push some into criminal activities that endanger lives. What we should all focus on is showing love and care for others.
