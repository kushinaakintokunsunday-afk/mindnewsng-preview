Akodo-Ise: Sea incursion threatens to wipe out Lagos coastal community

By Punch Punch 3d ago 13 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Residents of Akodo-Ise in Ibeju-Lekki area of Lagos lament how advancing seawater is eroding their livelihoods, cultural identity and hopes of remaining on their ancestral land, DARE AKOGUN reports.

The Baale of Akoko-Ise, Lateef Temitope, no longer sees the sea as a source of livelihood for fishermen.

It has become a threat to the community he inherited, the family home where he grew up and the ancestral land that connects generations of his family.

Located in Ibeju-Lekki Local Government Area of Lagos State, rising waters have swallowed the chief family’s house and coconut farmland, forcing him to move into a rented apartment.

Places that once defined his childhood have either disappeared beneath the water or become increasingly difficult to reach.

About 20 years ago, Temitope recalled, walking from the family’s coconut plantation to the ocean took roughly 30 minutes. His father’s house was only about five minutes from the farm.

The Baale said the community began noticing significant changes around 2016, as seawater increasingly encroached on the shoreline and threatened homes and farms.

By 2023, the situation had become so severe that about 40 palm and coconut trees were falling into the water daily for roughly a month.

Residents who depended on the land for food and income also lamented to Saturday PUNCH about the losses they have incurred and how their source of livelihood is gradually disappearing.

“We did not know what was happening until some environmental people came and explained it to us,” Temitope said, recalling how residents were introduced to the term ‘ocean surge’.

Since then, the community has watched the water claim homes, farmland and public infrastructure.

The Baale said the local primary school was also swallowed by the sea last year, leaving residents increasingly anxious about the future of their settlement.

Fishing under threat

For generations, fishing has sustained families in Akodo-Ise. The community’s proximity to the Atlantic Ocean and its network of waterways provided residents with access to fish and other aquatic resources, supporting fishermen, fish sellers and processors.

But residents say the changing coastline has made fishing increasingly difficult and expensive.

Temitope said the fishermen who once worked relatively close to the community now travel several kilometres into the lagoon in search of catches that are often smaller than what they used to bring home.

Women who buy fish for processing and trading also struggle to obtain enough fresh supplies locally. Some now travel to Epe to purchase fish, adding transport costs to already strained household businesses.

The threat is not limited to declining catches. Residents also recount incidents in which strong waves damaged or swept away fishing equipment, leaving fishermen with substantial replacement costs.

The Baale recalled an incident about two years ago in which waves reportedly dragged away approximately 15 fishing boats and nets. Some of the affected fishermen had to borrow money to replace their equipment.

A 35-year-old fisherman, Seun Rasaki, described the uncertainty which has now become part of his everyday life.

He said he now worries that his boat and equipment could be swept away whenever the water becomes turbulent. To reduce the risk, he moves his boat closer to his house and keeps its engine indoors.

Another fisherman, Femi Johnson, 42, said he was among those who lost a boat and fishing net during the incident involving about 15 fishermen. He subsequently took a cooperative loan to replace his boat, net and engine.

The financial burden has continued beyond the cost of replacing the equipment. Johnson said he now spends considerably more on fuel because he has to travel farther to find fish.

“Before, five litres of fuel could take me for a fishing trip, but now I use about 35 litres,” he said.

At the prevailing price of N1,400 per litre, that amounts to about N49,000 for fuel alone on a trip, before other operating expenses are considered.

For a fisherman whose income depends on the size of his catch, the additional cost leaves less money to support his family and repay loans.

Wale Mutiu, another fisherman, said his monthly earnings, which previously ranged between N300,000 and N500,000, had fallen sharply. He now struggles to make N100,000 in some months.

He also said he had lost about three boats within a year, with replacing one costing between N2.5m and N3m.

The women in the fish trade are also hit by the layers of hardship caused by the advancing seawater in the community.

A fisherwoman, Esther Fawole, 35, said her house was destroyed in August, leaving her and her family to cope with the loss of shelter while her husband’s fishing business also suffered.

She said her husband’s boat was damaged, while the reduced availability of fish made it difficult for her to sustain her trading business.

Fawole now smokes and sells fish around the Law School area of Ajah and sometimes joins friends travelling to Epe market to buy supplies.

Her husband, she said, has resorted to riding a commercial tricycle for another person while saving towards buying a replacement boat engine.

A widow, Wuraola Sanusi, said she had also lost an inherited coconut farm and could no longer depend on the community’s fishing activities for a steady supply of fish.

The 39-year-old now travels to Epe to buy fish, but transport expenses reduce her profit. With four children to support, she said the additional costs have made it increasingly difficult to meet household needs.

Ancestral land and graves disappearing

Beyond the loss of homes and livelihoods, residents say the advancing sea is threatening something that cannot easily be replaced – their ancestral heritage.

Speaking on how his family’s history is rooted in the community, Temitope, the Baale, said his forefathers are buried there, and the land holds memories that cannot simply be transferred to another settlement.

He said about 20 ancestral burial grounds had been relocated because of the advancing water. One of the affected graves belonged to his grandfather, who died in 1956.

The relocation of ancestral remains carries significance beyond the physical movement of a grave.

In Yoruba communities, burial grounds can represent family continuity, ancestral identity and spiritual connections between the living and the dead.

Relocating remains may also involve traditional rites and other cultural obligations, depending on the family and community.

Temitope said the community’s attachment to its ancestral territory makes relocation a difficult option, even as residents confront growing environmental risks.

“We have nowhere else to go. This is where our forefathers lived and where they were buried,” he said.

His concern is that, without meaningful intervention, residents could eventually be forced away from the place that defines their identity.

An uncertain future

Temitope estimated the population of Akodo-Ise at between 600 and 800 people; he said residents were increasingly worried about the safety of their homes, particularly at night, when the sound and movement of the water heighten fears that more land could be lost.

“We cry out to the government that time when Akodo-Ise and about 12 neighbouring settlements staged a protest in 2025, officials of the Lagos State Ministry of Waterfront Infrastructure Development visited the community about two years ago; we didn’t receive any clear feedback on what action would follow the visit,” he said.

The other communities he identified included Imedu, Akodo-Ise, Okun-Ise, Folu, Ikegun, Lekia, Oke Gelu, Oto Ilu, Ori Yanrin, Ita Maarun, Apaki and Ijekodo; their concerns, he said, were driven by the fear that the advancing water could eventually make several settlements uninhabitable.

The Baale said he hoped the government would consider interventions similar to coastal protection projects undertaken in parts of Lagos, including Victoria Island.

What is driving the sea inland?

Although residents describe the changes they have witnessed, establishing the precise causes of coastal retreat in Akodo-Ise requires scientific investigation.

According to scientists, coastal erosion, sea-level rise, storm surges and tidal flooding are related but distinct processes.

While erosion involves the removal of sediment from the shoreline, sea-level rise raises the baseline level of the ocean and storm surges produce temporary rises in water levels during storms.

Tidal flooding occurs when high tides inundate low-lying areas, and environmentalists have said these processes can interact, making vulnerable coastal settlements more exposed to flooding and land loss.

Checks by Saturday PUNCH showed that a 2026 study titled ‘Monitoring Coastal Erosion-Driven LULC and Shoreline Changes Along the Lagos Coastal Corridor with Sentinel-2 Imagery and DSAS’, published in the journal Geomatics, Natural Hazards and Risk, appears to provide a wider picture of the pressures affecting the Lagos coastline.

The researchers, Ahmed Abdulrahaman, Aqil Tariq,  Zuliehat Abdullahi, Oluwatosin, Abolaji Afolayan, Dmitry Kucher, Sajid Ullah, Hela Elmannai and Yahia Said, examined land-use and land-cover changes along a 180-kilometre coastal corridor between 2015 and 2025, using satellite imagery and the Digital Shoreline Analysis System.

The study found that built-up areas increased from 22 per cent, representing 283.61 square kilometres, in 2015 to 53 per cent, or 683.96 square kilometres, in 2025.

Vegetation cover declined from 31 per cent to 19 per cent, while the proportion classified as water bodies fell from 19 per cent to four per cent.

Wetlands, however, increased from nine per cent to 24 per cent, a change the researchers associated with factors including inundation and sediment redistribution, alongside the limitations of land-cover classification.

The analysis also used 3,658 shoreline transects to assess coastal movement. It recorded erosion rates of up to 25.1 metres per year in parts of the Okun-Ajah and Ibeju-Lekki axis, while some depositional areas recorded accretion of up to 32.29 metres per year.

The findings point to significant and uneven changes along the Lagos coastline.

Commenting on this, an environmental expert at the Greening Space Institute, Grace Adeyinka, said activities that disrupt the natural movement of sediment could destabilise shorelines.

She explained that dredging and major coastal developments could alter sediment flows, potentially leaving some sections of the coast more exposed to wave action.

“Where sand is no longer replenished naturally at the same rate it is removed, waves can exert greater pressure on the shoreline, increasing the risk of erosion,” Adeyinka said.

Vanishing natural protection

The mangroves that form part of the coastal ecosystem also matter to the future of communities such as Akodo-Ise.

Mangroves provide habitats for fish and other aquatic organisms; help stabilise shorelines and can reduce the force of waves. Their dense roots trap sediment and create sheltered environments where young fish, crabs and shrimp can develop.

The Managing Consultant of SmitBridge Consulting, Dr Samuel Babatunde, said mangroves play a critical role in sustaining fisheries.

“Mangroves are the nursery of the fishery. Many fish, shrimp and crab species spend their early life among those roots. Lose the nursery and catches fall, which hits the main livelihood in Ayetoro,” he said.

Although Babatunde, who is also a lead technical and consortium partner to the Ondo State Oil Producing Areas Development Commission, made the observation in reference to Ayetoro, the ecological function he described appear relevant to mangrove ecosystems elsewhere.

He further stated that mangrove restoration must be based on the conditions of the site, warning that planting seedlings along an actively eroding shoreline without measures to reduce wave energy and retain sediment could lead to failure.

Babatunde cited a mangrove-planting exercise undertaken at Akodo-Ise in May 2026 with Rotary and other partners, during which some seedlings reportedly survived while others were washed away.

The experience, he said, demonstrated the importance of understanding local hydrology and shoreline conditions before undertaking restoration.

A study conducted in 2026 by Senakpon Tcheton, a GreenSTEM scholar from the University of the Witwatersrand, South Africa, provided further insight into the relationship between mangroves and livelihoods in Lagos.

Working under the supervision of Professor Ismail Ibraheem, and Dr Temitope Sogbanmu of the University of Lagos Department of Zoology, Tcheton conducted field research between February and August 2026 on the ecological role of mangrove forests in the state.

One of the study’s important findings was that mangrove roots served as fish nurseries and were central to local livelihoods. The research also identified stronger grassroots conservation efforts in the community than those observed during the preliminary assessment in Epe.

Nigeria’s promises to protect its coastal communities are increasingly being tested by the reality confronting residents of Akodo-Ise, where advancing seawater is steadily eroding homes, livelihoods and ancestral heritage.

Plans by FG and Lagos

Over the years, the Federal Government has made ambitious commitments to protect marine ecosystems, restore mangroves and strengthen the resilience of vulnerable coastal settlements under its Third Nationally Determined Contribution.

Submitted to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change in September 2025, Nigeria’s NDC 3.0 sets out a range of measures that could make a difference to communities such as Akodo-Ise.

They include the conservation and restoration of 13,000 hectares of mangroves, the protection of coastal wetlands, the establishment of marine protected areas and measures to make fisheries more resilient to climate change.

The document also recognises that climate change threatens more than the physical environment.

It proposes insurance for vulnerable entrepreneurs, support for climate-affected livelihoods, disaster-risk management plans for frontline communities and mechanisms to address climate-induced displacement.

It further commits to identifying and protecting cultural heritage sites threatened by climate change.

Similarly, the Lagos State Government has acknowledged the severity of coastal erosion along its shoreline.

In May 2025, the state Commissioner for Waterfront Infrastructure Development, Yacoob Alebiosu, announced plans to strengthen coastal protection through partnerships and the possible deployment of groyne technology.

The commissioner also acknowledged the losses suffered by communities including Akodo-Ise, Itoke and Origanrigan.

In June 2026, the state government disclosed that it had engaged Dutch consultancy firm CDR International to undertake studies that would guide shoreline-protection interventions. It also said it was strengthening surveillance against illegal dredging and working with relevant stakeholders to address coastal degradation.

The funding question is equally important. Nigeria’s NDC 3.0 estimates that the country will require about $337bn between 2026 and 2035 to implement its climate commitments, including approximately $141.5bn for adaptation.

Financing required

Speaking to Saturday PUNCH, the Executive Director of the International Climate Change Development Initiative, Olumide Idowu, said that the national financing requirement is not the same as money released for vulnerable communities.

“Without clearly identified allocations, implementation timelines and accountable institutions, commitments to restore ecosystems and protect livelihoods risk remaining distant from the people who need them most.

“There is also the question of coordination. Protecting Akodo-Ise requires more than planting mangroves or constructing a coastal barrier. It involves scientific assessment of shoreline changes, regulation of dredging, monitoring of coastal developments, protection of fishing grounds, livelihood support and the preservation of cultural heritage.

“The challenge is particularly urgent because coastal interventions must be based on the physical characteristics of each location. Poorly designed groynes, seawalls or other structures can alter sediment movement and transfer erosion to neighbouring stretches of coastline,” Idowu said.

Lagos keeps mum

Saturday PUNCH learnt that the Lagos State Government divided responsibilities for addressing coastal and environmental challenges among relevant ministries.
While the Ministry of the Environment and Water Resources, headed by Tokunbo Wahab, is responsible for flooding, ocean surges and climate adaptation, the Ministry of Waterfront Infrastructure Development, led by Dayo Bush-Alebiosu, oversees coastal erosion and shoreline protection projects, including the construction of groynes.

However, efforts to obtain Wahab’s reaction to the concerns raised by residents were unsuccessful. WhatsApp messages sent to his phone were not answered, while calls to his number did not connect as of the time of filing this report.

Also, attempts to reach the Commissioner for Physical Planning and Urban Development, Dr Oluyinka Abiodun Olumide, were unsuccessful. His ministry works with the Ministry of Waterfront Infrastructure Development on enforcement against illegal dredging and reclamation activities along the Lagos Lagoon.
