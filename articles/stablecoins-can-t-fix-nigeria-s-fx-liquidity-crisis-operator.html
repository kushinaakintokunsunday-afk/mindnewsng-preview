Stablecoins can’t fix Nigeria’s FX liquidity crisis – Operator

By Punch Punch 16h ago 14 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

CEO of Luno Nigeria, Ayotunde Alabi, speaks to FELIX OLOYEDE on how Africa can leverage blockchain technology to tackle cross-border payments challenges For yea

CEO of Luno Nigeria, Ayotunde Alabi, speaks to FELIX OLOYEDE on how Africa can leverage blockchain technology to tackle cross-border payments challenges

For years, cryptocurrency discussions across Africa have largely focused on trading and speculation. What has changed to suggest that the greater opportunity now lies in the underlying infrastructure supporting financial transactions?

I think what has changed is that we are beginning to understand where the real economic value of digital assets lies.

For much of the last decade, the conversation was understandably dominated by cryptocurrency prices, trading activity and investment returns. But while that conversation was happening, the underlying technology was quietly demonstrating something potentially more significant: the ability to transfer and settle value across different markets without relying on the traditional chain of intermediaries.

For Africa, that is particularly relevant. We have significant trade, remittance and capital flows across borders, yet the infrastructure supporting these transactions remains fragmented, expensive and, in many cases, inefficient.

What excites me is that we are moving beyond asking whether people should buy cryptocurrencies to asking how this technology can help banks, payment companies and businesses move money more efficiently.

At Luno, we increasingly see digital assets not simply as an investment category, but as an additional layer of financial infrastructure. I believe the long-term opportunity is considerably bigger than trading alone.

Nigeria already has a sophisticated payments ecosystem, with banks, fintechs and payment companies moving money at scale. What specific problems in that existing infrastructure do you believe blockchain and stablecoin technology can realistically solve?

Nigeria has built one of Africa’s most sophisticated domestic payment ecosystems. We can move naira between bank accounts almost instantaneously, and that is an achievement we should be proud of.

However, the experience changes considerably when that same transaction needs to cross an international border.

A Nigerian business paying a supplier in another African country may have to navigate multiple banks, currency conversions, correspondent banking relationships and different settlement arrangements.

Blockchain infrastructure can help simplify the movement and settlement of value between financial institutions. Stablecoins, particularly those backed by credible and liquid currencies, can provide a common settlement instrument across otherwise disconnected markets.

There are also opportunities around treasury management, transaction transparency and the ability to operate beyond conventional banking hours.

The important distinction is that we are not suggesting blockchain should replace Nigeria’s existing payment infrastructure. In fact, the greatest value may come from connecting it to global financial networks more efficiently.

Nigeria has already solved a significant part of the domestic payments problem. The next opportunity is improving how our financial system interacts with the rest of the world.

Cross-border transactions remain costly and sometimes time-consuming. Where do you see stablecoins making the most practical impact on Nigeria’s international financial flows, whether through remittances, business payments, trade, treasury management or other areas?

I see the most immediate opportunity in business payments, trade settlement and institutional treasury operations.

Consider a Nigerian importer who needs to settle an invoice with an overseas supplier. Beyond finding the necessary foreign currency, the business must manage settlement timelines, intermediary costs and uncertainty around when the beneficiary will receive the funds.

A properly structured stablecoin settlement arrangement can potentially reduce some of that friction, particularly where the participating financial institutions have access to reliable liquidity and compliant conversion channels.

There is also a compelling opportunity for fintechs, international money transfer operators and payment companies that need to manage liquidity across multiple markets.

Remittances are another important use case, but I believe institutional adoption will be particularly consequential because of the transaction volumes involved and the potential efficiency gains.

At Luno, this informs our increasing focus on businesses and financial institutions. Rather than expecting every consumer to understand stablecoins, we can provide infrastructure that enables regulated businesses to deliver better financial services to their customers.

Ultimately, the customer may not even know that blockchain technology is involved. They will simply experience a faster, more predictable transaction.

New financial technologies often fall short of their promises when deployed on a large scale. What specific challenges in cross-border payments can stablecoins realistically address, and which obstacles are likely to persist regardless of technological advances?

This is an important distinction because I think the industry sometimes overstates what technology alone can achieve.

Stablecoins can materially improve the speed and availability of the settlement leg of a transaction. They can reduce dependence on multiple correspondent banking intermediaries and facilitate the transfer of value across networks that operate continuously.

However, stablecoins do not remove the fundamental economic and regulatory constraints associated with international money transfers.

You still need foreign exchange liquidity. You still need compliant entry and exit points into local currencies. You must comply with anti-money-laundering requirements, sanctions screening and applicable capital flow regulations.

For example, moving a dollar stablecoin between two wallets can happen very quickly. But if the recipient needs naira deposited into a Nigerian bank account, the quality of the local conversion and payout infrastructure becomes just as important as the blockchain transaction itself.

This is why I believe the winning proposition is not simply a stablecoin. It is the combination of liquidity, banking relationships, regulatory permissions and reliable settlement infrastructure.

Technology can make money move faster, but the surrounding financial infrastructure determines whether that movement creates real economic value.

Luno has largely been recognised as a consumer-focused cryptocurrency platform. What insights have you gained from operating in Nigeria that led you to identify greater opportunities in developing infrastructure to support other financial service providers?

One of the most important lessons from operating in Nigeria is that the demand for digital assets extends well beyond individuals looking to invest or trade.

Over time, we have seen increasing interest from businesses seeking solutions around liquidity, international settlement, treasury operations and access to digital asset markets.

We also realised that many financial institutions are interested in participating in the digital asset economy, but understandably do not want to build the entire infrastructure themselves.

Building a secure custody solution, establishing liquidity relationships, maintaining compliance controls and integrating blockchain networks requires substantial investment and specialist expertise.

Luno has spent more than a decade developing many of these capabilities.

The opportunity, therefore, is to make that infrastructure available to other businesses rather than limiting its usefulness to customers who transact directly on the Luno platform.

For me, that represents a natural evolution of our business. We are taking capabilities developed through years of serving retail customers and making them relevant to a much broader financial ecosystem.

Luno is expanding its engagement with banks, fintechs and payment companies through its B2B infrastructure strategy. How does this shift redefine its position in Nigeria’s financial ecosystem, from serving individual cryptocurrency users to providing the underlying infrastructure that other businesses can leverage?

It changes the nature of our relationship with the broader financial services industry quite significantly.

Historically, the typical relationship with Luno involved a customer coming onto our platform to buy, sell or hold a digital asset.

What we are now building is a model where another financial institution can integrate some of those capabilities into its own products and customer experience.

For example, a bank or fintech may want to offer digital asset access, institutional custody or stablecoin-enabled settlement without building a cryptocurrency exchange or managing the technical complexity of blockchain infrastructure.

Through propositions such as Crypto as a Service, we can provide components of that infrastructure while the institution maintains its own customer relationship.

Our broader ecosystem capabilities, including BlockTower’s stablecoin infrastructure and GTXN’s international payment and settlement capabilities, create opportunities to address more of the financial transaction lifecycle, subject to the appropriate regulatory permissions.

This moves Luno beyond being solely a platform customers transact on to potentially becoming an infrastructure partner for businesses serving much larger customer bases.

Importantly, this does not mean we are abandoning our retail business. It means we are extending the value of our existing capabilities into additional markets and customer segments.

If stablecoins become an integral part of financial infrastructure, how will this reshape the traditional banking sector? Will banks increasingly compete with cryptocurrency firms, collaborate with them or integrate the technology into their existing operations?

I believe partnership and integration will ultimately be much more important than competition.

Banks remain fundamental to the financial system. They provide deposit-taking, lending, access to domestic payment systems, regulatory oversight and established relationships with businesses and consumers.

What blockchain technology potentially introduces is a more efficient mechanism for transferring and settling value, particularly across borders.

I can envisage a financial system where a bank continues to provide the customer account, compliance framework and banking relationship, while integrating digital asset infrastructure into the settlement process.

In that scenario, the customer does not necessarily need to leave their bank or maintain a separate cryptocurrency account to benefit from the technology.

Of course, there will be competition in certain areas, particularly around international payments and transaction costs. But competition is not inherently negative if it encourages efficiency and better customer outcomes.

I do not see the future as banks on one side and crypto companies on the other. I see a future where the distinction becomes less relevant as digital asset capabilities are increasingly incorporated into mainstream financial services.

Nigeria’s regulatory framework for digital assets continues to develop. As an operator in this sector, what additional regulatory clarity is required to move digital-asset infrastructure beyond experimentation and towards widespread adoption in mainstream financial services?

Nigeria has made meaningful progress, particularly through the SEC’s regulatory framework for digital assets and the growing engagement between industry participants and financial regulators.

However, for digital asset infrastructure to achieve mainstream institutional adoption, there needs to be greater clarity around the intersection of capital markets regulation, banking, payments and foreign exchange.

For example, a digital asset exchange, a stablecoin issuer and a company providing international settlement infrastructure may all use blockchain technology, but they perform fundamentally different economic functions and present different regulatory risks.

It is important that the framework recognises those distinctions.

We also need clear rules around institutional participation, stablecoin issuance and redemption, reserve management, access to banking services and permissible use cases for digital assets in cross-border financial transactions.

I am particularly encouraged by the move towards greater coordination between the SEC, CBN and other relevant authorities.

Ultimately, businesses need regulatory certainty to make long-term investments, while financial institutions need confidence that the infrastructure they are integrating is properly supervised.

The objective should be a framework that protects the financial system without unnecessarily limiting responsible innovation.

Luno recently joined the SEC’s Accelerated Regulatory Incubation Programme. How does participation in the initiative change the way the company operates, and what insights have you gained so far about the requirements for responsible digital-asset innovation in Nigeria?

Our admission into the SEC’s Accelerated Regulatory Incubation Programme is an important milestone for Luno Nigeria and reflects years of engagement with regulators around the responsible development of the digital asset industry.

Importantly, admission into the programme provides an Approval in Principle, not an unrestricted or final operating licence. It allows us to operate within the scope and conditions established by the Commission while progressing through the applicable regulatory requirements.

What I particularly value is the opportunity for more structured engagement with the regulator.

It creates an environment in which regulators can better understand the technology, assess our operational controls and evaluate how these products function in practice.

One thing our experience has reinforced is that responsible innovation cannot be an afterthought.

You need strong corporate governance, effective compliance systems, appropriate custody arrangements, sound risk management and clear accountability from the outset.

For Luno, regulation and innovation are not opposing objectives. In fact, I believe regulatory credibility will become one of the strongest competitive advantages for businesses seeking to serve institutional customers.

If digital assets are going to form part of mainstream financial infrastructure, they must meet the standards of trust, security and accountability expected of any serious financial institution.

Stablecoins are frequently touted as a means of easing foreign exchange and settlement challenges. However, Nigeria must also consider its domestic currency and monetary policy objectives. What factors should policymakers and financial institutions consider as stablecoins become more widely integrated into financial transactions?

I think policymakers need to approach this from two perspectives: the efficiency benefits of the technology and the broader implications for monetary and financial stability.

Stablecoins can help facilitate international settlement, but we must recognise that the widespread use of foreign currency-denominated instruments can have implications for currency substitution, capital flows and the transmission of monetary policy.

For a country like Nigeria, those considerations are particularly important.

The objective should not be to create an alternative monetary system operating outside established safeguards. Rather, it should be to determine how digital settlement infrastructure can support legitimate economic activity while remaining consistent with Nigeria’s monetary and foreign exchange framework.

This means paying attention to transparency of flows, reserve quality, redemption arrangements, regulatory reporting and the institutions permitted to facilitate conversion between digital assets and local currency.

I also believe there is an important conversation to be had around properly regulated local currency stablecoins and other forms of tokenised money, although their usefulness will ultimately depend on adoption, liquidity and trust.

We should not confuse the benefits of blockchain settlement with the need to encourage foreign currency dependence.

A well-designed framework should allow Nigeria to benefit from technological innovation while preserving the authority of its monetary institutions.

The growing adoption of stablecoins also raises concerns around consumer protection, cybersecurity, reliance on private issuers and emerging forms of financial concentration. What are the major risks the industry must address as this infrastructure evolves?

I would group the major risks into three broad areas: financial integrity, operational resilience and trust.

First, a stablecoin is only as credible as the assets and governance arrangements supporting it. If an issuer cannot demonstrate adequate reserves, transparent redemption arrangements and sound financial management, the technology underlying the token becomes almost irrelevant.

Second, there are operational and cybersecurity risks. Blockchain transactions may settle quickly, but that also means mistakes, compromised credentials and unauthorised transfers can be difficult to reverse.

Third, as financial institutions increasingly depend on a smaller number of infrastructure providers, we must be conscious of concentration risk and the consequences of operational failures across interconnected systems.

There are also legitimate concerns around illicit finance, consumer understanding and the responsibilities of the institutions offering these products.

My view is that the industry should be held to rigorous standards, particularly where its infrastructure begins to support critical financial activity.

At Luno, our experience has taught us that trust is built through consistent investment in security, compliance, governance and transparency.

The industry will not achieve sustainable adoption simply by demonstrating that the technology works. It must demonstrate that the entire financial proposition is safe, reliable and accountable.

How do you envision Nigeria’s financial system evolving with widespread adoption of blockchain-based infrastructure, and how could this transform cross-border transactions for individuals and businesses in the country?

I think the most meaningful change would be that the experience of moving money internationally begins to resemble the experience Nigerians already enjoy when making domestic transfers.

Imagine a Nigerian business paying a supplier in Kenya, South Africa or Europe with greater visibility over the exchange rate, transaction costs and expected settlement time, without having to understand the complexities of the underlying financial infrastructure.

Imagine Nigerian fintechs and banks being able to offer international payment services through interoperable networks rather than having to establish separate arrangements across every market.

That would have important implications for trade, business competitiveness and the ability of African companies to participate in the global economy.

But I do not believe this future will be built by blockchain companies acting in isolation. It will require collaboration between banks, fintechs, payment providers, liquidity providers and regulators.

For me, the real measure of success will not be how many Nigerians own cryptocurrencies. It will be whether the technology helps businesses transact more efficiently, lowers the cost of financial services and makes our financial system better connected to global markets.

That is the opportunity we are pursuing at Luno, and I believe it is one of the most consequential developments in the next phase of Africa’s financial evolution.
