On food inflation and agriculture

By Punch Punch 1d ago 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Food, a major and the first basic need for human existence, remains one of the main items of concern in Nigeria’s developmental trajectory. Each time inflation figures are released by the National Bureau of Statistics, food inflation figures usually show up like a cancerous sore. But we move on as if it does not matter, yet it is one of the figures used in poverty calculation. The leadership of a country that cannot feed its populace should bury itself in shame, even though agriculture is better handled by the private sector.

Policy, design, and the push or commitment to food self-sufficiency should come from the government. The priority the government gives agriculture would serve as an impetus for the private sector to develop the agricultural sector. Nigeria is blessed with abundant human and non-human resources that can enable any serious, people-centred, and non-corrupt government to govern at an exceptional level. Beyond abundant mineral resources underground, the surface soil is rich in everything needed for all kinds of plants to grow. If an asphalt-laid road is abandoned for just three months, grass would emerge from any available crack and grow luxuriantly within weeks. How can a country with such blessings go hungry?

 It is time the government is intentional about agriculture. It is not going to be agricultural reform but agricultural revolution, as there seems to be no agricultural policy on the ground. Everything relating to agricultural production seems to be in the cooler. There are ministries that, if mentioned, the principal actor, the Minister, is immediately recognised; not so with agriculture. The “actor” seems not active. I listened to a television programme where students were asked to mention the names of ministers of “top” ministries like works, education, health and social welfare, finance, agriculture and labour. None of the students remembered the name of the Minister for Agriculture! One of the organisers said the Minister of Agriculture was probably a silent achiever. The ministry deserves an aggressive achiever.

 Whenever the government talked of releasing grains from the national reserves, I felt proud until I learnt that what we have in the reserves is imported grains. For many years, we have observed that concentration on oil production and financial rewards from the sector has diverted the attention of the government and the non-public sector away from other sectors that can equally provide income or revenues and, more importantly, generate employment, like agriculture, which was hitherto the mainstay of the Nigerian economy. Importation of food items into Nigeria should be limited to manufactured items; we do not have the raw materials, and that too should be a temporary issue.

 Just recently, a report emerged that the Republic of Benin has regained its top spot as a main producer and exporter of cotton.  Nigeria was a major exporter of cotton during the First Republic. Albeit the Nigerian government’s concern presently should be how to become self-sufficient in the production of agricultural produce we currently import. This is to reduce our dependence on other countries for food and industrial raw materials.

 The agricultural revolution required must be championed by the Federal Government through policy formulation, general coordination, and consistent/sustainable funding.

 The last time Nigeria had an agricultural revolution scheme, like President Shehu Shagari’s Green Revolution, it was more sloganeering than practical action. Now, we need to be practical in defining what we want to achieve; when, in terms of timeline; the modus operandi; and the deliverables. We may need to concentrate production firstly on some products, before moving to others and many others within the food crop gangre and the cash crop sector. Many models of agricultural revolution can be adopted or adapted around the world.

 India and China are the most populous countries in the world, with each boasting over one billion citizens. They are self-sufficient in food and also export food to other countries. Nigeria, with a population less than one-quarter of the population of these two giants, imports food from around the world. The Green Revolution of India in the mid-to-late 1960s helped India shift from food shortages and heavy grain imports to food grain self-sufficiency. High-yielding varieties of rice and wheat were introduced with the use of chemical fertilizers, irrigation and use of modern farming machinery.

 Other major agricultural revolutions in India are White Revolution, or Operation Flood, which boosted milk and dairy production through the establishment of dairy cooperatives. The Blue Revolution led to the expansion of fisheries and aquaculture, while the Yellow Revolution resulted in increased oilseed production, and the Golden Revolution focused on horticulture, fruits, and honey. The Silver and Pink Revolutions were carried out to take care of poultry (silver), pharmaceuticals, prawns, and onions (pink); and Evergreen Revolution was an omnibus action geared toward overall productivity, growth and ecological sustainability.

 China has less than 10 per cent arable land for agriculture but a massive population. The government’s programmes on food for all were taken by the jugular and executed with honest determination. The Maoist revolution involved land reform and collective farms, expanded irrigation, and double-cropping in the early 1950s. The post-1978 Household Responsibility System, which replaced communes with household contracts and market incentives, sparked rapid output growth in all aspects of agriculture and shifted diets towards more vegetables, meat, and dairy. China is now undergoing a modern high-tech transformation. This involves land consolidation, greenhouses, drones, mechanization, and high agrotechnology investments.

 Reports show that Israel’s agricultural revolution took the shape of necessity-driven, high-tech farming combined with cooperative organisation and strict water management. The model involved drip irrigation, wastewater reclamation, natural infrastructure, and desalination methods, as well as Cooperative Farming Models, the type that was introduced in the Western Region during Chief Obafemi Awolowo’s leadership.

 Agriculture accounted for over 60 per cent of the Gross Domestic Product and about 85 per cent of foreign exchange earnings of Nigeria at independence in 1960. These were derived from cash crops from the regions. Cocoa was from the Western Region, groundnut and cotton from the Northern Region, and Palm oil from the Eastern Region. The crude oil boom of the 1970s nearly eliminated agriculture’s importance in terms of income and job opportunities, as the sector’s dominance declined steeply.

 To reverse the decline, a number of Federal Government interventions were taken. National Accelerated Food Production Programme was launched in 1972; Operation Feed the Nation in 1976; the River Basin Development Authority in 1978; and the Green Revolution Programme in 1980. Some other minor initiatives also took place. The programmes could not succeed because of inconsistent funding, poor agricultural infrastructure, policy reversal and the main impediment to national progress – corruption. The recent framework, the National Agricultural Technology and Innovation Policy, which focuses on commercialisation of agriculture and boosting local production, does not carry the required revolutionary measures that the agricultural sector deserves today.

 Nigeria cannot continue to rely on food importation to feed itself or to stock its reserves. We have the land, we have the human resources, and the technology is available, but we are looking for sustainable financing and policy consistency. Nigeria can adopt the Indian model of sub-sector revolution. In this connection, the focus should first be on food production to bring down food inflation and reduce importation of those food items. There must be coordination among the three tiers of government, as State governments own the land while the federal government has the policy and funding. Coordination at the local government level is equally imperative.
