How costly tests, poor equipment delay patients’ treatment

By Punch Punch 3d ago 17 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Patients seeking care at public hospitals across the country face an impossible choice: pay exorbitant fees for diagnostic tests at private facilities or forgo essential investigations because of the lack of functional diagnostic equipment in public hospitals. For those unable to afford the costs, abandoning tests often means delayed diagnosis and treatment, with potentially devastating consequences. Experts are calling for dedicated government funding, functional equipment and subsidised tests to ensure timely diagnosis and treatment. JANET OGUNDEPO reports

With several bouts of persistent chest pain and constant discomfort, a retiree and mother of four, Mrs Comfort Uwana, decided to seek medical attention at a public hospital in Lagos State, hoping to get medication that would relieve her symptoms.

After listening to her complaints and reviewing her medical history, the doctor issued a referral requesting five diagnostic tests deemed necessary to establish an accurate diagnosis and determine the appropriate course of treatment.

Uwana said she was surprised that she had been asked to undergo several tests rather than being given a prescription for medication. Although the doctor explained why the investigations were necessary, her concern deepened when she enquired about the cost of the tests that were available at the facility.

“I was asked to do five tests; one of the tests at the facility costs N65,000, another costs N12,000, and one costs N32,000. The other ones were not available at the hospital, so I asked to go to a private facility, which was more expensive. Where do I get the money to do these tests?” she asked.

The retiree, who said she depended on her pension and the goodwill of her children, told PUNCH Healthwise that she left the laboratory and returned home, where she has been managing her pain with painkillers.

Uwana recalled another instance where a relative who was advised to undergo an abdominal computed tomography scan at a private facility and was asked to pay N175,000 returned home without undergoing the investigation because of the cost.

For Titilayo Aina, a student of Lagos State University, Ojo, the financial burden began even before she could establish what was wrong with her.

After paying N15,000 for a consultation with a dermatologist at the Lagos State University Teaching Hospital, Ikeja, Aina said she was given requests for about six investigations.

Having already spent money on the consultation, the third-year student began thinking about the additional costs she would incur before obtaining a diagnosis and receiving the appropriate treatment.

She told PUNCH Healthwise that she did not proceed to the hospital’s diagnostic centre to enquire about the prices of the tests but returned home without knowing how much they would cost or how she would pay for them.

As a student paying out of pocket for healthcare, Aina said the prospect of financing several investigations at once was a source of concern.

For a resident of Ibadan, Oyo State, identified only as Deborah, the financial strain of several medical tests has drained her so much, leaving her with little to begin the much-needed treatment.

The middle-aged lady told PUNCH Healthwise that an accident over 10 years ago and increasingly intense pain led her to the hospital, where she was diagnosed with osteoarthritis in 2025 after several tests.

Deborah said she was asked to undergo X-rays of her waist and knee, including other tests, and, having already spent considerable money on medical investigations, she has yet to raise the funds needed to begin the recommended physiotherapy.

The Ibadan resident, who works in a small firm and earns below the minimum wage, says while she has made enquiries about the cost of the treatment, she has been unable to afford treatment.

Deborah said she currently lives with the constant pain and manages it with painkillers while trying to find the money to proceed with physiotherapy.

 

Postponing tests due to cost

When an entrepreneur and resident of Alimosho, Lagos, Sabirat Murtala, was pregnant in 2023, she was required to undergo several investigations, such as blood and urine tests, HIV screening, blood group and genotype tests, as well as several ultrasound scans to monitor her baby’s growth and development.

Murtala, who received antenatal care at a private hospital, said the cost of investigations varied by test and scan.

While the mother of three said she could afford the cheaper tests, she admitted that she delayed doing some of the ultrasounds until she could raise the money.

During the periods when she delayed the tests, she could only hope nothing would go wrong with her baby.

While Aina, Deborah and Murtala, as well as Uwana and her relative, represent different experiences, their stories reflect the delays and financial difficulties many Nigerian patients face when accessing diagnostic services, especially those without health insurance or whose insurance plans do not cover the required investigations.

 

Why patients need medical investigations

An epidemiologist, Professor Adebayo Onajole, said medical investigations were central to effective treatment because they helped doctors identify diseases early and make decisions based on evidence rather than assumptions.

Onajole, a public health physician and Professor of Community Health and Primary Care at the University of Lagos and the Lagos University Teaching Hospital, said treating patients based only on symptoms could lead to wrong diagnoses because different diseases sometimes present with similar signs.

He cited fever as an example, noting that patients should not automatically be treated for malaria without first confirming the infection through testing.

“Today we do agree that you must not start treating for malaria unless you have done the test to confirm that the individual actually has malaria. Because so many other features might present you with fever,” he said.

The physician warned that treating patients without establishing the actual cause of their symptoms could expose them to inappropriate use of medicines, which could contribute to drug resistance, adverse drug reactions and additional expenses.

 

Private versus public diagnostic centres

To compare the prices of medical investigations in hospitals and private laboratories, PUNCH Healthwise visited some tertiary healthcare facilities and private diagnostic centres. The prices of an abdominal CT scan and a brain MRI at these facilities were obtained to achieve a direct comparison.

According to the Cleveland Clinic, MRI scans provide detailed images that can help identify conditions that may not be visible through other imaging methods, including soft tissue injuries, spinal cord abnormalities and brain conditions such as tumours or strokes. The scans can help doctors establish accurate diagnoses and determine appropriate treatment.

A CT scan, on the other hand, combines X-rays with computer processing to produce detailed images of structures inside the body.

Findings from a private laboratory in Lagos showed that a brain MRI scan cost N133,150, while a CT abdomen and pelvis scan cost N117,500. The facility also charged an additional N10,000 for patients who wanted a hard copy of the scan film.

At another private laboratory in Lagos, a CT abdomen scan cost N110,000, while a brain MRI was priced at N170,000.

PUNCH Healthwise gathered that the average private-sector prices of the CT abdomen and brain MRI scans were between N120,000 and N160,000, respectively.

A visit to the BT Health and Diagnostic Centre at LASUTH, Ikeja, revealed that the centre charged N110,000 for a brain MRI and N160,000 for an abdominal CT scan.

Patients were also required to pay an additional N3,000 for a creatinine test before the scans could be conducted.

Further findings from staff members at the facility showed that its diagnostic services operated under a public-private partnership arrangement with the hospital.

At the Lagos University Teaching Hospital, a source informed PUNCH Healthwise that an abdominal CT scan cost between N130,000 and N150,000, however, the cost of the brain MRI could not be obtained as the MRI machine at the hospital was not functional.

In 2025, PUNCH Healthwise reported that, among eight state and federal teaching hospitals in the South-West, only LUTH had a functional MRI machine. A year and several months later, however, the hospital’s machine was no longer operational.

Findings by PUNCH Healthwise also reveal that many tertiary hospitals across the country do not have specialised diagnostic equipment.

For example, it was gathered that there were only three MRI machines in Enugu State. Also, in selected federal and teaching hospitals across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones, PUNCH Healthwise found that many had either unreliable, non-functional, or no CT scanners and MRI machines.

A 2017 study by the Association of Radiographers of Nigeria paints a worrying picture of persistent disparities in the availability and functionality of these critical diagnostic tools.

According to the study, 68 per cent of the CT scanners in Nigeria were privately owned, leaving only 32 per cent spread across federal and state facilities or operated through public-private partnerships.

Further research published in 2018 showed that three states in Northern Nigeria had no CT scanners at all, and of the scanners installed nationwide, only 83 per cent were functional at the time.

Unarguably, the unavailability of this life-saving equipment leaves many patients paying more in private facilities, with some having to travel to other states to have the tests done.

The challenges also extend beyond the availability of equipment to the additional expenses patients incur when they have to repeat investigations or pay for tests that could not be carried out at their usual hospitals.

A report from the Nigerian Health Continuity Survey showed that at least two in five Nigerian patients had paid more than once for the same laboratory test or medical scan because their previous results could not be retrieved.

The survey also found that 73.4 per cent of respondents spent at least two hours from leaving home until the end of their hospital visits, while 17.6 per cent said the process took a full day or longer.

Financially, 41.8 per cent of respondents spent more than N50,000 out of pocket on their most recent healthcare journey, including transportation and medicines, while 25.3 per cent spent more than N100,000.

An analysis of the 2023/24 General Household Survey found that 45.5 per cent of Nigerian households faced catastrophic health expenditure at the 10 per cent budget-share threshold, with rural households, female-headed households and those with elderly members particularly affected.

The report also revealed that out-of-pocket payments accounted for about 72 per cent of Nigeria’s current health expenditure.

Need for medical investigations in pregnancy

A Professor of Obstetrics and Gynaecology at Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Ernest Orji, said routine investigations during pregnancy were important for identifying conditions that could put mothers and babies at risk.

Orji, an Honorary Consultant at the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex, identified urinalysis, blood tests and ultrasound scans as important investigations during antenatal care.

He said urinalysis could help detect abnormalities, including sugar or protein in the urine, which might point to underlying health problems requiring further investigation.

The gynaecologist also advised pregnant women to undergo ultrasound scans within the first three months of pregnancy to confirm and date the pregnancy, establish the baby’s position and monitor its development and the placenta.

He said scans were particularly important for women with underlying conditions such as diabetes and sickle cell disease, those with a history of delivering babies with abnormalities, and older pregnant women, who might have a higher risk of foetal abnormalities. Such women might require more detailed investigations to assess the development of the baby’s organs.

Orji identified the cost of investigations as a major reason some pregnant women failed to undergo recommended tests, noting that doctors could advise and appeal to patients but could not force them to pay for investigations.

He said government support could make pregnancy care and essential investigations more accessible to women who could not afford them, but warned that poor management of public funds could undermine such interventions.

Why diagnostic tests are expensive

The Lagos State Chairman of the Association of Radiologists of Nigeria, Dr Raymond Kuti, attributed the high cost of medical investigations to the rising prices of imported consumables, expensive diagnostic equipment, increasing operational costs and the fees paid to specialists.

Kuti said most consumables used in diagnostic medicine were imported and priced in foreign currency, making them vulnerable to exchange rate fluctuations.

He cited the cost of contrast medium, a substance used during some CT and MRI examinations, which he said had risen significantly over the years.

“Five years ago, one bottle of contrast medium was about N4,000. As of today, it’s about N15,000. So, it has increased to about 400 per cent. To buy a brand new CT machine now is N500m,” he said, explaining that borrowing such an amount at interest rates of between 32 and 35 per cent could make repayment almost double the original loan within three years.

The A.R.I.N Lagos chairman further identified electricity and diesel costs as major expenses involved in running diagnostic centres, saying facilities often had to rely on multiple sources of power, including the national grid, generators and solar energy.

The immediate past president of the Guild of Medical Directors argued that the cost of diagnostic investigations in government hospitals should be considered in the context of the financial support they received from the government.

He said public hospitals generally did not bear the same costs as private facilities in areas such as building rent, staff salaries, and equipment acquisition because the government provided these.

“If the government buys me N500 million CT, and they say I’m not using my money, of course, my price will come down,” he said.

He, however, argued that government hospitals did not always carry out the actual costing of diagnostic services, making it difficult to determine the true cost of providing investigations.

Kuti also explained that the growing use of public-private partnership arrangements for diagnostic services was partly because governments could no longer afford to purchase some of the expensive equipment.

He said an average 1.5 Tesla or 3.0 Tesla MRI machine could cost between N1bn and N1.4bn, making it difficult for many government hospitals to acquire the equipment without private-sector involvement.

Under the arrangement, he explained, a private investor provides the equipment in a government hospital and shares the revenue generated with the government.

He said private investors entered such arrangements to recover their investments within an agreed period, a consideration that could influence the charges for investigations.

Kuti also questioned the reimbursement rates proposed by the National Health Insurance Authority for some diagnostic investigations, arguing that the amounts were insufficient to cover the cost of providing the services.

He cited proposed payments of N50,000 for a CT brain scan, N62,000 for a CT chest scan and N65,000 for a CT abdomen scan, saying the rates were below the charges at diagnostic centres.

He maintained that the difference between charges under public-private partnership arrangements in government hospitals and those in private diagnostic centres could be between 20 and 30 per cent.

Beyond the cost of investigations, Kuti said diagnostic tests were important because they provided medical evidence to guide treatment, rather than leaving doctors to rely solely on patients’ symptoms.

“It’s better to measure ten times and cut once than to cut ten times and then measure once,” he said, using the expression to explain why doctors needed to establish what a patient was suffering from before commencing treatment.

Kuti said evidence-based medical practice was the current norm, adding that concerns about medico-legal consequences had also contributed to the increasing reliance on diagnostic tests.

On patients being asked to repeat investigations, he acknowledged that there were situations where another test might not be necessary.

He explained that doctors sometimes requested repeat investigations because they were not satisfied with the quality of images or reports obtained from a previous diagnostic centre. In other cases, repeat examinations were necessary for follow-up and monitoring of a patient’s condition.

He, however, advised doctors who had concerns about the quality of previous results to consider seeking another radiologist’s opinion before asking patients to pay for the same investigation again.

“What I usually encourage in that situation is to send it to another radiologist to evaluate, rather than tell the patient to go and repeat the test,” he said.

Need for tax waivers, dedicated diagnostic fund

The National First Vice-President of the Association of Medical Laboratory Scientists of Nigeria, Olusoji Billyrose, said the rising cost of diagnostic investigations was partly driven by heavy taxation, the high cost of imported medical supplies and equipment, and inadequate health insurance reimbursement rates.

Billyrose, who is also the Medical Director and Chief Executive Officer of Labcrest Laboratories Ltd, called on the government to consider removing or reducing taxes and import duties on medical equipment, reagents and other healthcare consumables to make diagnostic services more affordable.

He also identified inadequate reimbursement rates by Health Maintenance Organisations as a factor limiting patients’ access to laboratory investigations.

He said some HMO tariffs were far below the actual cost of conducting tests, making it difficult for laboratories to provide comprehensive investigations while maintaining quality standards.

“What you find is that most labs do not want to even work with the HMOs; they prefer people to pay out of pocket to them because the HMO tariff is not consistent and cannot even bear a quarter of the cost of doing that particular test,” he said.

He urged HMOs to involve medical laboratory scientists and laboratory directors in discussions on reimbursement rates to ensure that payments reflected the actual cost of providing quality investigations.

The laboratory scientist also proposed the establishment of a Bank of Health to provide low-interest loans to healthcare providers seeking to acquire diagnostic equipment and improve infrastructure.

He said the high cost of machines such as CT scanners and MRI equipment made it difficult for both private providers and government facilities to invest in diagnostic capacity without financial support.

Billyrose further called for a dedicated fund for medical diagnostics, describing diagnostic infrastructure as essential to the functioning of the health system.

He said strengthening laboratory accreditation and regulatory oversight was necessary to improve the quality and reliability of diagnostic services.

He urged hospitals to allow the Medical Laboratory Science Council of Nigeria to inspect their laboratories, saying such inspections would help assess their equipment, personnel and quality assurance systems.

Billyrose also advised members of the public to use laboratories licensed by the council and accredited to the ISO 15189 standard, noting that accreditation helped assure patients that laboratories operated according to recognised standards and produced reliable results.

“We should not just make the laboratories a ‘cash cow,’ a place where people must go to just because you want the money but rather to get good diagnostics,” he said.

Electronic sharing of results can reduce repeat tests

The National President of the Healthcare Providers Association of Nigeria, Dr Austine Aipoh, proposed an interoperable electronic health records system that would allow test results from one laboratory or hospital to be accessed by healthcare providers elsewhere.

He also advised that patients should be given soft copies of their results to share when they seek care at other facilities.

“If we get to the stage where all facilities, including diagnostic centres and hospitals, have electronic health records, then there will be interoperable results,” he said.

He warned that patients who could not afford requested investigations might delay treatment and resort to self-medication, including moving from one pharmacy to another in search of relief.

Aipoh advocated wider health insurance coverage, arguing that a larger pool of contributors would make it easier to finance expensive investigations. He said the country’s insurance coverage remained below 10 per cent and called for greater government investment in healthcare financing.

He also called for the establishment of a dedicated fund for diagnostic services and a health bank through which hospitals and healthcare providers could obtain loans to acquire equipment.

“If, for example, somebody brings in equipment for an MRI, and he is paying little for the equipment, he will not charge so much,” he said.
