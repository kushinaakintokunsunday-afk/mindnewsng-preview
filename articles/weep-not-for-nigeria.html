Weep not for Nigeria

“A child that is not well trained will sell the house that is well built.” – Yoruba proverb

Some months ago, I was travelling late in the evening with a very senior colleague along the Lagos-Ibadan Expressway, Mowe outbound.

My boss, as I fondly called him, a deep lover of Nigeria as most of us, were, was quick to notify me of the recent developments on the Long Bridge (the bridge along Berger (Lagos) and Warewa/Arepo, Ogun State): an array of glistering solar-powered streetlights that brighten the entire stretch of the bridge.

People conversant with this route will understand that this new facility is just not an afterthought embellishment; it’s a much-needed infrastructure for night travellers along the bridge due to the occasional thefts and robberies whenever there’s heavy traffic along the corridor.

The news was a signal of hope that good things still happen in my country, and I could remember discussing this good initiative for many minutes during the journey.

Just recently, seven months after this first encounter, I was privileged to travel again with the same boss along the same route, at approximately the same time.

With his index fingers slightly raised and in a discordant tone, he called my attention to the once-glittering streetlights that dazzled the entire bridge – almost 80 per cent of them had vanished; the stumps of the clean-cut pipes and the resurrected darkness on the bridge were there as witnesses.

It was like an eyesore. We all shook our heads in disbelief, and I must confess the grief was so heavy that our words were few, unlike other days.

Recently too, a friend shared a video of how some people were mining the iron rods that were used to reinforce a popular bridge in Lagos. The harvesting was so professionally executed that one needs to zoom in closer to see the unimaginable damage to the bridge.

Who is responsible for these evil acts? Your guess is as good as mine.

Albeit, because of the nature of their jobs, I have heard some people point fingers at bowlers and scavengers, but if you want to know how deplorable our country has descended, just drop any valuable iron around your house and monitor it; you will discover that our situation is beyond what you think.

But as we approached the middle of the bridge, still in bewilderment, I gently nudged my boss with some pertinent questions: “Sir, irrespective of whatever economic challenges or situation you may find yourself in, can you do this? Can I do it?”

It happens that one of the main reasons we could not do this is not because we are rich or poor, nor because of the level of education or status; it is not even just the fear of God; it’s based on the perceived advantages and privileges—how we feel the nation has fared with us.

Besides the inherent greediness and wickedness of men, there’s another foul side of us that can be triggered by the feeling of unfairness or being cheated.

That’s why it doesn’t matter how much you are being paid; you still want to know if you are being treated unfairly, and it doesn’t matter how much you score in an exam; you are likely to be comforted by the marks of others.

Our nation is currently on the cusp of inequality, with citizens who are a million miles away from their rulers. The gaps continue to widen every day, and the trust in the leadership or the nation itself has reached a palpable ebb. It is now a fight for self-survival, not a spirit of patrimony about our land; the cohesive spirit of a nation is in a silo.

Leaders seek positions to amass wealth and secure the future of their children, including the next generations and the unborn, not for the nation; citizens seek any opportunity to have their own bites of the national cake.

Everyone is making a demand of what the nation can offer, not what we can give, and any attempt to build anything meaningful without first building the people will end in futility, like the streetlights on the long bridge.

Why you should not weep for Nigeria!

 A nation that left the building of her people to frivolities is not a serious type—I – I have studied the projects and focus of our successive governments over 20 years; none of them has been able to address the teeming youth unemployment and underemployment that have plagued our nation.

It’s unprofitable to waste your tears on a nation where it is more profitable to become a politician than to work, and the citizens know this.

You should refrain from weeping because those who should really wail for the nation are wining, those that should be tearing are toasting, and those that should groan are gallivanting.

Solomon the wise said it is not wise to declare that the past is better than the present, but probably Nigeria’s case is a bit unique, for I have heard many elders telling me, while shaking their heads, that we, the current generation, do not really enjoy Nigeria; how do you say the best of a nation is in the past?

I believe in Nigeria and pray for her salvation, but please spare me my tears.

  • Charles is a pharmacist
