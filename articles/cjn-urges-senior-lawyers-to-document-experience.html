CJN urges senior lawyers to document experience

By Punch Punch 1d ago 8 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Chief Justice of Nigeria, Kudirat Kekere-Ekun, has urged senior lawyers to document their professional experiences to preserve valuable knowledge and guide

Kekere-Ekun spoke on Friday in Lagos while chairing the public presentation of six books authored by and in honour of Senior Advocate of Nigeria, Kemi Pinheiro, at the Civic Centre, Victoria Island.

The publications cover arbitration, insolvency, criminal litigation, legal practice and contemporary jurisprudence, with the CJN describing them as a significant contribution to legal scholarship because they combine doctrinal analysis with practical experience.

She said, “One of the most valuable legacies a senior member of the profession can leave behind is something from which the next generation can learn and grow.

“Experience that is not shared may remain a personal achievement. Experience that is documented and transmitted becomes part of the profession’s collective inheritance.”

The six publications comprise four books authored by Pinheiro – Defining Moments: Chronicles of a Silk; Strategies in Criminal Trial – A Practitioner’s Guide; Insolvency Law in Nigeria: Practice and Procedure; and Arbitration Law and Practice in Nigeria – and two tribute volumes, The Compendium in My Honour and Trending Issues of Law and Faith: Essays in Honour of Kemi Pinheiro.

Also present at the unveiling of the six books were the Chief of Staff to the President, Femi Gbajabiamila; a Justice of the Supreme Court, Habeeb Abiru; the Presiding Justice, Lagos Division of the Court of Appeal, Yargata Nimpar; and other Justices of the Court of Appeal. The Chief Judge of Lagos State, Kazeem Alogba; other judges of the Lagos State High Court; and the Attorney General and Commissioner of Justice, Lagos State, Lawal Pedro, SAN, were also in attendance.

Kekere-Ekun said the books addressed significant areas of legal practice, engaged with contemporary developments in jurisprudence and sought to make legal knowledge more accessible to practitioners and others involved in the administration of justice.

She particularly highlighted Defining Moments: Chronicles of a Silk, describing it as a personal account of the experiences, encounters, challenges and defining moments that had shaped Pinheiro’s career spanning close to four decades at the Bar.

According to her, memoirs of that nature preserve aspects of professional life that might never find their way into law reports or formal institutional records. They also help younger practitioners understand the uncertainties, disappointments and perseverance that often accompany successful legal careers.

On Arbitration Law and Practice in Nigeria, the CJN said the book examined the framework established by the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 and the developing jurisprudence of Nigerian courts.

She noted that its practical treatment of arbitration agreements, recognition and enforcement of awards made it a useful resource for practitioners involved in commercial dispute resolution.

She also commended Insolvency Law in Nigeria: Practice and Procedure, saying its examination of corporate rescue, restructuring and administration was particularly relevant under the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020.

The CJN said preserving viable businesses, where appropriate, could serve the interests of creditors, employees, investors and the wider economy better than immediate liquidation.

On Strategies in Criminal Trial – A Practitioner’s Guide, she said its coverage of arraignment, bail, confessional statements, electronic evidence, no-case submissions and written addresses would benefit experienced practitioners and younger lawyers.

She described its consideration of emerging issues, including electronic evidence in the age of artificial intelligence, as particularly timely.

The two tribute publications, she said, brought together contributions on issues including arbitration, taxation, maritime law, judicial precedent, technology in dispute resolution, evidence, criminal law and contemporary jurisprudence.

Addressing younger lawyers, Kekere-Ekun said, “I hope that younger lawyers will see these works not simply as additions to their libraries, but as encouragement to read more deeply, think more critically and take their own professional development seriously.”

She added that a book presentation was an invitation to dialogue, in which authors offered their thoughts and experiences for readers to engage with, question and learn from.

Congratulating Pinheiro, the CJN said the opportunity to translate professional experiences into literature that could educate and inspire others was a legacy of lasting value.

In his remarks, Pinheiro said the four books he authored were drawn from nearly four decades of legal practice and were intended to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge acquired in law school and the realities of legal practice.

“These books are the curated end products of the differences between the theory of the law schools and the reality of legal practice,” he said.

The lawyer, who will mark 40 years at the Bar in October 2027, said the publications contained lessons gathered from his work as an insolvency practitioner, criminal prosecutor and arbitrator.

Reflecting on his professional journey, he recalled that his elevation to the rank of SAN in 2006 came after an unsuccessful attempt the previous year, an experience that informed some of the reflections in Defining Moments.

He also recounted how a distant uncle, Rasaq Shonibare, helped set him on the path to becoming a lawyer by giving him money to purchase his Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board form, which enabled him to secure admission to study Law at the University of Benin.

Pinheiro said he later represented the same uncle pro bono in a dispute in which his assets were at risk of being taken over by a bank.

“At the time he gave me the Naira notes on Bamgbose Street, Lagos Island, neither of us knew that the money he gave me was the professional fee for the legal services I would be rendering for him later in life,” he said.

He also recalled the financial difficulties he faced during his early years in practice, including his inability to afford a wig and gown.

He said a senior lawyer once came to his rescue after the person who rented him his professional attire attempted to retrieve it while court proceedings were still ongoing. The senior lawyer lent him his gown and waited outside the courtroom until he completed his appearance.

Pinheiro said the experience and other acts of kindness inspired him to establish a free wig and gown project for indigent graduates of the Nigerian Law School, through which more than 800 young lawyers had benefited.

He recalled one young lawyer who travelled from Lafia, Nasarawa State, to Lagos in a truck conveying tomatoes because he could not afford transportation. After arriving, the young man trekked from Oyingbo to Pinheiro’s law firm in Ilupeju to collect a free wig and gown.

On his criminal trial book, Pinheiro said it was motivated by the consequences of errors in criminal proceedings, which could result in the loss of life or liberty.

“If there is one area of law where a counsel cannot afford to make a mistake, it is Criminal Law,” he said.

He said the publication examined procedural, evidential and strategic issues in criminal trials, including the admissibility of electronic evidence.

On insolvency law, he said his experience in the field informed the need to produce practical reference material for lawyers and other stakeholders dealing with financially distressed businesses.

He acknowledged the Managing Director of the Asset Management Corporation of Nigeria, Gbenga Alade, who wrote the foreword to the book.

Pinheiro said his arbitration book focused on the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 and addressed issues including anti-suit injunctions, post-award litigation and appeals, drawing on comparative experiences from leading arbitration jurisdictions.

He also commended Kekere-Ekun for reforms in the judiciary, citing improvements in court infrastructure, digital access to court information and efforts to address delays in the administration of justice.

Recalling an appeal that had been pending for 13 years, Pinheiro said a younger lawyer recently informed him that the Supreme Court had contacted him to schedule the matter for hearing.

He praised the CJN for making the Supreme Court’s diary accessible online, describing her as a “silent revolutionary”.

“You abhor accolades. You are a silent revolutionary. History and posterity will certainly be kind to you, My Noble Lord,” he said.

Pinheiro also acknowledged his mentors, particularly the late Abiola Oshodi and his uncle, Toyin Pinheiro, SAN, whom he credited with shaping his career.

Describing legal practice as a relay race, he said senior practitioners had a responsibility to pass their knowledge and experience to younger lawyers.

“Law practice is a continuum. The legal profession is not a sprint of one hundred metres. It is a relay race where each runner collects the baton and passes it on to the succeeding runner. Our primary task is therefore not to drop the baton,” he said.

He also paid tribute to his wife, Yetunde Pinheiro, for standing by him after his unsuccessful SAN application in 2005.

He recalled that she comforted him as they returned to Lagos from the Supreme Court and assured him that he would eventually attain the rank, which he achieved the following year.

“She wiped my tears as we came back to Lagos alone, with the rank of SAN disappearing into the distance. She assured me prophetically that I would still wear the Silk. She believed in me,” he said.

He added, “If there is one single decision I have never regretted making, it is the choice of Yetunde as my spouse and life partner.”

Reviewing Strategies in Criminal Trial, the President of the Nigerian Bar Association, Oyinkansola Badejo-Okunsanya, SAN, said the book would help lawyers improve their courtroom skills and understand the reasoning behind effective advocacy.

She said the publication encouraged practitioners to reflect on their decisions during court proceedings, particularly in cross-examination and the handling of witnesses.

Badejo-Okunsanya said lawyers were often willing to recount what happened in court but did not always explain why they made particular decisions.
