PINL’s host community strategy and the future of oil pipeline security

By Vanguard Vanguard 16 Nov 2026 7 min read

By Tayo Olu

Protecting oil pipelines in the Niger Delta requires more than the surveillance of facilities and the deployment of security personnel. The pipelines run through communities whose relationships with operators, access to economic opportunities and grievances over local development can influence the security of the infrastructure.

This relationship between community participation and pipeline security was the focus of a recent study by Dr. Oyeinfie Emmanuel Jonjon, a sociologist at Ignatius Ajuru University of Education, Port Harcourt, who examined Pipeline Infrastructure Nigeria Limited (PINL)’s approach to pipeline surveillance along the Trans Niger Pipeline (TNP).

Jonjon, a lecturer and researcher in the Department of Sociology, Faculty of Social Sciences, at Ignatius Ajuru University of Education in Rumuolumeni, Port Harcourt, Rivers State, has developed what he calls the ‘Weaver Colony Theory of Communal Living,’ using PINL’s community-based surveillance model to examine how host communities, private security contractors and statutory agencies can work within a common framework.

His paper, published in August 2026 in the American Journal of Social and Humanitarian Research (Volume 7, Issue 8, pages 126 to 145), is titled “Community-Based Surveillance of Oil and Gas Pipeline Infrastructure in the Niger Delta: A Theoretical Analysis of Pipeline Infrastructure Nigeria Limited Through Structural Functionalism and Weaver Colony Theory.”

The study examines how host communities, surveillance firms and security agencies can share responsibilities in protecting oil pipelines while maintaining clearly defined roles.

Jonjon believes that the cooperation of host communities is as important as the security measures put in place to protect oil infrastructure, and oil companies must build lasting relationships with host communities if they want to protect their facilities.

Pipeline theft, illegal bunkering, vandalism and production disruptions have remained persistent problems in the Niger Delta. Jonjon argued that addressing them mainly through security operations overlooks the influence of host communities and their relationships with oil operators.

He argued that operators often turn to media campaigns or spending on host communities after security incidents, while their corporate social responsibility programmes have done little to resolve longstanding grievances.

The TNP, which passes through communities in Rivers, Imo and Abia states, provides the setting for his examination of PINL’s operations. PINL, which was incorporated in 2019 to provide engineering, construction and security services to the country’s oil and gas sector, uses community-based surveillance along the pipeline corridor.

Jonjon’s argument is based on Structural Functionalism, a sociological theory developed by thinkers such as Émile Durkheim, Talcott Parsons and Robert K. Merton. The theory explains how different parts of society have roles that contribute to the stability of the whole.

However, he argued that the theory does not fully account for power imbalances, historical grievances or disagreements over who has the right to speak for a community. It also offers little guidance on how private operators should involve residents in their day-to-day activities.

He used the Weaver Colony Theory to explain how different groups can work together while maintaining their individual roles. He described the idea as “Many Nests, One Tree,” which was borrowed from weaver-bird colonies.

The tree represents the shared physical, economic, social and institutional environment, while the nests represent the different groups operating within it. Each group has its own responsibilities but must work together for the system to function.

In the framework, host communities contribute local knowledge, continuous presence and vigilance. A surveillance firm like PINL provides professional monitoring, operational coordination and structured engagement. Statutory security agencies and regulators retain their legal authority, enforcement responsibilities and oversight functions under the Petroleum Industry Act.

The model encourages community participation in pipeline protection without giving residents or surveillance contractors such as PINL powers reserved for statutory security agencies. Residents help identify and report threats, while authorised agencies remain responsible for enforcement.

The theory also links pipeline security to the economic relationship between operators and host communities. It identifies employment, scholarships and community investment as benefits that should go hand in hand with residents’ responsibilities to share information, remain vigilant and facilitate access to pipeline facilities.

The framework is built around interdependence, differentiated roles, visible contribution, resilience and the enforcement of norms. It also stresses that communication, fair representation, reciprocity and accountability are necessary to sustain the arrangement.

It cited PINL’s recruitment and engagement of more than 35,000 youths across the Niger Delta to protect installations and pipeline corridors, the clearance of longstanding scholarship backlogs owed to host communities, as well as women’s empowerment and vocational training programmes in communities along the Trans-Niger Pipeline in the three states.

The study also mentioned stakeholder meetings PINL held in April 2025 and June 2026, attended by traditional rulers, Community Development Committees, youth leaders, women’s representatives, company officials and statutory agencies.

The activities provided examples of how PINL engages host communities in pipeline surveillance. The study examined them against its proposed framework, assessing how community participation and representation could help prevent pipeline theft, vandalism and production disruptions.

The study also outlined a nine-stage process known as the Weaver Operational Cycle, covering identification, assignment, engagement, listening, verification, action, reporting, review and adaptation.

Under the cycle, information received from communities must be professionally verified before action is taken. Operators are also expected to give feedback to residents who provide information and review their procedures to determine what worked and whether any groups were excluded.

The model reserves decisions and enforcement actions for competent management, regulators or statutory security agencies. Here, communities contribute information and local vigilance but do not assume the responsibilities of those authorised to enforce the law.

The June 2026 attack on the TNP at Odau in Rivers State showed how important PINL’s relationship with host communities can be during security incidents. Following the attack, the company held meetings with community leaders, youth representatives and statutory security agencies.

The discussions focused on improving communication, sharing information and coordinating recovery efforts.

The incident reinforced the need for regular communication between PINL and host communities, particularly when pipeline infrastructure comes under attack.

PINL’s relationship with host communities has also featured in the debate over pipeline surveillance contracts. In 2026, petitions to the National Assembly called for the contracts to be distributed among oil-producing communities, while other regional leaders and parliamentary panels backed continuity and central coordination.

On October 13, 2026, the National Assembly passed a vote of confidence in PINL, citing the company’s operational gains, community empowerment programmes and the absence of evidence to support allegations against it.

The debate touched on community representation in pipeline surveillance contracts and how the economic benefits are distributed among oil-producing communities.

Lawmakers commended PINL for engaging more than 35,600 local youths as pipeline surveillance guards, noting the jobs created and the contribution to stability in the Niger Delta.

Also acknowledged were the company’s interventions in host communities, including more than 5,000 undergraduate and postgraduate scholarships, skills training for 4,000 women and flood relief efforts across Rivers State.

The paper warned that community engagement must be properly organised to ensure that its benefits reach different groups. It said that, without such arrangements, benefits could become concentrated among a small group of local elites and leave other sections of host communities without an effective voice.

It also compared PINL’s approach with that of Tantita Security Services Nigeria Limited, noting that the same model could apply to both companies. But it stressed that having locals involved in pipeline security is not enough. Communities must have clearly defined roles, see the benefits of their involvement and have a way to hold those responsible accountable.

The paper further situated the model within the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 and the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission’s framework for Host Community Development Trusts. It argued that community participation in pipeline surveillance must operate within the law and support, rather than replace, the work of security agencies.

The paper recommends that pipeline surveillance firms make community engagement a regular part of their operations rather than treat it as a corporate social responsibility project.

It also calls for formal agreements to sustain cooperation even when community leadership changes or security challenges arise.

*Olu wrote in from Abuja.
