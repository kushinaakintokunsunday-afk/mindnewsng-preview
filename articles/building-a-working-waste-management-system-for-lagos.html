Building a working waste management system for Lagos

By Punch Punch 5d ago 5 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Lagos generates thousands of tonnes of waste daily. Managing it takes collection vehicles, roads and facilities, adequate funding, capable operators, oversight and public responsibility.

To picture the scale, at the upper estimate of 17,000 tonnes a day, Lagos’s waste weighs as much as 340,000 bags of 50kg rice. If each bag were laid end to end, they would stretch roughly 306 kilometres, about 26 times the 11.8-kilometre Third Mainland Bridge.

Waste management can also create businesses, jobs and environmental benefits. Since 1977, the Lagos Waste Management Authority has evolved with the city. Its efforts to improve collection, involve private waste companies, upgrade infrastructure and increase recycling provide a foundation to build on. The task now is to make the system more coordinated, financially sustainable and accountable, and this can be done on five pillars:

Plan around the waste Lagos actually produces: Household, market, industrial, construction and healthcare waste need different collection and treatment. Decisions should reflect how much of each type there is, what it contains and where it comes from.

A World Bank study found significant amounts of plastic in waste at Olusosun. But dumpsite waste may not represent all waste generated: informal collectors recover some materials, and some waste bypasses the formal system.

Regular waste studies across communities, markets and industrial areas should guide investment in collection, transport, recycling, food-waste processing, waste-to-energy and safe disposal. Each type needs a workable solution. Better information can show where useful materials are lost and services are missing.

Waste becomes a useful resource only when its quality, quantity, collection and buyers are understood. Without this evidence, facilities may be costly and underused.

Strengthen local accountability and central coordination: Waste services are local. Residents see missed collection in their streets and markets. Lagos State Waste Management Authority should set standards for the whole state and clarify local responsibility.

Each service area should have a named manager, clear standards and an easy way to report problems. Boundaries should reflect population, roads, waste volumes, company capacity and access to treatment or disposal sites.

Residents should know collection days, which company serves them and where to report missed service.

Private waste collection companies, often called PSP operators, remain essential. Their contracts need clear duties, fair incentives and transparent checks, with payment terms that support reliable service. LAWMA should coordinate infrastructure and enforce standards across Lagos. Reviews should assess both company performance and whether routes, equipment and payment terms allow reliable service.

Turn waste into useful products and jobs: Reliable collection can turn plastics, food waste, metals and paper into useful products, businesses and jobs. Organic waste is especially relevant in Lagos, with its food markets, hospitality businesses and manufacturers.

My work in organic waste recovery has shown how rubbish can become a useful resource. But a facility needs a steady supply of suitable material, reliable transport, skilled staff, operating capital and buyers. Poorly sorted or irregular supplies lower quality and raise costs, so collection must meet the needs of processors and customers.

The government can support this through clear rules, facilities and incentives; businesses can bring investment, expertise and customers. Bringing informal collectors and sorters into safer formal work can improve conditions, recycling and livelihoods.

Waste can also produce energy where the material, safeguards and costs make sense. No technology solves every waste problem. A circular economy keeps useful materials in use instead of dumping them.

Even with more recycling, some waste will still need safe disposal.

Fund the whole waste system: Building facilities is not enough. Collection vehicles need fuel and maintenance. Transfer stations, where waste is gathered before being moved, need reliable transport. Processing plants need money to operate and buyers for their products. Disposal sites need environmental controls and long-term management.

Lagos needs a plan to pay for collection, transport, treatment and disposal. Funding could combine public budgets, clear waste service charges, manufacturers helping pay to collect and recycle their packaging, private investment, sales of recovered materials and development finance. A credible plan should show who pays for each stage and how the money is tracked.

Charges should be clear and tied to reliable service, with support to protect lower-income communities. Investors need predictable rules, credible contracts, reliable information and confidence they will be paid as agreed.

Judge investments by whether facilities operate reliably and affordably over time, not simply by whether they are built.

Make performance visible and involve residents: Lagos should publish measures showing whether waste is collected on time, how much is collected and safely treated, how much is recycled, operating costs, illegal dumping and complaint resolution. Reports should distinguish proposed projects from facilities that are operating. Reliable, independently checked data would help residents judge progress and guide spending.

Residents, businesses and markets also have responsibilities. Sorting fails if waste is mixed together again during collection, and enforcement needs convenient disposal options. Schools, markets and community groups can promote good habits. Clear schedules, simple sorting instructions and incentives can help; enforcement should support reliable service, not replace it.

 Shared accountability means residents dispose of waste properly and government delivers reliable services.

 Lagos has delivered major infrastructure programmes; waste management deserves the same long-term commitment.

 The next phase should connect government, waste companies, investors, communities and businesses through clear policy, sound financing and measurable results.

 Lagos can move from simply moving rubbish to reliable collection, recycling, useful products and safe disposal, supporting health, jobs, business and a more resilient city.

 Success means waste is collected when promised, useful materials are recovered where possible, and the rest is handled safely. That is the foundation of a waste system fit for Lagos’s future.

 Olumide Thompson, an entrepreneur with a speciality in waste recovery and resource utilisation, writes via olumide.thompson@gmail.com
