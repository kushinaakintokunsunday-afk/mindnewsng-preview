Fountain: Okonkwo preaches unity, Jimmy insists court order not served

By Punch Punch 2d ago 13 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Fountain of Life Church, Ilupeju, Lagos, on Sunday received an intervention from one of Nigeria’s prominent Pentecostal leaders, Bishop Mike Okonkwo, who, a

Okonkwo, founder of The Redeemed Evangelical Mission, said he had been prompted by the Holy Spirit to visit the church and bless its congregation after his telephone was inundated with calls over the preceding week, with people demanding to know why he had remained silent as the dispute between Pastor Jimmy Odukoya and his sister, Tolu, threatened the church’s unity.

But Jimmy, who also spoke during the service, addressed the interim court order restraining him from presenting himself as the church’s Senior Pastor and Spiritual Head, saying he had heard about the injunction but had not been formally served.

Okonkwo, in his sermon, said, “I have not slept since 1.30 am.

The Holy Spirit said to me, son, go to the Fountain and bless them. That’s why I am here,” he told the congregation.

His appearance and sermon, titled The Glory of the Church, were an attempt to redirect attention from the personalities at the centre of the dispute to the institution they inherited from their late parents, Pastor Taiwo and Pastor Bimbo Odukoya.

But beneath his generally conciliatory message were pointed admonitions that appeared to address the warring parties, the congregation and a wider public that has followed the controversy, cautioning against turning a leadership disagreement into a battle capable of destroying the church.

Opening with an assurance that “all is well”, Okonkwo recalled the traumatic period following Taiwo’s death, when he had addressed the congregation and urged members to preserve the legacy of the deceased founder.

“I remember when Pastor Taiwo passed, it was a traumatic time and experience. We cried, we wailed, we blew our noses and dried our tears, and the responsibility fell on me to come here to talk to us,” he said.

He said he had reminded the congregation at the time that the greatest honour it could pay the late pastor was to ensure that his life’s work continued.

“And I know one of the things I said that day is, please, the only honour we can do for the sacrifice, the price, the pains and everything… that the only thing is that the legacy must continue. And I told you that day that Fountain will never die.”

The bishop said he remained personally invested in the church and could not be indifferent to developments there.

“For the past one or two weeks, we have been literally battered on social media. There’s nothing that has not been said, both the ones that are true and the ones that are not true. Phone calls from far and near: ‘Bishop Mike, where are you?’” he said.

“You can’t separate me from Fountain because it is a covenant that God himself created by himself, so what happens to Fountain affects me. So don’t think that I am just aloof.”

However, the central thrust of his message was that the church was bigger than the individuals currently fighting over its leadership.

Drawing extensively from biblical passages on redemption, the value of the church and the sacrifices behind its establishment, Okonkwo argued that the institution could not be reduced to a human organisation whose survival depended on the fortunes of particular individuals.

“The church is not a human institution. It is God’s house. It belongs to him. Think of the price that God had to pay for his church,” he said.

He subsequently made one of the most direct references to the leadership dispute, telling members that their allegiance should not be to either of the Odukoya siblings or the church’s governing board.

“Every one of us, from myself downwards, should know: you are not for Tolu or for Jimmy or for the board. None of them died for you. Church is not a political party. The blood. Life was involved. The church is not an ordinary thing. God could not redeem the church with anything other than his life,” he said.

“We must be careful so that none of us will be an instrument of destroying the church of Christ.”

The appeal appeared directed not only at the two sides in the dispute but also at members whose public declarations of support, social media exchanges and competing narratives have increasingly made the crisis a subject of wider controversy.

Okonkwo’s emphasis on the sacrifices of the founders also appeared intended to remind the contending parties of the responsibility attached to the institution they inherited. He insisted that the present difficulties were not evidence that God had abandoned the church, arguing that its challenges had already been anticipated in God’s plan.

Using Jesus Christ’s illustration in Luke 14:28 of a builder who must first calculate the cost of constructing a tower, Okonkwo questioned why members would allow the present crisis to unsettle their faith in the church’s future.

“Question, Fountaineers, are you saying that Jesus did not factor in the challenges you will go through? He is saying that if I want to build a house, I will first of all be sure that I have all that is necessary, and he is the builder of the house, we are the building. Are you saying that all the crises, all the gossips, all that is happening to you individually is taking you by surprise?” he asked.

“He has already factored all those in even before you were called. He has already taken care of business. That is why I know without shadow of doubt whatever the devil does, he is already a failure. The believer never ever ends in shame.”

But while assuring the congregation that the church would survive, the bishop also warned against reactions that could aggravate its problems.

“Do you know the challenges of the believers? When trials come, temptations come, difficulties come, we fall apart, we forget what he told us, we begin to run helter-skelter, and many times we do the things we are not supposed to do and further arm the devil,” he said.

“If there was a time Fountaineers needed to chill, it is this time. Let God perfect what he has started.”

He declared that the church would eventually surpass the achievements of its late founder. “This church is going to do seven times more than Taiwo left it,” he said.

“The game is not over. You are still in the will of God. You are still in his hands. God has not left this church. And can I tell you, he will never leave this church; the glory of God remains in this house.”

He ended with a call for collective responsibility, assuring members that the church would not be destroyed by the present controversy.

“Be assured. No matter what anyone says, that is not your business. We will build this church. Together we will build this church.”

Jimmy’s personal battle

But the atmosphere that followed Okonkwo’s sermon suggested that the bishop’s appeal for a collective response to the crisis might not have entirely displaced the personal dimension of the dispute.

Jimmy, who addressed the congregation after Okonkwo, appeared to frame the unfolding controversy more directly around his own predicament, a departure from the senior bishop’s sustained emphasis on the survival of the institution beyond its current leadership.

He mounted the pulpit and sang the popular hymn It Is Well with My Soul before moving to gospel musician Dunsin Oyekan’s My Worship Is My Weapon, whose refrain declares worship as a means of winning personal battles.

The choice of songs, coming immediately after Okonkwo had urged members not to identify with either of the contending parties, shifted the emotional emphasis towards a personal struggle and appeared to reinforce the perception among his supporters that he was fighting an unjust campaign against him.

Jimmy was the only party to the leadership dispute who addressed the congregation during the service.

He subsequently addressed the interim court order restraining him from presenting himself as the church’s Senior Pastor and Spiritual Head, saying he had heard about the injunction but had not been formally served.

“I heard that there was an injunction against me. I heard. I’ve not seen it. But I heard. But I’ve not been served. So, legally, I’ve not been served,” he said.

He also thanked members for remaining at the church despite the controversy and assured them of his continued commitment to their interests.

“In spite of everything, you are still here,” he said. “There is no Fountain without you. You are Fountain of Life. And as long as you are with me, I will continue to speak for you. I will continue to stand with you.”

While the remarks were likely to reassure his supporters, they offered little indication of an immediate willingness to de-escalate the dispute or pursue reconciliation with the opposing side.

Towards the end of the service, as the choir continued singing, Jimmy walked up to the choir leader at least twice to whisper instructions. On one occasion, the choir subsequently sang the popular gospel song in Pidgin by Bidemi Olaoba, Who Dey Talk Wetin God No Talk, a primarily individualistic chorus that became the closing song.

The choice of song, with its message about disregarding declarations contrary to God’s will, added another layer to the service’s underlying contest of narratives.

Jimmy also appeared determined to maintain his position as the church’s leader despite the legal challenge, leaving the question of reconciliation unresolved.

The Odukoya legacy

The controversy has also revived questions about the personality, public conduct and religious identity of the Odukoya family, particularly as Jimmy’s unconventional appearance and style of ministry continue to attract criticism from sections of the Christian community.

Tattooed, dreadlocked, fond of swaggering dance routines and wearing earrings, Jimmy has attracted considerable attention for a public persona that departs from the conventional image of a Pentecostal pastor in Nigeria.

But the apple did not fall far from the tree.

His late mother and co-founder of The Fountain of Life Church, Bimbo Odukoya, also faced considerable criticism during her lifetime for challenging the conservative social conventions and strict codes of appearance that characterised much of Nigeria’s expanding Pentecostal movement in the 1990s and early 2000s.

At the time, many Pentecostal churches maintained restrictive expectations around dressing, hairstyles, jewellery and other forms of personal adornment, reflecting a broader doctrine of separation from worldly influences.

Pastors’ wives were generally expected to dress in long skirts and modest clothing, often complemented by church hats and restrained accessories.

Bimbo, however, cultivated a considerably different public image. Her fashionable outfits, shorter skirts, wigs and jewellery attracted criticism from conservative Christians who considered her appearance inconsistent with the standards expected of a pastor’s wife.

Yet, her appearance was accompanied by a formidable preaching ministry. Her sermons, which frequently focused on purpose, relationships, leadership and personal development, established her as one of the more recognisable female Christian voices of her generation.

She was also known for her forceful, scripture-based responses to criticism, challenging interpretations of biblical doctrines that she considered unnecessarily restrictive.

Her ability to combine an unconventional public image with an intellectually engaging preaching style helped distinguish her from many of her contemporaries.

Jimmy’s public persona, however, has generated a different kind of debate. While his appearance and contemporary style have endeared him to a section of younger Christians, questions have persisted over whether his public responses and conduct consistently reflect the temperament, depth and intellectual maturity expected of someone leading a large congregation.

The distinction is particularly evident in the way both generations have approached public criticism.

During Sunday’s service, Okonkwo used a football analogy, drawing on the nature of a Manchester United match, to explain that conceding a goal did not mean the referee would immediately end the game. The match would continue until the allotted time elapsed before the final result was determined.

The analogy was a measured attempt to contextualise the reputational damage the church might have suffered, reminding members that the present controversy was not the end of its institutional story.

It contrasted with Jimmy’s own much-discussed social media engagement with Manchester United, in which he adapted a popular Christian song, invoked the name of Christ while praying for the club’s victory, and danced before the camera.

The performance attracted criticism from social media users who considered it unbecoming of a pastor of his stature.

The criticism raises a broader question about the boundaries between contemporary Christian expression, personal branding, and the public responsibilities attached to pastoral leadership. While younger audiences may appreciate Jimmy’s informality and entertainment-oriented presentation, the same approach can be received differently by members of a religious institution whose identity was shaped by more conventional standards of pastoral conduct.

Indeed, Nigerian Pentecostal churches are increasingly confronting the challenge of negotiating these competing expectations.

The country’s large youth population is growing up in an environment shaped by the internet, international entertainment, social media and cultural influences that increasingly challenge traditional assumptions about dressing, music, personal expression and religious identity.

Tattoos, dreadlocks, earrings and contemporary dance styles, once widely regarded with suspicion in conservative Christian circles, have become increasingly common forms of expression among younger generations.

Some Pentecostal leaders have argued that these practices are not inherently sinful and should not be confused with a person’s spiritual disposition.

Even traditionally conservative denominations, including the Deeper Christian Life Ministry, have over time deemphasised or relaxed some of their earlier positions on aspects of dressing, hair covering, jewellery and contemporary music.

The challenge for churches is how to accommodate a changing generation without losing the doctrinal identity and institutional traditions that defined their establishment.

For The Fountain of Life Church, this generational transition is history re-unfolding alongside a particularly sensitive leadership dispute involving the children of its founders.

The immediate legal battle remains unresolved. The Lagos State High Court, presided over by Justice O.O. Adewunmi-Oshin, issued an interim order on Wednesday, November 18, restraining Jimmy from presenting himself as the church’s Senior Pastor and Spiritual Head.

The order followed an application by the Incorporated Trustees of The Fountain of Life Church. The court also restrained him, either personally or through proxies, from interfering with or disrupting the church’s activities, governance and other business.

The matter was adjourned until December 1 for mention.

Sunday’s service offered little indication that the dispute was nearing a resolution. While Okonkwo sought to redirect the congregation’s attention to the church’s founding vision and collective future, Jimmy’s address placed his own position and relationship with members at the centre of the unfolding controversy.

For a church founded on the ministry and public identities of Taiwo and Bimbo Odukoya, the crisis is now testing not only the succession arrangements but also the meaning of the legacy they left behind.

Okonkwo’s message was that the church must survive its present troubles and become greater than it was under its founders. Whether those now contesting its leadership can subordinate their differences to that vision remains the more immediate test.
