Singing old national anthem not a crime – APC chieftain

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

A chieftain of the All Progressives Congress, Dr Josef Onoh, has said that neither the 1999 Constitution nor the National Anthem Act 2024 criminalises the singi

Onoh stated this during an interview on Arise News on Monday, describing the controversy surrounding Nigeria Democratic Congress presidential candidate Peter Obi and members of the party for singing the old anthem at a party gathering as a trivial matter.

He argued that although the National Anthem Act 2024 reinstated ‘Nigeria, We Hail Thee’ as the country’s official anthem, it did not prohibit citizens from singing the previous anthem or prescribe punishment for failing to recite the current one.

“Why I think it’s a trivial matter is, one, we have the old national anthem, which is the Arise, O Compatriots, and we have the new national anthem,” he said.

Onoh said both anthems represented Nigerians’ allegiance and historical connection to the country, adding that the 2024 legislation did not criminalise the use of the former anthem.

“And these two national anthems symbolise our allegiance, our deep-rooted and historical affiliation to our nation,” he added.

He explained that the National Anthem Act, signed by President Bola Tinubu in May 2024, did not contain any provision making it an offence to sing the former anthem.

“And when the National Anthem Act of 2024 was created, they did not in any way, even in the Constitution, criminalise any part of the old national anthem that says, ‘Ok, if you do not recite the old national anthem, if you ever recite the old national anthem, it becomes a crime’,” Onoh said.

The APC chieftain also questioned the argument that the NDC gathering was legally required to render the new anthem, citing Section 3 of the Act, which outlines occasions when its performance is mandatory.

According to him, the provision covers official government and legislative gatherings as well as political gatherings but does not specifically list private political town hall meetings as occasions requiring the rendition of the anthem.

“And you have to also understand that Section 3 of the Act mandates the performance of the new anthem in official government places and official legislative holdings and gatherings and political gatherings too,” he said.

He, however, pointed to what he described as a loophole in the provision, arguing that private political town hall meetings were not expressly included among the gatherings where the anthem must be performed.

“But one particular part of this section, it’s like a loophole in the whole essence, is that when you look at the whole section, it never in any way made it, listed private political town hall gathering as mandatory, as mandatory requiring you to cite the national anthem,” he added.

Onoh further cited Section 24(a) of the 1999 Constitution, which lists respect for the national anthem among the civic duties of citizens.

He argued that although the Constitution recognises respect for the anthem as a civic responsibility, it does not provide criminal punishment for failing to sing or recite it.

“Then when you look at Section 24A of the 1999 Constitution as amended, it listed respecting the national anthem, which basically we all know, as a civic duty,” he said.

“But neither the Constitution nor the National Anthem Act of 2024 criminalised the not reciting the national anthem,” he added.

The controversy followed a town hall meeting organised by the NDC in Lagos last week, which featured Obi and the party’s governorship candidate in the state, Funso Doherty.

During the event, the former anthem, ‘Arise, O Compatriots’, was rendered after the gathering did not join in singing ‘Nigeria, We Hail Thee’, prompting political reactions.

The incident attracted criticism from several supporters of the ruling party and government officials, including the Minister of the Federal Capital Territory, Nyesom Wike, and the Minister of Aviation and Aerospace Development, Festus Keyamo.

Wike accused Obi of disrespecting national symbols, while Keyamo described the incident as an indication of secessionist tendencies.

However, some NDC figures dismissed the criticism, arguing that the singing of the former anthem was a spontaneous reaction by attendees rather than a deliberate rejection of the official national anthem.

Nigeria adopted ‘Nigeria, We Hail Thee’ as its national anthem at independence in 1960 before replacing it with ‘Arise, O Compatriots’ in 1978.

Tinubu signed the National Anthem Bill into law on May 29, 2024, restoring the original anthem after 46 years.

The legislation followed its passage by the National Assembly and generated public debate, with critics questioning the decision amid the country’s economic challenges.
