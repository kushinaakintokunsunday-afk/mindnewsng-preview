As deadly super El Niño looms, Kwali Council battles deforestation, high Cooking Gas costs

By Oladosu Adebola Oluwaseun

​Addressing concerns over a University of Chicago prediction warning of extreme heat waves which could result in over 30,000 additional heat-related deaths annually an official from the Department of Environmental Services at the Kwali Area Council, Abuja, Obamila John, has outlined local efforts to combat deforestation and environmental degradation.

​Responding to questions on how the council is protecting residents from the impacts of climate change, John noted that the department relies heavily on its forestry unit to regulate tree felling and promote reforestation across local communities.

​”According to environmental regulations, if you cut down one tree, you are supposed to plant at least ten replacements,” he explained. “If this law is properly implemented, we can reduce extreme heat to a minimum and protect the ozone layer.”

​However, John acknowledged that enforcing anti-deforestation laws remains difficult due to the high cost of alternative cooking fuels.

With liquefied petroleum gas (cooking gas) costing around ₦1,800 per kilogram, most rural households cannot afford it and continue to rely on firewood and charcoal for daily cooking.

​”It is very hard to tell people to abandon firewood when they cannot afford gas,” he noted, adding that the economic situation in the country has even driven the sale of charcoal from rural communities into major cities.

​To bridge this gap, the department conducts community sensitization campaigns, educating villagers on the dangers of indiscriminate tree felling and its link to extreme heat and environmental damage.

​John also pointed to rapid urbanization and infrastructure development as major drivers of tree loss in the Federal Capital Territory (FCT). Recalling his time living in Kuchigoro about 20 years ago, he described how the stretch from the community toward Area 1 was once lined with dense, shady trees including mango trees where residents could walk, rest, and eat fruit safely without fear.

​”Today, those trees have been cleared to make way for roads, residential buildings, and commercial structures,” he said. “Even designated green areas where people used to relax have been taken over and developed. While development is necessary, it is costing us our tree cover.”

​Emphasizing that environmental protection requires a collective effort, John noted that the crisis extends beyond the FCT to northern and eastern Nigeria, urging both residents and authorities to prioritize tree planting and preserve remaining green spaces.
