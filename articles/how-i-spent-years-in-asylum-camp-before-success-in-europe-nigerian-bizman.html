How I spent years in asylum camp before success in Europe – Nigerian bizman

By Punch Punch 3d ago 9 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The founder and Group Chief Executive Officer of Jeffangs Global Trade and Investment, Jeffrey Ekhator, shares story of childhood, migration to The Netherlands, and business with BIODUN BUSARI

What were the highlights of your childhood?

I was born and raised in Uselu, Benin City, Edo State. I attended my primary and secondary schools in my village, Oluku. During those years, which were between the 80s and 90s, I was already into business with my mother. She was a commodity trader, trading in various goods, and I hawked for her. When I returned from school, I would open my kiosk to sell goods. With my business mindset, an opportunity came for me to work at a filling station, after I finished secondary school. I was helping the owner grow his business but I enjoyed it. As I got commendations from him, I was gaining confidence that I would become a very successful businessman in the future. I sat the university matriculation examination as I worked but I didn’t pass. I made a second attempt but to no avail. With that, I focused on my work and found fulfillment in doing it. My friends were already at the university. Then, from nowhere, a bigger opportunity came for me to leave Nigeria, and I grabbed it with both hands.

How did you feel leaving Nigeria at that time?

It felt great. Leaving the fuel business became an issue because my boss had entrusted everything into my care. He was dissuading me from leaving but my mind was made up. He was afraid the business would collapse. He disappeared for three weeks because he knew I wouldn’t abandon his business. His business was booming and he wanted to make certain arrangements for me. It was during the June 12 crisis when Gen Sani Abach was in power, so I had no reason to stay in the country. I was in my 20s. That was how I travelled to France in search of greener pastures.

What was the first thing you pursued abroad?

I spent some months in France before proceeding to the Netherlands. I located Nigerians to help in one way or the other. Most people travelled to the United Kingdom, Germany and only a few would go to the Netherlands at that time, so I was trying to navigate. I was integrating myself into Dutch society. I was meeting people and asking questions. Different people would come with different stories both positive and negative. But the decision was mine to take at the end of the day. Some advised me to go to clubs regularly and find a Dutch woman to marry. But, the process was even more difficult because I would have to come to Nigeria to wed and return to the Netherlands. At the end of the day, I applied for a political asylum because it was impossible to go to school without being documented. I was in the camp for years before they granted me asylum to stay in the Netherlands.

What was your next step after that?

I went to school. Going to school gives you the understanding of how the country works. So, I started from the beginning. I started the integration school, then I went to college. After that, I went to business school for two years. I only got certificates from that school, because I could not get a diploma. The reason for that was because I couldn’t find a place to do my internship in that sector. It was a racist approach.

What did you study?

It was a car business school. There, you learn how to couple cars in the factory and sell them. I looked for an internship in that sector, but the people in the sector were mainly white people at that time. After graduation, you can obtain a dealership certificate and start selling new cars, but most of those chances were given to the Europeans. When that didn’t work out for me, I advanced my education to the university. In my first year, I was putting myself together for a business. I worked at McDonald’s in the evening to save money. I told my wife, who was my girlfriend then, to manage the business. So, I faced school and worked in the evening. We opened an African shop and filled it with goods. I got documents for the approvals, insurance and did all necessary things. That was in 2004. Every arrangement concluded on a Wednesday evening. We scheduled Friday for the opening ceremony.

How did the ceremony go?

My African brothers were already happy for me. A lot of people anticipated it because they said nobody had ever ventured into that kind of business. So, I was coming on Thursday morning for the finishing touches and I could not find my shop. A guy who was driving me and I just saw that the street was barricaded. We rushed out of the car. A Dutch woman came to meet me and said they were trying to reach me in the night. She said my shop was burnt to the ground. I couldn’t believe my ears. I was shocked. They said the shop caught fire at night. The emergency workers, firefighters and the police were trying to gather the wreckage. I was asking how it happened. I started asking questions but they told me an investigation had started. In the end, I was told they suspected a guy who did it but there was not enough evidence like footage, fingerprints to arrest him. That’s why I said corruption is everywhere, but it might not be as pronounced in Nigeria. I am a black and they are white. All the investments were gone. The business never saw the light of the day. Later, people told me that nobody had brought that kind of business to Vlaardingen because it was a Dutch-dominated town. So, it was another racist approach that I faced.

What did you do to pick yourself up?

All the companies that I took goods from on credit knew about it. I explained to them.

 I never started the business before it finished.

I lost everything. So, I owed the distributors of Coca-Cola and Heineken companies. I tried to fight it through the insurance company, but because my insurance was new, they said they could not do anything. I took a lawyer, but I couldn’t get any meaningful headway. That was how I faced my evening work at McDonald’s. I started from scratch, gathering money again. I was not sleeping at home. So, I was walking on the street in Rotterdam one day. It was raining very hard and I ran to a shop to stay. The owner of the shop was an elderly Moroccan man. We talked and I told him about what happened to me. So, we went into business. That kick-started my rise again.

What did you discuss?

He was emotional about my story. He told me that Vlaardingen was a racist area. He told me he wanted to retire and go back to his country and he would give me his shop. I looked around the shop but it wasn’t what I needed because he sold Arab products. So, I went home and I discussed it with my wife. My wife said we couldn’t have another business because we just lost one. Later, we thought about it and went to the old man. He told me he wanted to sell that shop for €25,000. I didn’t have the money but I told him to give it to me on credit and I would pay back in monthly installments. He promised to talk to his son and we scheduled a meeting together after that. They agreed on my terms but they gave their own conditions that if I failed on my promises, they would seize the shop. So, we agreed on the contract. They gave me the key to the place. I threw away most of the stuff that wasn’t needed.

I went back to the companies that gave me goods on credit. I explained to them that I wanted to start all over and still needed their products on credit. It was difficult but they agreed in the end. I started gradually. I arranged the shops with African goods and drinks. I would sell and pay back till I paid up all the debts. In one year, I paid the Moroccan guys and they gave me the full ownership of my shop. I started full operation and we opened a second branch. We opened another nightclub business for African people to have parties. We organised parties twice in summer and once in winter. My place became a place where Africans meet for relationships and it was a booming business for me. Then, I had a restaurant, and with much patronage, it extended to a supermarket. After a while, we had a salon. I import goods from Nigeria, Cameroon, Uganda, the United States, the United Kingdom and China.

Is it only business you ventured into?

I contested for the municipal election in 2022. Our community was growing as Nigerians and Africans and there was a need for us to influence policies as well. Whenever I talked to the Dutch, they would ask, “Why are your people not involved?” For instance, if the Dutch government builds a low-coast estate, and Nigerians don’t have part in it, it is because they don’t have representatives to speak for them at the grassroots. So, I thought about this and contested when the opportunity came. I won but gave my position to a Ghanaian lady who came sixth. I used it to test my popularity and opened the eyes of our people to politics. I couldn’t do it because I handle a lot of things in terms of business. It would be difficult for me to sit in parliament when my phone is ringing. Now, we know better in politics as Nigerians.

What has sustained you in diaspora?

Everyone has principles that work for them but mine is focus. I don’t get distracted. I focus on what I want. It also means that you must be honest with yourself. I work hard and believe strongly in success. With all this, I have many recognitions and awards. Different communities like Edo and Yoruba have given me awards. But, I thank God for all those opportunities because of my selflessness and hard work.
