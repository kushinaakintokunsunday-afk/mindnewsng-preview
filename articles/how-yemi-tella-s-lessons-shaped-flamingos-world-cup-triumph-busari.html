How Yemi Tella’s lessons shaped Flamingos World Cup triumph – Busari

By Punch Punch 4d ago 10 min read

After guiding Nigeria’s Flamingos to a historic FIFA U-17 Women’s World Cup triumph, head coach Akeem Busari speaks on the journey to global glory, the experiences that shaped his coaching philosophy and how he built a world-beating team in just seven months. He shares his thoughts with ABIODUN ADEWALE

Congratulations on leading Nigeria to the FIFA U-17 Women’s World Cup title and becoming the first African team to achieve that. How does the achievement make you feel, knowing the passion Nigerians have for football?

Football is a game of emotions, and we know Nigerians to be passionate. For us, representing over 200 million Nigerians at this level, we had to do our bit to show why we were saddled with that responsibility. We are happy, elated and proud that God has used us to put smiles on the faces of Nigerians and Africa as a whole. It is a source of pride for our names, our families and the integrity of the game.

Let’s go back to the days when nobody knew the name Akeem Busari. How did you put in the work over the years to obtain your coaching certificates, build your network and identify grassroots talents to shape your career as a coach?

Some people start in football and quickly move to the sidelines. For others, it may be a rare opportunity, while injuries also push some people into coaching. But the bottom line is perseverance and determination. Whenever an opportunity has not come, you just have to keep developing the talent God has given you and continue to improve yourself through coaching education in line with modern-day demands.

I started after my playing days and obtained my Advanced Diploma, the highest diploma at the National Institute for Sports. I got those certificates between 1999 and 2005. Aside from that, I have attended different courses, both online and physically. I became a CAF-licensed coach in 2016 and have since upgraded to a CAF A Licence.

I have represented Nigerian teams in the CAF Champions League, both male and female. I was with Enyimba during the Confederation Cup in 2022, and I was also with Bayelsa Queens, winning the President Federation Cup with them and taking them to the CAF Women’s Champions League. These are things I have been doing passionately, moving from one club to another. When the opportunity to lead the women’s U-17 team came in April, I had to look inward and identify players within the age bracket who could fit into the team. When we arrived at the numbers we needed for qualification, we played all our matches and won all five games at the WAFU B tournament, scoring a high number of goals.

The World Cup was another level of the game, and we were able to see maturity. Some of our opponents from Europe had players from big clubs and had global experience compared to some of our girls, who were still in school. But we are a gifted country when it comes to talent and capacity, and we were able to apply the basic methodology, which helped us.

At the World Cup, we lost our first game, which was quite difficult but understandable. However, we knew the team we had and believed we could come back. We analysed the games and changed our methodology individually, in pairs, in groups and in terms of team cohesion. That was what fostered the winning formula that took us to the final.

Nigeria had previously won bronze under coach Bankole Olowokere at the 2022 edition in India. How closely did you follow that campaign, and what lessons did you take from that achievement into your seven-month preparation for the tournament?

Coach Bankole is a colleague, and it was good for Nigeria to have been on the podium before. We watched the games they played last year, when they were knocked out in the second round. We saw why we lost to Italy, and it wasn’t because we were bad. We used that as a point of reference. We also picked games that we won and used them to learn. From there, we adopted a mindset that, against every team we played, nobody should score before us. And if they did, how would we respond? If you look at all the games, it was only Poland that scored before us, and that was the only game we lost. So, it was all about mentality. Immediately, we locked into that mindset; it helped us mentally. Even in training, we played with a high numerical advantage against the opposing team and told the players to play. That anxiety, tension and ability to cope with one another, when transferred to our matches against other teams, coupled with our physical ability as Africans, really helped us. We played against teams that had bigger players than ours, but it was a matter of mindset and approach.

What was the biggest challenge you faced during the seven-month preparation for the World Cup?

One of the biggest challenges was the headache of selection. We had some injury challenges, and those players were good enough to make the team. When about two or three of them, who could have added more depth to the team, sustained injuries and went for medical checks, we were told they would be out. We had to look inward, and today, we are celebrating all of us.

We saw the matches on television, but not what happened behind the scenes. What were some of the moments in the dressing room and at camp that helped turn the team into champions?

The first thing was discipline. If we scheduled any activity for 4 pm, everybody would be out and ready 10 minutes before then. Whenever we wanted to do match analysis, nobody was late, and everyone had to come with their roommates. We took it as a mission because we wanted to go back home with the trophy. Everyone was locked in. If there were any issues among the players, the captains would walk up to me and say, ‘Papa, this is what is happening, but don’t say I told you.’ I knew how to manage the situation. They are young players, and we had to ensure they did not lose confidence. We managed them physically and mentally and were even mindful of what we said to them.

How important was the support of your backroom staff to the team’s success?

I had two assistants, Coach Jegede and Coach Omoidiaghe, and Coach Kola Ige as the goalkeeper trainer. There was also Dr Oyinye, the physiotherapist, Charity, Rhoda, the kit manager, the team secretary and everyone else. They were fantastic. The moment I sent a programme, they set up the cones. We made decisions together, and it was a big family.

You have repeatedly emphasised the importance of grassroots football in your career. How did you identify and select the players who eventually formed the World Cup-winning squad?

The grassroots has been my main catchment area. I started from there and was in the setup for 14 years. Even when I moved to big teams, I had one or two grassroots players to work with. I normally produce players and even go as far as writing programmes for players who want to go on trials.

You were the technical director of Pacesetter Queens before your appointment as Flamingos coach. Given the team’s position in the NWFL at the time, some people questioned your credentials for the national team job. How did you handle those doubts, and what does the World Cup triumph say to those who underestimated you?

I was the technical director of Pacesetter Queens, and I joined the team a week after the start of the league. We were a youth team with players who did not have the experience of playing in the league. But we stuck with them, and people would have seen the level of coaching in that team. We gave teams like Edo Queens and Bayelsa Queens a run for their money. One thing Nigerians should understand is that it is about knowing the technical ability of a coach. It is about who the person is and their track record. Sometimes, you have the materials and things work well, but when you don’t have the materials, you also have to ask whether the coach can still achieve something.

Which coaches have had the greatest influence on your career, and whose methods did you draw from as you prepared the Flamingos for the World Cup?

My biggest mentor was the late coach Yemi Tella. I was in camp with him before the 2007 FIFA Men’s U-17 World Cup. That was where I gained experience in managing an U-17 team. I had been with the late Yemi Tella since 1997, even before I attended any coaching course, and he was the one who took me around when I was Lagos State head coach for the National Sports Festival in 2004 and 2005. When he was in camp with his U-17 players in 2007, I was offering technical assistance, and the experience I gathered there was a plus for me when I got the women’s U-17 job almost 19 years later. I think what I sowed is what I am reaping, and I just pray for God’s guidance moving forward.

Can you take us through your journey as a footballer and how injuries influenced your decision to become a coach?

When I was at CMS Grammar School in 1982, I played football for the school and hockey for Lagos State. In 1983, I played both football and hockey for the state, and in 1985, I represented Lagos State in football. That was when I was preparing to pursue further studies after completing my O’Level. In 1986, I sustained an injury to my left knee and could no longer play football as much, so I focused on hockey. I played a couple of club games in hockey, but I did not return to football until I ventured into coaching around 1993. I started with a youth team, and people used to tell me that what I had to offer was better than what some more experienced coaches were offering. So, it is just a gift, and when people see what you have, that pushes you to do more. That was what encouraged me to go to the NIS and become a CAF A-licensed coach, as well as an instructor for CAF and the NFF.

Coaching at the grassroots level is often driven by passion, with limited resources and little financial reward. Now that you and your players are receiving millions of naira in rewards, how significant is this moment for your career and personal life?

Sometimes, you don’t expect anything, and then it comes in multiple folds. I want to thank God for the players and everyone around us. I pray that bigger things will come our way. We cannot rest; we need to redouble our efforts and talents. As I always tell them, immediately you achieve one thing, you want to achieve two. The destination is higher than this, and with discipline, we will get there.
