Opposition coalition fixes November 30 for unity pact — Lukman

By Punch Punch 4d ago 9 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The coalition of opposition political parties seeking to challenge President Bola Tinubu in the 2027 presidential election has shifted the date for the signing

This came amid continuing uncertainty over whether the parties can agree on a common presidential candidate.

Former Director General of the Progressives Governors Forum, Salihu Lukman, disclosed this during an interview on Arise TV.

Lukman explained that the date was moved by one week following consultations with political party leaders and presidential candidates who wanted more time to adjust their campaign schedules.

He said the coalition was working to ensure that party chairmen, presidential candidates and their running mates would be present at the Abuja gathering to formally endorse the agreement.

He described the planned signing as a significant step in the effort to bring opposition parties together, insisting that the process had moved beyond the initial stage of discussions and was now being driven by the participating political parties.

“One of the thing we want to be sure on that day is that all the party leadership and all the presidential candidates and the running will be in that hall. That is the first thing and while majority of them are ready.

“Others said they needed to adjust their campaign schedule. So we discuss and reach some compromise. So, it’s likely that some of them may not be there. We agree to shift it by one week to 30th November. We shift it to 30th November,” he said.

The coalition brings together six political parties and factions, including the African Democratic Congress (ADC), Allied Peoples Movement (APM), National Democratic Congress (NDC), a faction of the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) associated with Tanimu Turaki, the People’s Redemption Party (PRP) and the Social Democratic Party (SDP).

According to Lukman, the parties are expected to sign a formal agreement committing them to cooperation, with details of possible joint campaigns and other forms of political collaboration to be worked out.

However, the most difficult issue confronting the coalition remains unresolved: whether the participating parties will eventually agree to support a single presidential candidate against Tinubu.

Lukman disclosed that the coalition had developed three major documents to provide the foundation for its proposed political cooperation.

They are a National Covenant for Democratic Renewal, a Framework for Opposition Unity and a National Reform Programme.

He stated, “So we spend the better part of the time from August to now trying to put the table and convince all of them to sit on the table.

“I think we have reached that agreement right and on the basis of that on that 30th November all of them will be there and the party chairman will sign a formal agreement committing to working together and what working together will entail will include a number of things that will be announced that very day which may include some joint campaigns. The detail will be worked out and will be announced on that very day.”

He explained that the covenant would establish the principles guiding the coalition, while the framework would provide mechanisms for managing disagreements among participating parties.

The third document, the National Reform Programme, would outline the minimum policy commitments expected of any government produced by the coalition.

Describing it as a collective manifesto, Lukman said the programme had been developed by examining the policy proposals of the different parties and identifying areas where they could reach common ground.

“The National Reform Programme is basically like the minimum undertaking that a government produced by the coalition will commit to,” he said.

He added that the documents would be made available to the media and the Nigerian public on November 30 for scrutiny.

Lukman insisted that the coalition was not asking participating parties to dissolve themselves or abandon their individual political identities.

Rather, the objective was to create a platform through which they could cooperate while retaining their structures and existing candidates.

Despite the progress claimed by the coalition, Lukman acknowledged that there was currently no agreed mechanism for selecting a common presidential candidate.

He said the organisers needed the direct involvement and consent of the presidential candidates before such an arrangement could be discussed conclusively.

“We are not asking any party to dissolve itself and stop existing. They will continue to exist,” he explained.

He also acknowledged that some parties were prepared to cooperate only on issues such as electoral credibility and the protection of votes, without necessarily agreeing to support another party’s presidential candidate.

“The issue is particularly sensitive given the competing ambitions of opposition figures, including former Vice-President Atiku Abubakar and former Anambra State governor Peter Obi.

“The NDC, for instance, has indicated that any discussion about a common presidential candidate must take account of its preference for the Obi-Wan ticket,” he said.

Lukman said such differences would have to be addressed through further negotiations, describing the entire process as one of confidence-building.

He expressed concern that supporters of opposition parties were increasingly engaging in public attacks against one another rather than concentrating on challenging the ruling APC.

“One of the things that is so disturbing to us is a situation where people in opposition are fighting themselves and dissipating energy against themselves instead of facing a common enemy,” he said.

He warned that the APC would ultimately benefit if the opposition allowed its internal disagreements to undermine the coalition.

According to him, party leaders and presidential candidates must take responsibility for regulating the conduct of their supporters to prevent further divisions.

Lukman argued that the opposition’s failure to unite was a major factor in the outcome of the 2023 presidential election.

He recalled that opposition candidates collectively secured substantially more votes than the eventual winner, but failed to translate that support into electoral victory because they contested separately.

“Two opposition candidates, or even three combined, got more than 40 million votes. The winner of the election got eight million votes,” he said.

He argued that the figures demonstrated that the opposition had sufficient electoral strength to challenge the APC if its supporters and political structures could be coordinated.

“It was not even the APC that defeated the opposition; the opposition defeated itself,” he declared.

Lukman challenged opposition politicians to decide whether their ultimate objective was to win the presidency or simply participate in an election to satisfy their individual ambitions.

“Are they working to get their candidate to win the election and become president of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, or are they just going to the election to satisfy themselves and their candidates?” he asked.

He urged the opposition leaders to demonstrate statesmanship and place the national interest above personal political calculations.

Beyond the question of a common presidential candidate, Lukman identified electoral credibility as one of the most important areas of cooperation being negotiated.

He said the coalition’s first common national undertaking would be to protect the integrity of votes at every polling unit.

This could involve the joint deployment of polling agents by participating parties, particularly in areas where individual parties lack the resources to cover all polling units.

Lukman noted that some opposition parties did not have the capacity to deploy agents across Nigeria’s approximately 176,000 polling units.

He said the coalition intended to map the country, identify areas of electoral strength and pool the resources of participating parties to ensure adequate coverage.

“All the parties must come together and deploy their resources and get their agents together to protect the vote,” he said.

He argued that such cooperation would also help restore public confidence in the electoral process and encourage greater voter participation.

According to him, voter apathy remains a serious concern because many Nigerians believe their votes may not necessarily determine the eventual outcome of elections.

“When citizens now see parties coming together in the way we are trying to do, that will give them confidence to come out,” he said.

The coalition convenor also argued that Nigeria’s political system required fundamental reforms beyond simply replacing the ruling party.

He questioned the existing approach to zoning and power rotation, insisting that political arrangements should not be allowed to produce leaders who are unaccountable to citizens.

“The way this country is today, the democracy and the politics have been so bastardised that everything needs to be renegotiated,” he said.

He added that zoning arrangements that failed to guarantee accountable leadership had become a problem rather than a solution.

Lukman also called for stronger political parties, arguing that many of the country’s political organisations were not functioning in accordance with their own constitutions.

He warned against producing another president who would dominate the legislature and impose political leadership on the National Assembly.

“We don’t want to have another president produced by the coalition that will now come and bulldoze his way to impose leadership of the National Assembly and reduce the National Assembly to basically people singing choir for the president,” he said.

For him, the proposed coalition must, therefore, be about more than defeating Tinubu.

Although Lukman expressed satisfaction with the progress made, the timing of the proposed agreement presents a considerable challenge.

With the election approaching, the coalition still has to resolve disagreements over presidential ambitions, possible power-sharing arrangements, joint campaign structures and the practical details of electoral cooperation.

The November 30 meeting is therefore expected to be an important test of whether the opposition parties can translate their public commitment to unity into a workable political arrangement.

Lukman acknowledged that considerable work remained, but said the fact that party leaders were now sitting together was itself evidence of progress.

He maintained that the process had moved beyond the early scepticism that greeted the initiative, insisting that the participating parties had taken ownership of the negotiations.

The 2023 presidential election exposed the divisions within Nigeria’s opposition, as several major parties fielded separate candidates against the APC’s Bola Tinubu.

Tinubu won the election with 8,794,726 votes. Atiku Abubakar of the PDP came second with 6,984,520 votes, while Peter Obi of the Labour Party secured 6,101,533 votes. Rabiu Kwankwaso of the New Nigeria Peoples Party polled 1,496,687 votes.

The three leading opposition candidates, Atiku, Obi and Kwankwaso, recorded a combined 14.58 million votes.

However, they contested separately and did not present a common presidential ticket.

The outcome has since fuelled calls for opposition parties to unite ahead of the 2027 election, with supporters of a coalition arguing that a common candidate could provide a stronger challenge to the ruling APC.
