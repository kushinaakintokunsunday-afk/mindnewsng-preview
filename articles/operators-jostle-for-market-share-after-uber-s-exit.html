Operators jostle for market share after Uber’s exit

By Punch Punch 1d ago 3 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Uber’s withdrawal from Nigeria has intensified competition in the country’s ride-hailing market, putting renewed focus on transport fares, passenger safety and

The global ride-hailing company discontinued its Nigerian operations on September 2, 2026, after 12 years in the market, creating opportunities for existing and emerging operators to strengthen their positions.

The exit has also heightened expectations around service reliability, affordability and the financial sustainability of app-based transportation, as commuters reassess their mobility options.

Ride-hailing services have become an increasingly important part of urban transportation, particularly in major cities where commuters depend on app-based platforms for convenience, predictable journeys and access to transport outside conventional public transport hours.

A 2025 Ride-Hailing Safety Index Report conducted by Ipsos and commissioned by Bolt found that 81 per cent of surveyed Nigerian passengers considered ride-hailing safer than other forms of transportation.

The survey, which covered Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Ibadan, also found that 96 per cent of respondents used ride-hailing services when public transport felt unsafe.

The findings highlight the importance of passenger confidence in a market where safety and reliability increasingly influence consumer decisions.

Onadipe Oluwafemi David, founder of Nigerian ride-hailing service Ona, said the industry’s next phase would depend on how effectively operators addressed the expectations of passengers and drivers.

“The opportunity in Nigeria’s ride-hailing market goes beyond filling the gap left by an existing operator. Commuters want a service they can depend on, while drivers need an operating structure that makes their participation worthwhile. Platforms that address both sides of the experience will be better positioned to compete in the long term,” he said.

Despite Uber’s departure, other operators, including Bolt and inDrive, remain active in the Nigerian market.

Their ability to retain existing customers and attract new users will depend partly on how they address transportation costs and service quality.

For passengers, affordability, waiting times, driver verification and access to customer support remain important considerations.

For drivers, rising fuel prices, vehicle maintenance costs and platform commissions continue to affect the financial viability of ride-hailing operations.

The situation presents a challenge for operators, as lower fares could attract more passengers, but driver earnings must remain sustainable to ensure service availability and encourage driver retention.

Safety infrastructure is also emerging as an important competitive factor. Features such as identity verification, trip monitoring, accessible emergency support and transparent driver information can strengthen accountability and build trust between platforms and users.

As the market adjusts to Uber’s exit, competition is expected to extend beyond the number of available platforms to the quality of their operating models.

For Nigeria’s ride-hailing industry, the immediate challenge is to absorb displaced demand while demonstrating that digital mobility services can deliver consistent value to passengers and support the livelihoods of drivers.
