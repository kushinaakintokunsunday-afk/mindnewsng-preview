Africa needs own payment system to escape Western control – PAPSS CEO

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 15 min read

In this interview with WALE AKINSELURE, the Chief Executive Officer of the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System, Mike Ogbalu, speaks on the platform’s expansion across Africa, efforts to boost intra-African trade through local currencies, reduce dependence on the dollar and make cross-border payments faster and cheaper

PAPSS was launched in 2022 to facilitate cross-border payments across Africa. Four years on, how much progress has it made towards achieving this objective?

Over the past four years, we have built a payment network that spans 31 countries. We initially started in West Africa with six countries, but today, we have 31 countries across Africa. We are in partnership with 25 central banks, and we expect that number to grow to 26, while our countries of presence will increase to 39 before the end of the year. We currently have about 120 commercial banks connected to our network, and we have about 17 switches. Switches are technology banks that connect multiple banks within a market or across multiple markets.

In terms of our growth, we have recorded about 1,000 per cent growth in our transactions compared with last year, and clearly, we are now beginning to see a lot of this impact trickle down to our users. It is important to talk about the Nigerian market, where we have at least 95 per cent adoption by commercial banks. We are beginning to see many of them open up PAPSS within their digital channels and make it available to their consumers. We have also started engaging with many fintech companies, and some Nigerian fintech companies have begun integrating with PAPSS, enabling them to interact with all the countries where we are present.

What tangible benefits are users gaining from PAPSS?

PAPSS is an instant payment system. So, one of the things PAPSS does is save time. In the past, many payment systems could take two to five days for transactions to settle. But with PAPSS, settlement is instant. The transaction originates in one currency and terminates in another local currency in not more than 120 seconds. In fact, 120 seconds is the maximum; today, we are processing those transactions in an average of about seven seconds.

To put it in context, within those seven seconds, a transaction would have originated from the originating market – it may be through a mobile channel or across the counter in a bank branch – and would have gone through our system and all our compliance checks. It would pass through our anti-money laundering checks, fraud checks and other screenings before being delivered to the recipient’s account in the other market. The recipient would also have received an alert, with all these happening within seven seconds.

So, instead of spending three days making calls to confirm the alert, that extra time can be used by people to turn around their inventory and make more money from their businesses. They enjoy the benefit of the speed of the transaction.

Secondly, in terms of cost, PAPSS averages about 0.1 per cent of the transaction value in charges to users, compared with between three and five per cent charged through alternative payment channels, or even up to 10 per cent, as we see in intra-African remittances. This 0.1 per cent is PAPSS’s fee. Sometimes, commercial banks are designed to make money. So, we need to plead with them not to mark up the charges excessively so that some of those benefits can get to the consumers themselves.

We have seen significant progress. You can now have small shoemakers in Aba selling across Africa without worrying about how payments from those African countries will be settled into their accounts or wallets in Nigeria.

Why has PAPSS yet to connect all 54 African countries, particularly South Africa, one of the continent’s largest economies?

People sometimes look at PAPSS as a fintech company, but we are very far from that. A typical fintech company may start in one market, succeed in that market and then scale across other markets. We are carrying along many countries at the same time and trying to get all of them to scale.

The biggest challenge we have faced is standardisation. We are talking about 54 different countries with 54 different standards, payment systems and regulators. So, trying to harmonise all these countries into one coherent way of doing business across Africa is a challenge.

Secondly, there are issues of a few countries thinking that joining PAPSS means we will cannibalise some of their regional payment systems. For example, we tell them that if they like their regional payment system, they should keep it and use PAPSS for transactions that move from one region to another. So, the more we evangelise and show them that PAPSS does not cannibalise but only enhances the offerings of whatever payment system they have in place, the more acceptance we receive.

This year alone, we welcomed over 10 countries, and next year, we expect to welcome at least 10 more countries. So, the growth has been slow but steady.

But if you also look at some of the successes we have accomplished, this is the largest cross-border payment system within any of the regions. If you look at Europe, you are talking about 27 countries, but here, we are talking about 31. Some of these 31 countries are among the most complex to bring together because they do not have a single standard that cuts across all of them.

So, it has been a journey. The more voices speak about the benefits of integration, the more some of the challenges we have faced begin to disappear. Also, the more countries join, the more value they create for themselves and others. Some of these challenges existed in the past, but they are gradually disappearing, and I believe that, in no distant time, we will be able to have South Africa on board.

South Africa is the only major country that is not currently on PAPSS. All the other major countries have joined PAPSS, and I believe South Africa will join soon.

What proportion of Africa’s cross-border payment transactions currently goes through PAPSS?

We are still gathering our data, and we will make our PAPSS impact report available in the second quarter of next year. However, compared with last year, we have recorded growth of over 1,000 per cent in the transactions we have processed this year.

We expect that in the next five years, 80 per cent of all African countries will be transacting through our platform, and we expect to see 80 per cent of commercial payments on the continent routed through PAPSS.

Our focus in previous years was connecting as many African countries as possible, and I believe we have achieved significant success in that regard. Our next level of focus is driving adoption and usage, and that is what you will see in the next few years.

How close is PAPSS to achieving its goal of enabling African countries to trade in their local currencies without relying on the dollar?

We are already achieving that ambition. However, the success we are recording needs to be scaled up significantly.

For example, in a transaction between two countries, if one is sending $10bn worth of naira to Kenya and Kenya is sending about $11bn worth of Kenyan shillings to Nigeria, our system makes it possible for what would ordinarily have been a $21bn transaction to become just a $1bn transaction because only the net difference will be settled in a third-party currency.

Beyond that, 95 per cent of the value of those transactions is conducted in local currencies. So, what you are seeing is that Africa is beginning to reduce its dependence on foreign currencies. We are not chasing dollarisation because Africa still needs to trade with the rest of the world. But we are saying that when Africa trades with itself, let us use PAPSS to reduce the amount of foreign currencies required for trade among African countries.

We have modelled it such that you can actually reduce that requirement to as low as five per cent in some markets and 20 per cent in others. At 20 per cent, it represents significant progress towards strengthening African currencies. The 80 per cent you have saved represents 80 per cent more demand for African currencies compared with what we see today.

But we can scale it up so that there are multiple folds of the benefits we are recording today. In terms of benefits, all our member countries are beginning to see them; we just need to scale those benefits.

When a Nigerian exporter receives payment in Ghanaian cedis, how is the money converted into naira, and who determines the exchange rate?

Our system is pre-funded, so all our participants within the ecosystem provide some pre-funding to guarantee their transactions.

When transactions originate from Ghana, the exchange rate is supplied by the participant within that Ghanaian market, and our system supports multilateral settlement. You can set your rate for your market against one single currency. It also supports bilateral arrangements, in which you can set the rate from naira to cedis and from cedis to naira.

So, our system supports multiple rate settings. But, to be clear, the rate settings are guided by the central bank of each of the various markets and the commercial banks that are participants on our network. PAPSS, in itself, does not determine or set any country’s exchange rate. The participants on our system provide those rates.

So, once a transaction comes from one market onto our platform, it sees the rate that has been set from one currency to another, and the transaction is able to proceed. Some participants can also set their rates in a third-party currency, but the fact remains that only the net position will require foreign exchange to settle.

Can PAPSS reduce Africa’s dependence on the dollar despite the weakness and volatility of many African currencies?

Currencies operate according to the laws of demand and supply. So, the more people conduct a large part of their transactions in their local currencies, the greater the demand for those currencies, and we will begin to see local currencies strengthen against third-party global currencies.

PAPSS is keen on ensuring that, as more countries adopt the system, they significantly reduce their dependence on third-party global currencies and strengthen African currencies through increased utilisation of PAPSS.

How does PAPSS support the African Continental Free Trade Area in creating a single African market?

What you find is that the AfCFTA is a silver bullet for the continent. But it requires other silver bullets to succeed.

Everything around prosperity and human life is about creating value. The AfCFTA has done a fantastic job of bringing the rules for conducting trade together and harmonising them into a consistent set of rules for the continent.

The second critical component is that, before now, Africa’s payment system depended on third-party entities outside the continent for transactions to go through. The challenge with payment systems is that, as good as they are as tools of exchange, they are also instruments of control. A payment service provider can decide that your payment will not go through, and this is the instrument of Western sanctions across the world.

So, they can wake up, instruct certain people and say, ‘We do not want payments from this market to pass through to this other market.’

PAPSS is a critical instrument of the AfCFTA. I do not have any issue with people wanting to sanction a country, but essentially, let that decision be made by people sitting around the table on the continent, not by some people sitting somewhere around the world and making decisions that affect the economic destiny of Africans.

PAPSS is a critical pillar in enabling the AfCFTA, and the AfCFTA is one of the three promoters of PAPSS. The others are Afreximbank and the African Union Commission. This is why PAPSS enjoys the full endorsement of African heads of state.

How can small businesses and ordinary entrepreneurs benefit from PAPSS, beyond the involvement of central banks and governments?

We started with the regulators because we recognised that the payment system laws of each country across Africa give powers to the central banks of those countries. In the absence of a continent-wide payment framework, we got the central banks to sit in a room to draft a set of by-laws.

Essentially, each country joining is saying, ‘I agree to be bound by the provisions of these by-laws for transactions that relate to PAPSS.’

So, it was important for confidence, trust and regulatory oversight that we carried the central banks along and got them to be part of the creation of this system.

What the regulators have done is provide a covering. At the core, we built an instant payment system, and around that system, we created an anti-money laundering system, a sanctions screening system, a fraud management system and a dispute management mechanism. We then created governance organs. There is the PAPSS Governing Council, which has the power of regulation and oversight over PAPSS.

It is made up of 15 central bank governors and four other institutions, which are the representatives of Afreximbank, the African Development Bank, the AfCFTA and the African Union Commission. All these institutions provide oversight over PAPSS.

We have taken this technology and these governance organs and started connecting them with commercial banks through switches.

What this means is that an SME in Aba, Nigeria, who already has a wallet or an account with OPay or Moniepoint, or an account with a commercial bank, can initiate a payment to anybody in Africa without having to worry about all the things that come together in this ecosystem to ensure that the transaction goes through.

The SME can also receive payments from any part of Africa without knowing the intricacies of what happened or what was put together.

What PAPSS has done is take upon itself the headache of bringing all the participants together so that people can innovate and offer value to those SMEs.

For example, if you bank with First Bank, you can go to a UBA ATM and insert your card. You do not care about all the things that have been done behind the scenes to ensure that the ATM processes your transaction and gives you cash.

It is that same type of system we have put together so that payment service providers can now offer the service we have created to SMEs, enabling them to make and receive payments across Africa.

Once they are able to receive payments from across Africa, an SME that was previously selling to 200,000 or 220 million customers can suddenly sell to 1.4 billion people. That SME should also be able to increase its production capacity because it is producing for a larger market.

It is a value chain, and what we have done is take on the most difficult part, which is bringing together all the regulations and stakeholders, and now enable them to create value for SMEs to sell across the continent.

How can a small business owner access PAPSS, and what are the requirements?

You access it through your commercial bank or payment service providers. If my PSP has provided a wallet service, PAPSS has integrated with the PSP, and the PSP is able to extend that service to the SME.

If the person banks with Zenith Bank, the service is already embedded within the bank that the person is comfortable with.

PAPSS does not handle the last mile. PAPSS does not own those customers. We will not compete with the banks, but we expect that the banks will be able to take the value we have integrated into their systems and offer it as a service to those SMEs.

So, they access it through their existing commercial banks, PSPs or anybody that offers credit payment services in Nigeria.

Would the adoption of a single African currency strengthen or undermine PAPSS’s relevance?

It would make life easy for me if we had a single currency across the continent. But we also know that it is a long journey because anything that has to do with currency also has to do with the sovereignty of a country and control.

If you look at the euro, each country’s control over the currency has diminished significantly. It is something good to have, but whether the continent has the political will to make it happen is a different matter.

You need to have a set of criteria that must be met by all member countries, which has been very difficult.

I think the future will be technology-driven and that a single currency will emerge from available continental data rather than from the more traditional approaches adopted by countries.

I think the more we see data on our system showing the exchanges or payments from one country to another, the more we can establish the exchange relationships among African countries over time. This will make some of the challenges they face today gradually disappear because there will be enough data to support that.
