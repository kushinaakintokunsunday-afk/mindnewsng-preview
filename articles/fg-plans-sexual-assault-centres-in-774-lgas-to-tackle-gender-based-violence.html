FG plans sexual assault centres in 774 LGAs to tackle gender-based violence

By The Sun Nigeria The Sun Nigeria 22h ago 8 min read

• Only 52 centres currently serve over 240m Nigerians – Minister

Federal Government has unveiled plans to establish at least one Sexual Assault Referral Centre in each of Nigeria’s 774 Local Government Areas as part of efforts to strengthen the fight against gender-based violence and improve access to justice for survivors.

The Minister of Women Affairs and Social Development, Imaan Sulaiman-Ibrahim, disclosed this in Abuja on Monday at a press conference to flag off the 2026 16 Days of Activism Against Gender-Based Violence.

She lamented that Nigeria currently has only 52 Sexual Assault Referral Centres providing medical, psychosocial and referral services to survivors, describing the number as grossly inadequate for a country with a population of more than 240 million.

According to her, the Federal Government is working with state governments to ensure that at least one centre is established in every local government area, with the possibility of extending the facilities to every ward.

The minister explained that the centres would provide comprehensive support beyond responses to sexual abuse, including psychosocial assistance and protection mechanisms for survivors.

She said: “For a nation of over 240 million people, 52 centres are grossly inadequate. We are actively working with state governments to ensure that at least one Sexual Assault Referral Centre is established in every local government area across the country.

“Indeed, nothing stops us from eventual establishment of a sexual assault centre in every ward.”

Sulaiman-Ibrahim also disclosed that gender-based violence costs Nigeria an estimated 1.5 per cent of its Gross Domestic Product, amounting to approximately $3 billion annually through lost productivity, healthcare burdens and disrupted livelihoods.

She further cited national data indicating that nearly one in three Nigerian women aged 18 to 49 had experienced physical or sexual violence, while more than 27,000 sexual and gender-based violence cases were documented in a recent reporting cycle, alongside millions of unreported incidents.

Describing the figures as a national emergency, the minister said the country must move beyond annual commemorations and translate awareness campaigns into measurable protection, justice and accountability.

“This is not just another global slogan or call. It is a clear demand to move beyond empty awareness and deliver real justice, real protection for survivors and an absolute end to impunity across our beloved country,” she said.

The 2026 campaign, themed, “Unite to End Violence Against Women and Girls: Ensure Justice, Protect Survivors and End Impunity,” runs from November 25 to December 10.

The minister said the Federal Government was adopting a coordinated national approach through the Renewed Hope Social Impact Intervention 774, a nine-pillar framework targeting 50 million women, children and vulnerable Nigerians across the country’s 774 local government areas.

She listed the intervention areas to include economic inclusion, clean energy, digital literacy, agriculture, healthcare, social protection, affirmative procurement, leadership and family development.

Under the initiative, she said the government was targeting the empowerment of five million women through the Nigerian for Women Programme scale-up, while the Happy Women Platform and App would onboard 10 million women to improve financial literacy, savings and enterprise support.

She added that the Digital Harmony initiative would train five million women in essential digital skills, while the Women Rise, Innovate and Create initiative would empower three million women in the creative economy.

The minister also highlighted the Power of 10 Million initiative, designed to organise women into grassroots groups for savings, access to credit, cooperative enterprises, mutual protection and civic participation.

On legal protection, Sulaiman-Ibrahim said the Child Rights Act had been domesticated in all 36 states, while the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act had been domesticated in 35 states.

She said the Ministry was working to strengthen the implementation of both laws and close existing gaps, while reviewing the National Gender Policy, which expires this year.

She also announced the commencement of efforts to strengthen the National Boy Child Policy, arguing that protecting boys alongside girls was necessary to break the cycle of intergenerational violence.

“For decades, social interventions have largely focused on the girl child. Yet, we can no longer ignore the rising school dropout rates, substance abuse, street vulnerabilities and social neglect confronting our boys.

“By intentionally mentoring, protecting and empowering the boy child alongside the girl child, we are breaking the cycle of intergenerational violence at its roots and raising responsible men who will become true partners in building peaceful homes and a secure nation,” she said.

The minister further disclosed that the Federal Executive Council had approved the National Strategy and Costed Action Plan on Ending Child Marriage in Nigeria, 2025–2030, as well as the National Policy on Menstrual Health and Hygiene Management, 2026–2030, and the National Care Policy and Implementation Plan, 2026–2030.

She said the menstrual health policy and incentives approved by President Bola Tinubu for local manufacturers of sanitary products would help reduce the country’s dependence on imported menstrual hygiene products.

Sulaiman-Ibrahim also announced that the national Gender-Based Violence Electronic Dashboard and Situation Room was now operational to strengthen real-time data management, evidence-based interventions, coordination and accountability.

She called for greater collaboration among government institutions, development partners, civil society organisations, traditional and religious leaders, and the media, stressing that the fight against gender-based violence required sustained commitment beyond the 16-day campaign.

The Minister urged all stakeholders to sustain their commitment beyond the campaign, insisting that the 16 Days of Activism must translate into 365 days of action.

“The time for silence is over. The time for collective action is now. Let us unite to end violence against women and girls, ensure justice, protect survivors and end impunity,” she said.

In their goodwill messages, representatives of United Nations agencies and development partners called for stronger enforcement of existing laws, improved survivor-centred services, adequate funding and accountability to end violence against women and girls.

The UN Women Country Representative to Nigeria (and ECOWAS) Beatrice Eyong, reaffirmed the organisation’s commitment to supporting the Federal Government, noting that approximately 840 million women globally, representing one in three, had experienced physical or sexual violence in their lifetime.

Eyong who was represented, identified intimate partner violence, rape, sexual assault, child marriage, female genital mutilation, trafficking, sexual harassment, harmful traditional practices and technology-facilitated violence as major manifestations of abuse in Nigeria.

She urged the government to strengthen laws, close implementation gaps, invest in survivor-centred services and ensure predictable funding for interventions.

The International IDEA representative, who delivered a message on behalf of the organisation’s Head of Programmes in Nigeria, Danladi Plang, said the fight against gender-based violence must go beyond the annual 16-day commemoration.

She lamented that girls who suffered rape were often blamed for their dressing, women beaten in their homes were told to endure the abuse, while some children were forced into marriage before they understood the meaning of childhood.

She called for effective implementation of the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act, stronger protection systems, accessible survivor-centred services and accountable institutions.

According to her, justice should not depend on a survivor’s social status or economic circumstances.

The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) representative commended the Minister for efforts to raise awareness about existing laws and encourage their domestication across states.

She emphasised prevention, believing survivors and ensuring that every form of violence was properly identified and addressed.

She also disclosed that UNFPA had worked with universities to institutionalise training on gender-based violence in emergencies, with 28 graduates completing the programme in the previous week.

She called for stronger implementation of existing laws, investment in essential services and sustained efforts to address the root causes of gender-based violence.

The United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) representative, Wafaa Abdelatef, said violence against children and adolescent girls required urgent intervention, stressing that survivors must be heard, believed, protected and supported.

Abdelatef, who was represented urged the media to report cases responsibly, protect survivors’ identities and dignity, challenge the normalisation of violence and hold institutions and decision-makers accountable.

“To every child listening today, our message is simple: violence is never your fault. You deserve to be safe. You deserve to be heard. You deserve justice,” he said.

The team leader of the SPRiNG Programme also called for stronger community-level interventions, noting that conflict, displacement and climate pressures continued to expose women and girls to violence by disrupting livelihoods and weakening community protection systems.

He disclosed that the programme was working with community leaders, legal experts and the Nigeria Police Force to strengthen referrals, provide pro bono legal services to survivors and improve forensic evidence gathering for stronger investigations and prosecutions.

He urged stakeholders to integrate prevention into institutional plans and budgets and ensure that survivors had access to timely, confidential and accessible services.

Similarly, the Daisy Hope Foundation’s Programme Director, Dr Kiema Ogunlana, called for sustained coordination and improved referral services to ensure that women and girls in underserved communities, including persons with disabilities, were not left behind.

She urged the media to protect survivors’ privacy and follow up on commitments made by government and development partners.

The Director-General of the Maryam Babangida National Centre for Women Development, Adedayo Benjamins-Laniyi, highlighted the importance of translating existing laws into practical community-level action.

She cited the Centre’s collaboration with UN Women on a 220-hour Guinness World Record attempt involving the reading of African laws against sexual and gender-based violence, describing the initiative as an effort to give voice to the law and strengthen its implementation at the grassroots.
