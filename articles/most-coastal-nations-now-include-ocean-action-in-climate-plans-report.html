Most coastal nations now include ocean action in climate plans — Report

By Punch Punch 16 Nov 2026 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

More than 95 per cent of recently submitted climate plans by coastal and island countries include ocean-based measures to tackle climate change, but most still

A new analysis by the World Resources Institute, titled Ocean-Based Climate Action in 2025 NDCs, found that ocean-related commitments in national climate plans, known as Nationally Determined Contributions, rose from 62 per cent in 2015 to 73 per cent in 2022 and more than 95 per cent in the latest submissions.

The report, however, warned that translating the commitments into measurable results would remain difficult without international cooperation and support, particularly for developing countries.

“Sixty-one per cent of the 1,281 ocean-related commitments analysed were classified as conditional or partly conditional, meaning countries could not deliver them with their existing financial, technical, and institutional resources. The challenge was more pronounced among developing economies, with all commitments from low-income countries classified as conditional or partly conditional. The proportion stood at 87 per cent for lower-middle-income countries and 92 per cent for upper-middle-income countries, compared with 35 per cent for high-income countries,” the report stated.

The analysis identified institutional coordination and governance, reliable data, technology, technical expertise and finance as major areas where governments needed assistance.

It said the support required went beyond direct project financing to include policy reforms, clearer institutional responsibilities, effective monitoring systems and stronger capacity in public institutions and regulatory agencies.

Citing research by the High-Level Panel for a Sustainable Ocean Economy, the report noted that ocean-based climate solutions could provide up to 35 per cent of the annual emissions reductions needed by 2050.

Such interventions could also protect coastal communities and sustain industries dependent on healthy marine and coastal ecosystems.

On the nature of the commitments, the study found that adaptation accounted for 69.9 per cent, while 22 per cent focused exclusively on reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Another 8.1 per cent combined mitigation and adaptation.

Adaptation measures included mangrove and wetland restoration, coastal protection, improved planning and monitoring, and responses to flooding and saltwater intrusion into groundwater.

In sub-Saharan Africa, 84.95 per cent of ocean-related commitments focused on adaptation, with fisheries and coastal zone management emerging as major priorities.

The report said this reflected the region’s need to reduce disaster risks, strengthen food security, protect coastal livelihoods and develop sustainable export industries.

“Stronger fisheries management and the protection of coastal ecosystems could support food production and the livelihoods of coastal communities, while investment in resilient ports and infrastructure could protect trade and economic activities from rising sea levels and extreme weather.

“Although mitigation accounted for a smaller proportion of ocean-related commitments, countries were increasingly looking beyond conservation to decarbonise maritime transport and energy industries. 51.8 per cent of the 282 mitigation-only commitments analysed concerned ocean industries, including shipping, offshore renewable energy, alternative fuels, and port decarbonization. The measures include improving vessel efficiency, adopting cleaner fuels, reducing emissions from ports, and expanding offshore wind energy,” the report stated.

The analysis also found that countries’ priorities varied by income level and geographical location.

“Adaptation accounted for 88 per cent of ocean commitments in low-income countries, 80 per cent in lower-middle-income countries, 62 per cent in upper-middle-income countries, and 57 per cent in high-income countries. Conversely, commitments with a mitigation component increased from 12 per cent in low-income countries to 43 per cent in high-income countries,” it stated.

The report said this focus was particularly important to Pacific Island economies, where shipping connects communities to markets and essential services, while dependence on imported fuels creates economic pressure.

It stressed the need for international cooperation, technology transfer and support tailored to countries’ development needs.

The findings come amid growing concerns over the gap between national climate commitments and the action required to limit global warming.

Citing the United Nations Environment Programme’s 2026 Emissions Gap Report, the analysis said current policies could result in about 2.8 degrees Celsius of warming this century, while full implementation of existing national climate commitments would limit the increase to about 2.6 degrees Celsius.

The report identified the 31st United Nations Climate Change Conference, COP31, as an opportunity to link national ocean priorities with practical implementation and financing.

It said discussions under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change’s Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue had highlighted the need for stronger cooperation to overcome implementation barriers.

The report also pointed to the COP31 Presidency’s Climate Implementation Bridge, which seeks to connect national priorities with project preparation, implementation capacity and financing partners.

It added that the Blue NDC Challenge, an initiative through which governments cooperate on ocean-related climate commitments, had brought together members whose plans account for 332 commitments, representing 25.1 per cent of the global total.
