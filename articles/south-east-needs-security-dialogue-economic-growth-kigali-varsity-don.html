South-East needs security, dialogue, economic growth — Kigali varsity don

By Punch Punch 4d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Deputy Vice-Chancellor for Research, Innovation and Enterprise at the University of Kigali, Rwanda, Professor Ogechi Adeola, has outlined a seven-point agen

Adeola said the resilience of the Igbo people should not be celebrated as a substitute for addressing the conditions that make survival difficult, including insecurity, disrupted livelihoods and inadequate systems.

“We can’t keep praising the Igbo man for surviving what a system should have prevented,” she said.

She presented the agenda in her keynote address titled, “The Southeast Paradox: Olaudah Equiano, Igbo Identity and the Choice Between Conflict and Conversation,” at the 2026 International Conference of the Forum of South-East Academic Doctors, themed “Nigeria’s Southeast at a Crossroads: Conflict or Conversation,” on Friday. 

The seven priorities she outlined include safety and the rule of law, inclusive dialogue, economic and infrastructure coordination, youth development and innovation, cultural and historical preservation, the application of indigenous knowledge, and accountability through measurable results.

On security, Adeola called for an environment where people could travel, trade, learn and express themselves without fear, intimidation or harassment. She also stressed that violence and abuses should be investigated lawfully.

She linked security to the possibility of meaningful dialogue, arguing that people cannot freely participate in conversations about the region’s future when they are afraid to speak.

“Can there be real dialogue where people still fear to speak?” she asked.

She called for conversations that include women, young people, victims, residents and other communities, insisting that participation must result in clear commitments and follow-through rather than remain a symbolic exercise.

On economic development, Adeola urged the five South-East states to coordinate their efforts, particularly in addressing challenges that cut across state boundaries. She called for connected markets and infrastructure, shared projects and joint ventures that could strengthen regional commerce.

“The economic and security problems cross borders. So there should be coordination,” she said.

The professor also identified skills development, research, innovation and enterprise as essential to creating opportunities for young people. She called for practical investment in youth development and the renewal of systems such as the Igba Boyi apprenticeship model to strengthen skills transfer and entrepreneurship.

On cultural preservation, she urged stakeholders to document Igbo history, the experiences of older generations, community achievements and living cultural practices to ensure they are preserved for future generations.

“If you don’t write your history, somebody else has a pen,” she said.

She also warned about the need to protect the Igbo language and strengthen its representation in digital technologies, including artificial intelligence.

“If we do not do something this generation, this beautiful language would die,” she said.

Adeola further urged researchers to look inward and incorporate indigenous knowledge into research and development, citing traditional agricultural practices and other local expertise as resources that should be documented and applied to contemporary challenges.

“Let our research be more impactful… look inward and see what you can do for your people,” she said.

The final priority, accountability, focuses on ensuring that development plans produce results that can be assessed. Adeola called for clear baselines on security, access to opportunities and commercial activities, as well as regular reporting to determine whether interventions were improving people’s lives.

She emphasised that the region needed to move from discussions to practical action, with responsibilities assigned and progress monitored.

“Conversation is necessary, but we shouldn’t just talk. We should demand safety… inclusion… accountability,” she said.

Among the early steps discussed were convening forums, agreeing on shared priorities, publishing a transparent baseline assessment and piloting a locally supported market initiative within the first 10 days. These were presented as suggested actions for consideration, not as programmes that had already been approved or commenced.

Adeola said the region’s renewal would require collective responsibility, credible institutions, evidence-based advocacy and cooperation among stakeholders.

“We need our voice. We need the evidence. We need coalitions. We need institutions,” she said
