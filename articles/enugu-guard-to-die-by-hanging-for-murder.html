Enugu guard to die by hanging for murder

By Punch Punch 5d ago 5 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

An Enugu State High Court has sentenced a security guard, Shuibu Mohammed, to death by hanging for stabbing a man, Njoku Nwabueze, to death with a dagger at Soi

Justice Kenneth Ikechukwu Okpe, in a judgment delivered on Wednesday in Charge No. E/66C/2022, convicted Mohammed of murder, contrary to Section 274(1) of the Criminal Code, Cap 30, Vol. II, Laws of Enugu State, 2004.

“I hold that the prosecution proved beyond reasonable doubt that the defendant intentionally killed the deceased, Njoku Nwabueze. Accordingly, the defendant, Shuibu Mohammed, is hereby convicted on this charge of murder,” the judge held.

He subsequently sentenced the defendant to death by hanging, adding, “May God have mercy on his soul.”

The charge stated that Mohammed murdered Nwabueze on or about October 1, 2021, at Soil Plaza, Emene, by stabbing him with a dagger.

The information was filed on April 14, 2022, by Nnaemeka Ngene, then a legal officer with the Enugu State Ministry of Justice. However, the case was prosecuted from the outset by C. N. Avah, a director in the ministry.

Following several adjournments, the defendant took his plea on April 24, 2024, while the trial commenced on July 17, 2024.

The prosecution called two police witnesses, Inspectors Amorha Nnaemeka and Chidi Opara, and tendered photographs of the deceased, the defendant’s statements to the police and a statement by the deceased’s brother, Njoku Onyekachi.

The defence called only Mohammed, who denied killing or quarrelling with the deceased.

In his judgment, Justice Okpe held that the prosecution had established the death of Nwabueze, dismissing the defence’s argument that an autopsy report was necessary to prove the cause of death.

“There are instances where the cause of death is so obvious that the requirement for a medical report to prove the offence of murder will be dispensed with,” he held, citing Oladapo v. State [2020] 7 NWLR (Pt. 1723) 238.

The judge also dismissed the defence’s objection to the evidence of the two police witnesses, who testified that they saw the deceased’s lifeless body, photographed it, observed a bleeding injury and later identified the corpse at the mortuary.

“Contrary to the submission of counsel for the defendant, these are not hearsay evidence. Rather, they are evidence of what PW1 and PW2 saw in the course of their investigation,” he held.

On the confessional statements, Justice Okpe noted that although they were recorded at different locations and on different dates, they were essentially consistent.

“At no point, not even in open court, did the defendant raise any issue on the voluntariness of his confessional statements. So, the statements were voluntarily made,” he held, adding that the confessions were direct and positive on the killing.

The court also relied on circumstantial evidence, particularly the defendant’s disappearance from his duty post after the incident and his subsequent concealment at Eke Obinagu until he was lured out.

“If the defendant did not kill the deceased and did not even fight with him, why did he go into hiding?” the judge queried.

Defence counsel, C. C. Azubuike, had argued that the court could not rely on the confessional statements because they were obtained without compliance with Section 17(1), (2), (3) and (4) of the Administration of Criminal Justice Law of Enugu State, 2017, which provides for video recording or the presence of a lawyer during the recording of confessional statements.

Counsel cited Charles v. The State of Lagos (2023) 13 NWLR (Pt. 1901) 213.

However, Justice Okpe dismissed the argument, holding that where there was no allegation of oppression or involuntariness, non-compliance with the ACJL provision would not automatically render a confession inadmissible. He relied on Iwah v. State (2026) LPELR-83365 (SC).

The judge also rejected the defence’s argument on an illiterate jurat, noting that the defendant testified in English without an interpreter. He equally dismissed the claim that the statements were unsigned, holding that they bore the defendant’s thumbprints, which he did not deny.

On the issue of intent, Justice Okpe held that the use of a dagger to stab the deceased in the heart region demonstrated that the defendant knew, or ought to have known, that death or grievous bodily harm was a probable consequence.

“The defendant intentionally pierced the deceased at his heart region and offered no help and no assistance,” he held.

Although the defendant did not raise the defences of provocation and self-defence, the court considered both on its own motion because of the capital nature of the charge.

On provocation, the judge held that even if the defendant had been provoked, “his mode of resentment was not proportionate to the provocation occasioned.”

He also rejected self-defence, noting that the deceased was stabbed in the back, indicating that he was not attacking the defendant at the time.

“The evidence showed that the deceased was no longer attacking the defendant when the defendant stabbed him, and, therefore, the defence of self-defence will also not avail the defendant,” he held.

During his allocutus, Azubuike pleaded for mercy, arguing that the defendant was the breadwinner of his family. The prosecution, however, informed the court that there was no previous record of conviction against him.

But Justice Okpe said the defendant had shown no remorse throughout the trial.

“From the commencement of this trial up to today, when the case came up for judgment, the defendant showed no remorse for his actions. He carried on as if the trial was a waste of his time,” the judge said.

He further held that the offence carried a mandatory death sentence and that the court had no discretion in imposing a lesser punishment, citing Lucky v. State (2016) LPELR-40541.

Avah appeared for the prosecution, while Azubuike represented the defendant.
