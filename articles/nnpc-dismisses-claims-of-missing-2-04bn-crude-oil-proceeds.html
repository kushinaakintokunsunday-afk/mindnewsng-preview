NNPC dismisses claims of missing $2.04bn crude oil proceeds

By Punch Punch 1h ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited has denied claims that $2.04bn in crude oil proceeds involving its trading subsidiary, Duke Oil Company Incorpor

The national oil company said the apparent gaps in dollar receipts cited in recent media reports arose from the settlement arrangement under the programme, through which the value of crude supplied was offset against the cost of refined petroleum products delivered to Nigeria.

In a statement issued on Tuesday by its Chief Corporate Communications Officer, Andy Odeh, titled ‘Duke Oil Cargoes Are Fully Accounted For’, the company said the $2.04bn represented the gross value of 29 crude cargoes and did not constitute missing or unpaid revenue.

NNPC said the absence of separate dollar payments for the cargoes was consistent with the contractual terms of the DSDP arrangement, under which crude oil was exchanged for refined products.

“NNPC Limited states categorically that no sum is missing, unaccounted for, or unpaid, in respect of crude cargoes lifted by Duke Oil Company Incorporated, now NNPC Trading S.A., under the Direct Sale Direct Purchase programme.

“No separate receipt appears because the crude sale proceeds were set off against the product purchase price, as the contracts provide,” the company stated.

It added that the reports in question acknowledged that the records pointed to reconciliation of transactions within the NNPC Group rather than any financial loss.

Under the DSDP programme, Duke Oil and other participating traders lifted Nigerian crude oil and supplied refined petroleum products, including petrol, for the domestic market.

While crude oil was priced in dollars, the petroleum products supplied were sold in naira. The value of the products delivered was subsequently reconciled against the corresponding crude oil value, with the transactions reflected in the audited financial statements of the relevant entities.

NNPC further argued that the reports had acknowledged that all 59 crude cargoes were accounted for and reconciled with their respective beneficiaries.

Providing a breakdown, the company said 30 cargoes were paid for in dollars, while proceeds from 28 cargoes, valued at approximately `$1.96bn, were remitted in naira into the NNPC Group Treasury or NNPC Upstream Investment Management Services accounts.

It said the remaining cargo, a February 2023 Bonga tax-oil lifting valued at approximately $75.9m, was paid directly into the then-designated dollar account of the Federal Inland Revenue Service.

The company maintained that the transactions were properly documented and that the absence of separate dollar receipts for some cargoes should not be interpreted as evidence of missing funds.

NNPC also defended the role of Duke Oil, following reports describing the company as NNPC operating under another name.

It said Duke Oil was incorporated in 1989 as a wholly owned trading subsidiary of NNPC, adding that its ownership had been disclosed in the national oil company’s corporate history, audited financial statements and the Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative’s 2023 industry report.

“Characterising Duke Oil as ‘NNPC wearing another name’ frames a long-disclosed fact as a revelation,” the company said.

It argued that using a dedicated trading subsidiary was standard practice in the international oil industry and that transactions between related companies were disclosed in accordance with International Financial Reporting Standards.

The latest clarification comes amid heightened public scrutiny of NNPC’s crude oil sales, domestic petroleum supply arrangements and financial remittances, particularly following the removal of petrol subsidy and the introduction of new arrangements for supplying crude to domestic refineries.

The DSDP programme was introduced to address Nigeria’s domestic fuel supply challenges by exchanging crude oil for refined petroleum products, reducing the immediate need for cash payments for imported petrol.

However, the structure has attracted questions over transparency, the accounting of crude allocations, the value of products supplied and the reconciliation of transactions involving NNPC and its trading partners.

NNPC said it remained committed to transparent financial reporting and accounting for crude oil liftings, product deliveries and payments to beneficiaries.

While acknowledging the importance of responsible journalism and public scrutiny, the company warned against what it described as false and misleading publications capable of damaging its reputation.

It said it reserved the right to pursue legal action against those responsible for such reports.
