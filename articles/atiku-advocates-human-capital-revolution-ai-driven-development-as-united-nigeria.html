Atiku advocates human capital revolution, AI-driven development as United Nigeria Airlines boss, Okonkwo hails his visionary investment in education

By The Sun Nigeria The Sun Nigeria 2d ago 10 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: The Sun Nigeria

From Billy Abel, Yola

Former Vice President Atiku Abubakar has called for a fundamental reorientation of Nigeria’s development priorities towards human capital investment, technological innovation and artificial intelligence, declaring that the country’s greatest national resource is its people, particularly its young population.

Atiku, who spoke through his representative, former governor Rotimi Amaechi, at the 18th Founders’ Day celebration of the American University of Nigeria (AUN), Yola, on Saturday, November 21, 2026, argued that Nigeria’s future would depend largely on its ability to educate, equip, employ and empower its citizens to compete in an increasingly technology-driven global economy.

The event also featured a compelling Founders’ Day lecture by Professor Obiora Okonkwo, OFR, who paid glowing tribute to Atiku’s commitment to educational development, describing him as a visionary institution builder whose investment in AUN represents a profound commitment to the transformation of higher education and human development.

In his address titled ‘The Future We Choose: Human Capital, Artificial Intelligence, and Nigeria’s Renewal’, Atiku maintained that the country’s development challenge was not necessarily a shortage of talent but its failure to consistently develop and deploy the enormous human potential available to it.

He warned that Nigeria must make a deliberate choice between transforming its growing population into a productive, skilled and confident citizenry and allowing demographic expansion to deepen unemployment, insecurity, poverty and social frustration.

According to him, the quality of the country’s human capital, rather than the abundance of its natural resources, will ultimately determine its economic competitiveness and national prosperity.

He observed that the world was entering a new technological age defined by artificial intelligence, automation, robotics, biotechnology and digital networks, technologies that would fundamentally transform virtually every sector, including agriculture, medicine, education, journalism, banking, manufacturing, law and commerce.

While acknowledging the enormous opportunities these developments present, Atiku cautioned that technology could also widen inequality if access remained restricted to a privileged few or if young Africans remained consumers of technological products rather than becoming creators, innovators and problem-solvers.

He therefore called for an education system that prepares young Nigerians not merely to use emerging technologies but to understand, improve, regulate and apply them to address the country’s peculiar developmental challenges.

He stressed the need for Nigerian solutions to Nigerian problems, identifying opportunities for technological applications in agricultural productivity, education in underserved communities, public healthcare, electricity, clean water and the expansion of small businesses.

The former Vice President also advocated a fundamental shift in the philosophy of education, arguing that academic certificates alone were insufficient to prepare graduates for the demands of the contemporary world.

He identified five essential qualities that education must develop: strong foundational knowledge, practical competence, entrepreneurial confidence, ethical judgement and a sense of responsibility to society.

He further challenged universities to evolve beyond traditional institutions of teaching and examinations into centres of innovation, research, entrepreneurship, civic responsibility and economic transformation.

Atiku particularly emphasised AUN’s responsibility to its host region, noting that its location in north-eastern Nigeria placed it in a strategic position to demonstrate that academic excellence could flourish even in challenging environments.

He urged the university to strengthen its commitment to entrepreneurship, technology, research, community engagement and leadership development, while ensuring that talented students from disadvantaged backgrounds were not denied opportunities because of financial circumstances.

He also called for a renewed national compact involving government, the private sector, the diaspora, universities and citizens to strengthen human capital development.

Government, he said, must provide the enabling environment through reliable electricity, affordable connectivity, security, transparent regulation and sustained investment in education and research.

The private sector, in turn, must invest in skills development, internships, technology transfer and local innovation, while the diaspora should be mobilised as a reservoir of knowledge, mentorship, capital and international networks.

He challenged young people to become lifelong learners, embrace technological change and measure success not merely by personal income or positions attained but by the problems they solve, the people they uplift and the institutions they strengthen.

Okonkwo celebrates Atiku’s enduring investment in education

A major highlight of the celebration was the Founders’ Day lecture delivered by Professor Obiora Okonkwo, OFR, titled ‘Purposeful Education and the 21st-Century University: Leadership, Innovation and Social Responsibility’.

The lecture provided an important intellectual complement to Atiku’s address, examining the philosophical foundations of purposeful education and the responsibilities of contemporary universities in responding to the developmental challenges of Nigeria and Africa.

However, beyond its academic arguments, Okonkwo’s address offered a particularly significant appreciation of Atiku’s role in establishing and sustaining the American University of Nigeria.

He described the former Vice President as more than an education enthusiast, characterising him as a visionary and outstanding institution builder whose commitment to AUN transcended the conventional responsibilities of a university founder.

According to Okonkwo, the conception and execution of AUN reflected Atiku’s conviction that transforming university education in this part of the world required an extraordinary level of personal commitment.

He drew attention to the university’s non-profit character, the substantial annual financial support Atiku had provided for nearly two decades, the significant proportion of students benefiting from full scholarships and the relatively affordable fees charged by an institution offering international-standard teaching and infrastructure.

He also acknowledged Atiku’s continued willingness to support major initiatives designed to consolidate AUN’s position as a leading development-oriented university in Africa.

Okonkwo described such commitment as evidence that education, particularly higher education, was a transgenerational investment in national advancement and the cultivation of a more enlightened, productive and transformed society.

He said: “Your Excellency, Sir, thank you for demonstrating, in the most practical way, that education, particularly higher education, is a transgenerational investment, not just in national advancement but also in the cultivation of a more enlightened, productive and qualitatively transformed humanity.”

The professor also celebrated Atiku’s forthcoming 80th birthday on November 25, describing his contributions to humanity as invaluable and expressing gratitude for the opportunity to witness his impact.

Education must go beyond certificates, Okonkwo insists

Moving from the commendation of AUN’s founder to the broader responsibilities of higher education, Okonkwo argued that the fundamental purpose of education must extend beyond the acquisition of academic qualifications to the development of human and social capabilities.

He maintained that a university’s relevance should not be measured exclusively by the number of certificates awarded, research publications produced or institutional prestige accumulated, but also by the capabilities it develops, the problems it helps resolve and the opportunities it creates for society.

He warned against an educational culture that prioritises qualifications without sufficiently developing the capacity of graduates to think critically, solve problems, communicate effectively, collaborate and exercise sound judgement.

According to him, purposeful education should produce individuals who can think clearly, act competently, disagree intelligently, create value and recognise responsibilities beyond personal advancement.

He further argued that the international orientation of a university should not be separated from its national and regional development responsibilities.

Using AUN as an example, he maintained that global academic standards and local developmental relevance were complementary rather than competing objectives.

He explained that international exposure should provide access to global knowledge, comparative standards and research networks, while Nigeria’s developmental realities should determine the relevance and application of such knowledge.

In his assessment, a university that pursues international prestige without addressing local realities risks becoming an institution of display, while one that ignores global intellectual engagement risks becoming academically constrained.

Okonkwo also identified leadership, innovation, research, social responsibility, graduate employability, entrepreneurship, international partnerships and institutional sustainability as essential pillars of the 21st-century university.

He called for university leadership that goes beyond routine administration to embrace institutional stewardship, strategic vision, accountability and the ability to mobilise people and resources towards meaningful objectives.

He further advocated stronger research and innovation ecosystems that connect universities with local governments, schools, healthcare institutions, businesses, civil society organisations and communities.

Such relationships, he argued, would enable universities to translate knowledge into practical solutions and ensure that development became something institutions actively participated in rather than merely discussed in classrooms.

He equally stressed the importance of institutional resilience, financial discipline, environmental sustainability, transparent governance and continuity beyond the tenure of individual leaders.

Addressing students and graduates, Okonkwo challenged them to transform their education into a platform for invention, enterprise and productive engagement.

He urged them to move beyond simply understanding society’s problems to developing the imagination, competence and determination required to design and implement solutions.

AUN announces Medical School, records nearly 1,700 students

The university’s growth, community engagement and future expansion also featured prominently in the welcome address by its President, Dr DeWayne P. Frazier.

Reflecting on the institution’s journey, Frazier disclosed that the 2026 celebration marked 23 years since the groundbreaking of AUN, while acknowledging Atiku’s 80th birthday, which falls on November 25.

He said the university was established around a vision that education should be measured not merely by what students learn in classrooms but by the impact of that learning on their lives and the communities around them.

Frazier highlighted the importance of faculty mentorship, extracurricular activities, student leadership and community engagement as essential components of the university’s educational philosophy.

He particularly referred to AUN’s Feed and Read initiative, through which children in Yola receive meals and opportunities to develop their reading and basic mathematics skills with the assistance of student volunteers.

He disclosed that some of the Almajiri boys participating in the programme would receive full scholarships to study at AUN after completing their junior and senior secondary education, subject to the university’s selection arrangements.

One of the major announcements of the occasion was the appointment of Dr Abdullahi Alkali Abba as the founding Dean of AUN’s Medical School.

Frazier described the proposed medical school as an important opportunity to integrate teaching, research and community service around the healthcare needs of north-eastern Nigeria.

He explained that the initiative would enable the university to deepen research into infectious diseases, improve understanding of community health challenges and prepare future medical professionals to respond more effectively to the circumstances and needs of their patients.

The AUN President also announced that the university had attained a record enrolment of nearly 1,700 students, drawn from all 36 states of Nigeria and more than 20 countries.

He said the growing diversity of the student population reflected the founding vision of building an institution that was accessible to people from different backgrounds and nationalities.

Frazier expressed optimism that AUN’s greatest achievements were still ahead, pledging to sustain its commitment to academic excellence, community development and the preparation of graduates capable of making meaningful contributions to society.

A shared vision for Nigeria’s future

Taken together, the three addresses at the 18th Founders’ Day celebration presented a compelling argument about the relationship between education, leadership, innovation and national development.

While Atiku’s address placed human capital and technological preparedness at the centre of Nigeria’s national renewal, Okonkwo’s lecture examined the institutional and intellectual foundations required to translate education into meaningful social and economic progress.

Frazier’s address, meanwhile, demonstrated how AUN’s founding vision was being translated into practical initiatives through community engagement, student development, institutional expansion and the proposed establishment of a medical school.

The convergence of these perspectives reinforced the underlying philosophy of AUN as a Development University, an institution established to combine international academic standards with the practical demands of Nigeria’s and Africa’s development.

Twenty-three years after its groundbreaking, the university’s 18th Founders’ Day offered an opportunity not only to celebrate its history but also to renew the responsibilities associated with its founding vision.

For Atiku, the future Nigeria chooses will ultimately depend on the quality of its investment in people. For Okonkwo, the university must justify its existence through the knowledge it produces, the capabilities it develops and the problems it helps solve.

And for AUN, the challenge remains to turn that shared vision into an enduring institutional legacy.
