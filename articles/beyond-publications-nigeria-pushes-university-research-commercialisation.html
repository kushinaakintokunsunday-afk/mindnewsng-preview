Beyond publications, Nigeria pushes university research commercialisation

Nigeria’s universities are rich in research and innovation, but can fresh government funding, commercialisation initiatives and industry partnerships turn academic discoveries into market-ready products, jobs and solutions to national challenges, writes DEBORAH TOLU-KOLAWOLE

From medical technologies and agricultural innovations to artificial intelligence, engineering, and renewable energy, researchers across Nigerian institutions continue to explore solutions to the country’s challenges.

Yet, many of these discoveries struggle to move beyond academic publications, exhibitions and research competitions into commercial products capable of attracting investment, creating employment and improving people’s lives.

The gap between research and commercialisation has remained one of the longstanding weaknesses of Nigeria’s higher education and innovation ecosystem, raising questions about the ability of universities to translate intellectual capital into economic value.

Recognising the issue, the Federal Ministry of Education has renewed efforts to promote research commercialisation, innovation funding and stronger collaboration between tertiary institutions and industry.

In January 2026, Alausa inaugurated the Steering Committee and Policy Drafting Committee for the National Research-to-Commercialisation Policy, designed to establish a structured pathway for converting research conducted in universities, research institutes and specialised agencies into commercially viable products and services.

The initiative was informed by concerns that substantial investments in research had not translated into corresponding industrial and economic benefits.

According to the minister, too many research outcomes remained on university shelves instead of becoming solutions to national problems.

The proposed framework is expected to institutionalise collaboration among government, universities, industries and financing institutions, while strengthening incentives for innovation, entrepreneurship and intellectual property protection.

Alausa announced a N2bn provision in the 2026 budget to support the work of the Research, Innovation and Commercialisation Committee, alongside a N3bn operational budget for its activities. The government also announced a N50m Student Grant Venture Capital Initiative to support student-led enterprises in tertiary institutions, as well as institutional innovation grants for universities and research centres seeking to develop market-ready solutions.

The financial commitments are significant because research commercialisation often requires a different funding structure from conventional academic research.

While traditional research grants may support literature reviews, data collection, laboratory experiments and publication, commercialisation requires additional resources for product development, testing, intellectual property registration, regulatory compliance, manufacturing and market entry.

A researcher who develops a promising medical device, for instance, may need access to specialised laboratories, clinical testing facilities, engineering support and regulatory expertise before the technology can be deployed in hospitals.

Similarly, an agricultural innovation may require field trials, production facilities, distribution networks and partnerships with farmers and agro-processing companies before it can become commercially sustainable.

Without these additional investments, promising research may remain confined to prototypes, irrespective of its potential value to society.

Speaking on the issue, the Vice-Chancellor of Ahmadu Bello University, Prof Adamu Ahmed, said universities were increasingly being assessed not only by their academic publications but also by their ability to apply research, commercialise discoveries and translate knowledge into measurable impact.

“Our research must begin to generate income for our researchers, for the university, and for the wider society,” Ahmed said.

He described commercialisation as “not a distraction from scholarship but a higher expression of it”, arguing that universities needed practical frameworks to move their research patents and outputs into the marketplace.

Ahmed also identified what he called the “valley of death” – the difficult gap between invention and adoption – as a major challenge the university’s committee was expected to address.

A similar position was expressed by the then Deputy Vice-Chancellor (Administration) of the University of Ibadan, now Vice-Chancellor, Prof Peter Olapegba, in May 2026, when he urged Nigerian universities to prioritise entrepreneurship-driven research capable of solving societal problems and boosting the economy.

Olapegba, who spoke at a TETFund capacity-building workshop for early-career researchers, said research findings should address problems in host communities, while patented university innovations should move from laboratories to local markets.

He encouraged researchers to design projects around societal and industrial needs, develop intellectual property literacy and integrate entrepreneurship into teaching and learning.

Olapegba clarified that an entrepreneurial mindset did not mean every professor should become a trader. Rather, he urged researchers to see “the laboratory as a prototype factory, the department as an innovation hub, and the students as potential employers of labour and not just job seekers.”

At Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, a similar conversation took place in June 2026, when professors, research leaders and industry practitioners examined how academic work could be connected more directly to industry and national development.

Niran Oluwaranti, a Professor of Computer Science and Engineering and Director of Partnerships at the university, said the traditional emphasis on publications and academic points needed to give greater consideration to whether research was being adopted.

Oluwaranti said, “It should no longer be only about publishing papers and accumulating points. The question should increasingly become: who is adopting your research, and what value is it creating?”

He added that rigorous research did not automatically translate into usable products, stressing the need for continued engagement between universities and industry.

Christianah Elusiyan, a Professor of Natural Product Chemistry and Senior Research Fellow at OAU, also called for a change in the traditional orientation of academic work.

“The traditional orientation has largely been research, publication, citations and recognition,” she said. “This is a fundamental shift to let our research impact society, which is where it is needed. We need that reorientation.”

The university’s Deputy Vice-Chancellor (Research, Innovation and Development), Prof Akanni Akinyemi, said researchers should increasingly be challenged to explain how their work improved lives, solved problems and created value beyond the university.

“The future of universities will depend not only on rigour, but on their ability to connect knowledge to impact,” he said. “Research becomes more powerful when the marketplace, policymakers and society can actually use it.”

An industry practitioner, Akin Monehin, who addressed the OAU researchers, similarly warned that researchers should not focus only on publications, awards and citations.

“Organisations are struggling with problems whose solutions may already exist somewhere on your shelf,” he said, arguing that the challenge was often connecting existing knowledge with the people and organisations that needed it.

Recent initiatives announced by the Minister of Education, Dr Tunji Alausa, suggest an attempt to change the relationship between academic research and national development.

One of the government’s strategies is the Research, Innovation and Commercialisation Committee, established by the Federal Ministry of Education in collaboration with the Tertiary Education Trust Fund.

The committee is intended to promote the uptake of research outputs, encourage collaboration between universities and private-sector organisations, and create pathways for moving discoveries from laboratories into the marketplace.

Its membership includes representatives of the Nigerian Economic Summit Group, Bank of Industry, Manufacturers Association of Nigeria, vice-chancellors and TETFund.

The approach is designed to bring together institutions with different but complementary capabilities. The government is also exploring partnerships with the National Agency for Science and Engineering Infrastructure to strengthen the link between universities and industrial production.

In August 2026, Alausa urged NASENI to establish stronger partnerships with universities through its proposed NASENI Campus, which is expected to connect researchers to advanced laboratories, prototyping facilities, industries and investment opportunities.

The minister said NASENI could help bridge the gap between academic research and commercial success by supporting patenting, connecting researchers with venture capital and facilitating the creation of spin-off companies.

NASENI Chief Executive Officer, Khalil Suleiman, said the proposed campus, located within a 100-hectare free trade zone, was being developed as an integrated environment where researchers, professors, prototyping facilities, industries and commercialisation opportunities could operate together.

The Vice-Chancellor of Bayero University, Kano, Prof Haruna Musa, also disclosed that two research projects from the institution had reached the final stage of NASENI’s research commercialisation grant process.

They included a reusable sanitary pad project and the commercial production of sweet potato-based starch derivatives.

The projects illustrate the potential of university research to address practical needs in healthcare and agriculture, although their eventual success will depend on further development, financing, production and market adoption.

Another component of the government’s strategy is the introduction of the Tertiary Institutions National Laureate Prize, an annual N365m initiative announced by Alausa in August.

The award is designed to recognise outstanding undergraduate dissertations, master’s theses and doctoral research with commercial potential or clear industrial and developmental applications.

It covers six thematic areas: Engineering and Technology; Medicine and Health Sciences; Arts and Social Sciences; Agriculture; Law; and Teaching Innovation.

The government also announced the Dr Stella Adadevoh Prize for Medicine and Medical Innovation in honour of the late Nigerian physician who played a significant role in the country’s response to the 2014 Ebola outbreak.

Alausa said the prize was intended to establish a national platform for recognising academic excellence and encouraging young researchers to develop solutions to Nigeria’s challenges.

“Scholarship matters. Research matters. Innovation matters. Intellectual excellence deserves a prominent place in our national consciousness,” he said during the unveiling.

The minister added that the initiative was not simply about celebrating academic achievement but encouraging research that could translate into commercial ventures, industrial applications and practical solutions.

“We want Nigerian students to understand that there is honour in scholarship, opportunity in innovation and national recognition for intellectual excellence,” he said.

The selection process is expected to begin at the institutional level before progressing to regional and national assessments, with professional bodies and experts participating in the evaluation.

The prize is also expected to work with the Nigeria Education Repository and Databank, NERD, to strengthen the documentation, verification, preservation and accessibility of academic research.

The National Laureate Prize could encourage students to approach academic research differently, particularly if the selection process rewards originality, practical application and the potential to address identifiable societal problems.

The Nigeria Education Repository and Databank is another initiative expected to support the government’s research and innovation agenda.

Although primarily established as a national digital repository for academic records and research outputs, the platform could become an important part of the infrastructure required to identify, preserve and make Nigerian intellectual work accessible.

Its contribution, however, will depend on how effectively research records are connected to commercialisation institutions, intellectual property services and potential investors. A repository is not itself a commercialisation mechanism, and making research available does not automatically provide the financing, technical expertise or industrial partnerships needed to turn it into a product.

The government will also need to ensure that researchers’ intellectual property rights are protected when their work is made accessible through the platform.

The government’s commercialisation ambition is also receiving support through the University Innovation Pods (UniPods, being implemented in partnership with the United Nations Development Programme and TETFund.

The national rollout was flagged off on April 7, 2026, with the launch of an artificial intelligence-focused UniPod at the University of Lagos.

The first cohort, backed by more than N30bn in public investment, spans six geopolitical zones. The specialised facilities include agriculture and food systems at Benue State University; resilience and recovery at the University of Maiduguri; manufacturing and trade at Michael Okpara University of Agriculture; green and blue economy at the University of Uyo; mining technology and resource innovation at Nasarawa State University; and industrial and government technology at Ahmadu Bello University.

The facilities are intended to provide spaces where students, researchers, engineers, entrepreneurs and industry practitioners can work together to develop practical solutions.

Speaking at the launch, TETFund Executive Secretary, Sonny Echono, said, “These innovation Pods/Hubs are not just buildings, but they are collision spaces where scientists and innovators meet the engineers, where students meet the mentors, and where the raw idea meets the industrial transformation.”

UNDP Nigeria Resident Representative, Elsie Attafuah, said, “This is not about innovation hubs – it is about building a system.”

She added that Nigeria needed to move from fragmented interventions to coordinated, investment-backed platforms capable of supporting enterprise, jobs and long-term economic transformation.

The partnership has also set specific targets. Under a memorandum of understanding signed by TETFund and UNDP in December 2025, the two institutions aim to activate eight University Innovation Pods and one Polytechnic Pod, upgrade more than nine additional innovation facilities, establish or strengthen between 15 and 20 technology transfer offices, equip more than 500,000 students and researchers with digital and innovation skills, support between 1,500 and 2,000 university-linked start-ups, and commercialise 5,000 research outputs. These are programme targets, not completed achievements.

The facilities could be particularly important in addressing infrastructure limitations facing researchers in many Nigerian universities.

Access to specialised equipment, prototyping facilities, testing laboratories and technical mentors can determine whether an academic idea progresses beyond the experimental stage.

For instance, a researcher developing an agricultural processing machine may require access to engineering equipment to refine the prototype, while a student working on an artificial intelligence application may need computing infrastructure, technical mentorship and opportunities to test the product with potential users.

The UniPods could provide some of these opportunities within university environments.

Nevertheless, the government and its development partners will need to demonstrate that the facilities are adequately equipped, maintained and accessible to researchers beyond their immediate host institutions.

Their effectiveness should also be measured by the number of viable products developed, start-ups established, patents registered, industry partnerships secured and innovations adopted by consumers.

One of the issues that could determine the success of the government’s research agenda is intellectual property protection.

Researchers may develop valuable technologies but lack the legal and commercial knowledge required to protect their inventions or negotiate agreements with companies interested in using them.

In some cases, uncertainty over ownership can discourage researchers from collaborating with private-sector organisations or sharing their discoveries with potential investors.

Recognising this challenge, the Federal Government has also intensified engagement with the World Intellectual Property Organisation to strengthen Nigeria’s intellectual property ecosystem.

In June 2026, Vice-President Kashim Shettima met with a WIPO delegation led by its Director-General, Daren Tang, to discuss ways of converting Nigeria’s intellectual property into financial assets.

The engagement followed the Federal Executive Council’s approval of the National Intellectual Property Policy and Strategy in November 2025.

Shettima said Nigeria needed an intellectual property system that served both inventors and investors, researchers and entrepreneurs.

He stated, “The countries that will lead this century are those able to create, protect, commercialise, and scale intellectual assets.”

For the education sector, the implications are considerable.

Universities will need functional technology transfer offices, intellectual property specialists and clear guidelines governing the ownership and sharing of revenue generated from discoveries developed with public funding.

Researchers must also understand the difference between publishing their findings and protecting inventions that could have commercial value.

Without such safeguards, promising discoveries could be commercialised without adequate benefits accruing to their original developers or institutions.

Despite the various initiatives, the challenge of converting Nigerian research into commercial products remains considerable.

One major issue is the sustainability of funding.

Research commercialisation can take several years, particularly in sectors such as medicine, biotechnology, engineering and agriculture, where products may require extensive testing and regulatory approval.

Consequently, annual grants and research prizes may not be sufficient to support innovations through every stage of development.

The government will need to establish predictable financing arrangements that accommodate different stages of innovation, from initial research and prototyping to product testing, manufacturing and market expansion.

The N50m Student Grant Venture Capital Initiative, for example, could support early-stage student businesses, but its impact will depend on the number of beneficiaries, the size of individual grants and whether successful ventures can access further investment.

The N2bn and N3bn allocations announced for research commercialisation will also require transparent reporting to establish how much has actually been released, how it has been spent and what results have been achieved.

Another concern is the relationship between universities and private-sector organisations.

While Nigerian universities have researchers and intellectual resources, companies may be reluctant to invest in technologies that have not been sufficiently tested or whose commercial prospects remain uncertain.

This makes the roles of institutions such as NASENI, TETFund, the Bank of Industry and the Research, Innovation and Commercialisation Committee particularly important.

They could help reduce the risks associated with early-stage innovations by providing technical support, facilitating partnerships and connecting researchers with investors.

The Federal Government will also need to ensure that commercialisation does not undermine the academic responsibilities of universities or restrict access to research findings that have important public health, agricultural or social benefits.

The objective should be to establish a system in which commercial interests and public welfare can coexist.

One development drawing attention to this renewed push is the emergence of GenScan AI, an innovation by Nigerian researcher Mary-Brenda Akoda, which won the 2026 Nigeria Prize for Science and Innovation organised by Nigeria LNG.

The emergence of GenScan AI provides a useful starting point for understanding the opportunities and challenges confronting Nigeria’s research ecosystem.

Akoda’s innovation was selected as the winner of the 2026 Nigeria Prize for Science and Innovation after competing against 236 other entries.

GenScan AI’s recognition raises an important question about the future of locally developed technologies: how can Nigeria ensure that promising innovations do not end with awards and public recognition but progress into products and services that can be deployed at scale?

Meanwhile, it is worth pointing out that GenScan AI was not originally developed at a Nigerian university. Akoda’s research originated at Imperial College London, where she completed postgraduate research in artificial intelligence and machine learning. She disclosed that the work received support from Innovate UK and UK Research and Innovation, as well as the NHS Clinical Entrepreneur Programme.

Its recognition in Nigeria therefore demonstrates the potential of Nigerian innovators and the value of international research collaboration, rather than constituting evidence that the technology emerged from the country’s university research system.

At the public presentation of the prize, Akoda explained that the technology, known as GenScan AI, uses artificial intelligence to reconstruct diagnostic-quality magnetic resonance imaging pictures from a fraction of the data normally required.

She said it could reduce a 20-minute scan to about two minutes, while an hour-long scan could be completed in approximately six minutes, without requiring hospitals to purchase new scanners.

According to her, the technology was developed after a personal experience involving an elderly relative who suffered a stroke but encountered delays in accessing an MRI scan.

Akoda said the system had been evaluated using thousands of MRI images and that a blinded assessment by a consultant radiologist at Buckinghamshire Healthcare NHS Trust in the United Kingdom found the images diagnostically equivalent to conventional scans in the cases assessed.

For Nigeria’s research commercialisation agenda, the recognition of GenScan AI represents an opportunity to demonstrate what can happen when scientific discoveries receive institutional backing and access to international research and innovation networks.

However, the circumstances surrounding the technology also offer an important lesson. Although GenScan AI won Nigeria’s 2026 science prize, its research originated at Imperial College London and received support from UK research and innovation institutions. The experience illustrates the importance of sustained research support, international collaboration and access to specialised expertise.

It also shows why Nigeria’s universities need stronger connections with international research institutions, technology companies, healthcare providers and investors.

For GenScan AI, the immediate task will be to translate the recognition and government support into wider clinical validation, appropriate regulatory processes, investment and eventual deployment.

Ultimately, the success of Alausa’s research agenda will depend on whether the government can build an ecosystem that supports researchers long after their discoveries have attracted public attention.

The number of research papers published, prizes awarded, innovation hubs established and grants announced will provide useful indicators of activity, but they will not, on their own, demonstrate commercial success.

More meaningful measures will include the number of patents secured, products developed, technologies adopted by industries, start-ups established, private investments attracted, jobs created and public services improved through locally developed innovations.

Nigeria has considerable intellectual resources across its universities, polytechnics and research institutions. What has often been missing is a dependable system for connecting those resources with finance, industry, technology and markets.

From the National Laureate Prize and NERD to the Research, Innovation and Commercialisation Committee, NASENI partnerships and University Innovation Pods, the Federal Government is attempting to establish that connection.

But the real test will be whether discoveries such as GenScan AI can become the beginning of a sustained pipeline of Nigerian innovations that move from research laboratories into hospitals, factories, farms, businesses and other areas of everyday life.

If the initiatives are adequately funded, transparently implemented and supported by industry, Nigeria’s universities could become more than centres for producing graduates and academic publications. They could become important sources of technology, enterprise, employment and solutions to national development challenges.
