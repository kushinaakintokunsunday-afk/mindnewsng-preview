Cultism: A growing threat to lives

By Punch Punch 2d ago 7 min read

CULTISM has become a deadly menace to public safety in Nigeria. Its alarming resurgence, geographical spread and increasingly violent character are compounding the country’s security crisis.

From university campuses to neighbourhoods, markets and rural communities, murderous gangs are holding innocent citizens hostage. Government at all levels must stop treating this menace as youthful delinquency and confront it for what it has become: a serious threat to lives and social stability.

What began decades ago as campus-based confraternities, ostensibly established to promote intellectual development, solidarity and resistance against oppression, has degenerated into criminal networks responsible for killings, intimidation, extortion and other violent crimes. The transformation is as tragic as it is dangerous.

The growing spread of these groups beyond university campuses into secondary schools, neighbourhoods and rural communities demands urgent and coordinated intervention.

Recent incidents across the country paint a disturbing picture of the scale of the problem. Last Saturday, two people were killed and several others injured during a clash between suspected rival cult groups at a masquerade procession in the Okepopo area of Lagos Island.

The violence, reportedly triggered by an attack on a rival member, escalated into a reprisal that claimed another life and sent residents and business owners fleeing for safety.

Last Friday, a yet-to-be-identified barber was hacked to death by suspected cultists, who reportedly severed his manhood, around White House Bus Stop, Okokomaiko, Lagos.

 In Ogbomoso, Oyo State, police arrested 10 suspects following a violent clash between rival cult factions reportedly fighting over the sharing of money gifts. The recovery of large quantities of illicit drugs during the operation further underscores the disturbing links between cultism, substance abuse and other criminal activities.

Similarly, the killing of a young man in the Issele-Asagba area of Delta State, captured in a disturbing viral video, prompted police investigations and arrests.

On November 13, the Rivers State Police Command arrested two suspected cultists over the murder of a commercial motorcycle rider, Sani Musa, whose body was recovered from a forest in Ahoada West Local Government Area.

Osun and Imo states have also witnessed deadly confrontations involving suspected cult groups.

In Ogun State, cult-related activities remain entrenched in major towns, including Abeokuta, Ijebu-Ode, Ota and Sagamu. The police are, however, intensifying efforts against the notorious 7/7 cult gang, which has terrorised schoolchildren and other residents for years.

These are not isolated criminal disturbances. They point to a widening pattern of organised violence that threatens public safety, disrupts economic activities and undermines the stability of communities.

The statistics are equally alarming. The Nigeria Police Force’s performance report for September and October 2026 documented 73 cultism clashes nationwide within eight weeks. The Eons Intelligence Security Index also recorded 59 cult-related incidents in October, a 52 per cent increase over the previous month.

The death toll is grimmer still. SBM Intelligence tracked 1,686 deaths in 909 incidents between January 2020 and March 2025. Citing Nigeria Watch, the European Union Agency for Asylum reported 371 cult-related deaths in 2023 and 328 in 2024. Naija Confra, meanwhile, reported 581 fatalities from cult wars in 2024.

Behind these figures are hundreds of lives cut short, families shattered, and communities traumatised.

Worse, innocent citizens increasingly pay the price for the bloody rivalries of criminal gangs.

Traders, commuters, artisans, students and other ordinary residents face the danger of stray bullets, machete attacks and sudden outbreaks of violence. Markets and residential streets that should be safe spaces for economic and social activities have become potential battlegrounds.

It is particularly disturbing that cultural and religious gatherings, which should promote communal harmony, are increasingly being exploited by criminal elements to settle scores. When masquerade processions and other public events become theatres of bloodshed, the threat has clearly gone beyond the control of individual communities.

The roots of this menace must also be confronted. Youth unemployment, poverty, peer pressure, weak parental supervision and substance abuse have created fertile ground for recruitment.

Equally culpable is the apparent inability of security institutions to dismantle entrenched cult networks and bring their financiers and masterminds to justice.

Perhaps most troubling is the role of politicians who recruit, finance or deploy violent groups for electoral intimidation and other partisan interests.

Such relationships provide criminal elements with money, weapons and, in some instances, protection from prosecution.

It is sheer hypocrisy for political actors to publicly condemn cultism while privately cultivating the very networks that sustain it. Those who arm and sponsor these gangs must be regarded as accomplices in the violence they unleash.

The education sector must confront its failure to prevent the spread of cultism within its institutions. Universities and secondary schools must not become recruitment grounds for violent confraternities.

School authorities should strengthen security arrangements, establish functional counselling services and work closely with parents and law enforcement agencies to identify vulnerable students before they are drawn into criminal networks.

Student unionism, sports and legitimate extracurricular activities should be encouraged as alternative avenues for young people to develop leadership skills, build friendships and find a sense of belonging without resorting to violent associations.

Parents and guardians also have a critical responsibility. They must pay closer attention to their children’s associations, behavioural changes and unexplained sources of income.

Traditional rulers, community leaders and faith-based organisations should intensify efforts to discourage young people from embracing violence as a route to social recognition, protection or financial reward.

But moral appeals and public condemnations alone will not defeat organised criminality. The police and other security agencies must move beyond the familiar pattern of reacting to killings with arrests after the damage has been done.

Intelligence gathering must be strengthened to identify and dismantle cult networks, including their leaders, financiers, weapons suppliers and recruitment channels.

Arresting a handful of suspected members after every bloody confrontation, while the masterminds and sponsors remain untouched, is little more than a cosmetic response. It allows the networks to survive, regroup and strike again.

The real test of effective policing is not the number of suspects paraded before the cameras but the ability to dismantle these organisations and prevent further killings.

The prosecution of arrested suspects must also be pursued diligently. It is unacceptable for individuals apprehended with firearms and other dangerous weapons to return to their communities and continue terrorising residents.

State governments must ensure that anti-cultism laws are effectively enforced, while the judiciary should expedite the prosecution of cases involving violent crimes without compromising due process.

Governments must, at the same time, address the socioeconomic conditions that make vulnerable young people susceptible to recruitment. Job creation, vocational training, access to quality education and credible youth empowerment programmes are indispensable to a sustainable response.

However, such interventions must be properly targeted and implemented, rather than reduced to political slogans and poorly monitored spending.

With political activities gathering momentum ahead of the 2027 general elections, the danger of politicians mobilising cult groups for electoral violence cannot be ignored.

Security agencies must closely monitor this threat and investigate and prosecute anyone found financing or deploying criminal gangs, irrespective of political affiliation, influence or social standing. No politician should be above the law.

Ultimately, the protection of lives and property is a fundamental responsibility of government. Nigerians should not have to negotiate their safety with criminal gangs or abandon their homes and businesses whenever rival factions decide to settle scores.

Cultism has outgrown the campus indiscipline and youthful excesses with which it was once associated. It is now an organised security threat that demands sustained intelligence-led policing, uncompromising prosecution and political will.

Governments must act decisively before more communities are consumed by the violence. The right to life and the freedom to live, work and conduct legitimate business without fear must not be surrendered to cult gangs.
