Giving traditional pottery new purpose

By Punch Punch 16 Nov 2026 4 min read

For generations, clay has been part of everyday African life. Shaped into cooking pots, water vessels, storage containers and objects that carry the knowledge and craftsmanship of communities across generations. For many of us growing up in African homes, clay was never just mud shaped into a pot; it was part of the rhythm of everyday life. We remember our grandmothers’ clay water pots, holding cool, refreshing water that seemed to taste better simply because it had rested in the earth’s embrace. There was something familiar about its earthy taste, the coolness against a parched throat, and the feeling of finally being home. Beyond these familiar comforts, clay also served practical purposes. In some traditional water-settling practices, water was left undisturbed so suspended particles could settle, making the clearer water above easier to draw off. These memories remind us that long before modern materials and technologies became widespread, our communities had developed everyday ways of working with the natural properties of clay. Today, that inherited knowledge offers more than nostalgia; it invites us to rediscover what this humble material can contribute to modern living.

Clay was also a faithful companion in our grandmothers’ kitchens. It was there when ewedu soup was stirred to its silky perfection, when rich stews simmered slowly, and when the aroma of a home-cooked meal filled the air. There was something special about food prepared and served in traditional clay pots, the earthy character of the vessel, the deep flavours of the soup, and the warmth that seemed to linger long after the fire had gone out. Even minutes after a pot had been taken off the fire, its heat remained, gently holding the meal warm as family members gathered to eat. Our grandmothers understood the value of materials that worked with the rhythms of everyday life. Today, those memories invite us to look at clay again, not merely as a relic of the past, but as a material whose practical value and cultural significance still deserve a place in our homes. But what happens when this age-old material is brought into conversations about today’s most pressing challenges?

For a Nigerian creative enterprise, The Lightning Artz, the answer is to rethink what traditional pottery can do. The Founder believes creativity should go beyond aesthetics to solve real-world problems, combining indigenous ceramic knowledge with modern innovation to develop practical products for healthier living and a more sustainable future. Its work reflects a simple but powerful idea: materials and knowledge passed down through generations can still help address present-day needs.

One of its clearest expressions is AquaMax, an electricity-free ceramic water-purification and dispensing solution that helps households access safer drinking water. By applying their expertise in clay, enhanced by technology to create a locally workable product, the product offers a solution for households seeking practical ways to treat drinking water, particularly where reliable access to safe water remains a challenge. For the founder, Obanijesu Francis, the work is personal. Growing up in an underserved community in Ifo Local Government Area of Ogun State, he experienced the difficulties of accessing safe drinking water. He and his siblings travelled long distances to fetch water, and the family experienced recurring bouts of typhoid that disrupted their schooling and daily lives.

Those experiences helped shape his conviction that creativity should not be reserved for decoration, luxury or the interests of the privileged. It can also be used to respond to needs that affect people’s health, dignity and quality of life. These possibilities are central to their mission.

By developing products locally and building pathways to reach underserved households, the enterprise connects creative innovation with practical community needs. Its approach suggests that cultural heritage and social progress need not exist in separate spaces; they can strengthen one another.
