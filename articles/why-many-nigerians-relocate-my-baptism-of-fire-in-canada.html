Why many Nigerians relocate: My baptism of fire in Canada

My baptism of fire as President of the Association of Nigerians in Nova Scotia, Canada, came in April 2024, the same week I assumed office. Some Nigerian students at Cape Breton University in Sydney, Nova Scotia, were travelling from Halifax to Sydney when their vehicle was involved in a serious accident. The journey takes over four hours by road. Some students live in Halifax because it offers more employment opportunities, and they arrange group transportation to attend classes and examinations in Sydney.

It was examination time. Their vehicle was 15 minutes away from Sydney, and daybreak was still a while away. Then their vehicle collided with an oncoming vehicle. Some of the occupants sustained minor injuries, but one Nigerian student was critically injured. Within a short time, an air ambulance had arrived to evacuate him for urgent medical attention.

As officials of the Nigerian association, we swung into action to provide whatever assistance we could. His church was making efforts to help, while his university was also involved. I was particularly worried. Having just assumed office, I was praying that our tenure would not begin with bereavement within our community.

As healthcare professionals battled to save his life, Cape Breton University facilitated arrangements to bring his wife and children from Nigeria to be with him. When they arrived in Halifax, we went to the airport to receive them at about 2am and drive them to their accommodation, thanks to the commitment of our fantastic brother, Oloruntoyin Balogun.

Eventually, our compatriot was removed from the danger list. We heaved a sigh of relief and thanked God for his survival and the swift emergency response in Nova Scotia.

Why am I telling this story? None of the students involved was a Canadian citizen. The critically injured student was neither a permanent resident nor someone with political connections in Canada. He was an international student carrying a Nigerian passport. Yet, when his life was threatened, the emergency services responded without making his nationality or immigration status the issue.

That experience has remained with me. That is not to say that emergency services in Canada don’t have shortcomings, which include ambulance delays and overstretched hospitals. But there is an established system in charge of responding to people in danger, regardless of their nationality. The student did not need to know a governor, senator or minister before an air ambulance came to his rescue.

Whenever I remember that incident and compare it with what happens in Nigeria, I feel sad. Recall what happened to Nollywood actor Junior Pope Odonwodo on April 10, 2024, the same period as this case. He and four others died after a boat accident during a film production on the River Niger. The subsequent investigation by the Nigeria Safety Investigation Bureau revealed that the boat operator was uncertified, the vessel was unregistered, and the rescue operation was not timely because trained divers were unavailable. Junior Pope’s story was actually the incident that made me draft this piece in April 2024 and keep it in the cooler since then.

Junior Pope was a popular actor, yet that could not protect him from a system in which basic safety precautions were neglected. Imagine the chances of an ordinary Nigerian whose name nobody recognises. We may never know whether faster intervention would have saved him or any of the other victims. What we know is that the arrangements that should have reduced the risk and improved their chances of survival were inadequate.

Then came the December 29, 2025 accident involving former world heavyweight boxing champion Anthony Joshua on the Lagos-Ibadan Expressway. Joshua survived with minor injuries, but two of his close friends and team members, Sina Ghami and Latif Ayodele, died when their vehicle collided with a stationary truck. Joshua received medical attention and was discharged two days later. The accident again exposed the dangers on Nigerian roads, where stationary heavy vehicles, reckless driving and weak enforcement continue to claim lives.

In 2024 alone, the Federal Road Safety Corps recorded 5,421 deaths in 9,570 road crashes across Nigeria. These were not merely figures for an annual report. They represented fathers, mothers, children and breadwinners whose absence changed families permanently. Yet, how many of our major highways have emergency medical services capable of reaching critically injured passengers quickly and transporting them to properly equipped trauma centres?

The same disregard for human life is evident in our response to insecurity. On Christmas Eve in 2023, armed attackers descended on communities in Bokkos and Barkin Ladi local government areas of Plateau State. According to Amnesty International, more than 190 people were killed, while the attackers moved between villages for over 48 hours. Amnesty questioned the failure of security agencies to halt the killings even after the attacks had continued for hours.

Imagine that. Human beings were being slaughtered, houses were burning, and families were fleeing for their lives while the violence continued from one community to another. Similar questions have followed attacks by Boko Haram, bandits and other armed groups across northern Nigeria. In some communities, residents have learnt that surviving an attack may depend more on their ability to escape than on the timely arrival of security personnel.

We cannot claim that Nigeria lacks courageous doctors, nurses, emergency workers or security officers. Many work under appalling conditions and risk their lives to save others. The problem is that individual courage cannot compensate for an unreliable system. An ambulance without equipment, a hospital without essential supplies, or a security post without the means to respond quickly offers little reassurance to someone facing death.

Life abroad is not all rosy. No sane person who enjoys the warmth, familiar food, communal relationships and sunshine of Nigeria would ordinarily be eager to exchange them for the freezing winters and often lonely lifestyle of Canada, the United States, Britain or Australia. The decision is even more painful for someone who has built a successful career and strong relationships in Nigeria.

Yet many Nigerians make that decision because they want assurance that their lives and their families’ lives will matter in an emergency. They want to know that when they dial an emergency number, somebody will respond. They want their children to attend school without the fear of abduction and their relatives to travel without wondering whether help will arrive if disaster strikes.

Nigeria has the resources and human talent to build such a society. What has been missing is political commitment, reinforced by a culture in which public office is treated as an opportunity for personal enrichment. Worse still, many citizens defend the politicians responsible for these failures because they share the same ethnic identity, religion or political party. Ironically, many of such defenders live in countries where politicians perform much better than their Nigerian counterparts but are still vilified for issues that will be considered inconsequential in Nigeria.

I will always remember that Nigerian student whose life was saved in Nova Scotia. He had no Canadian passport, no permanent residence and no political influence. Yet, when his life hung in the balance, the system responded. Until Nigeria learns to treat the life of an unknown citizen with the same urgency it accords the security and comfort of its political leaders, Nigerians will continue to seek safety and dignity elsewhere.
