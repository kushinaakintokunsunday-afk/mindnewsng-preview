Why goods remain expensive despite falling inflation — Oyedele

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Minister of Finance and Coordinating Minister of the Economy, Taiwo Oyedele, on Tuesday, warned Nigerians against interpreting falling inflation as a reduct

Oyedele said the continued high cost of essential commodities, including bread and milk, had fuelled public doubts about declining inflation figures, highlighting the need for better communication of the government’s economic reforms.

He explained that falling inflation did not mean prices of goods and services were falling, but that they were rising more slowly.

The minister spoke at the Global Alliance World Public Relations Forum in Abuja.

Using the latest inflation figures as an example, the minister said Nigeria’s inflation rate for October 2026 stood at 14.06 per cent, its lowest level in six years, describing the development as a direct reflection of the reforms introduced by the administration of President Bola Tinubu.

He said, “The confusion is understandable, and we must own our part of it. Falling inflation does not mean falling prices. It means prices are rising more slowly than before.”

Continuing, Oyedele explained that households could find it difficult to accept declining inflation figures when their daily market expenses remained high, adding that the disconnect could undermine confidence in official economic data.

According to him, when consumers lose confidence in inflation figures, their expectations could influence market behaviour, as traders and producers increase prices in anticipation of further increases in the cost of goods.

He said, “When people stop believing in numbers, they expect prices to rise, and expectations behave like instructions. So traders mark up in advance. Producers protect themselves. The baker and the dairy supplier price in that increase because they fear prices will go up. And the increase arrives in higher prices of bread and milk. It’s a self-fulfilling cycle.”

The minister argued that government communication must acknowledge the economic realities confronting households while explaining improvements in macroeconomic indicators.

“An improving indicator does not mean a struggling family’s experience is untrue. Just as a struggling family’s experience does not make an improving indicator false. Responsible communication must make room for both, which is how we explain progress without appearing indifferent to hardship,” he said.

He also blamed misinformation and distorted public narratives for undermining the government’s tax reforms, saying some commentators and analysts misrepresented provisions designed to protect small businesses and exempt low-income earners.

Beyond economic policies, the minister identified what he described as the ‘narrative cost’ of negative perceptions about Nigeria, warning that repeated claims portraying the country as a failed or unsafe investment destination could have serious economic consequences.

“We must desist from narratives such as ‘Nigeria is finished’, ‘Nigeria is a scam’ and ‘Nigeria happened to me.’ Such lines may begin in genuine frustration, and citizens, of course, have every right to expose government failure and demand accountability.

“But when repeated carelessly, they become a lens through which every development is judged, and they reach global investors pricing risk and young people deciding whether to build their future at home or abroad,” he warned.

Earlier in her speech, the Minister of Foreign Affairs, Bianca Odumegwu-Ojukwu, called for stronger diplomatic relations between Nigeria, members of the Economic Community of West African States and neighbouring Sahel countries to protect cross-border trade and tackle shared security challenges.

She warned that strained relations between neighbouring countries could undermine longstanding economic, social and cultural ties, stressing the need for sustained dialogue and cooperation regardless of political differences between governments.

The minister stated this while outlining Nigeria’s foreign policy priorities under President Bola Tinubu, which she said were anchored on democracy, development, demography and diaspora, adding that Nigeria’s relations with its West African neighbours should be guided by practical considerations, including the need to keep trade routes open and protect communities facing common security threats.

She said, “Here in West Africa, we know much is at stake when relations between neighbours become strained. Our borders run through places where people have traded, worked, and raised families for generations. Whatever differences governments may have, those ties endure.

“Nigeria values dialogue with our fellow members of ECOWAS and our neighbours in the Sahel because there are practical matters we must continue to address together, from keeping trade routes open to protecting communities that face common security threats.”

On Africa’s position in global affairs, she said the continent must move beyond being a subject of international discussions to actively influencing decisions affecting its people and institutions.

Explaining the Tinubu administration’s foreign policy direction, the minister said its four pillars must translate into measurable benefits for Nigerians, particularly young people and communities.

The foreign affairs minister also warned against the potential consequences of misinformation and disinformation on diplomatic relations, particularly in an era when fabricated audio and other digital content could spread rapidly through mobile devices.
