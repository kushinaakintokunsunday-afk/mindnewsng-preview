2027: Court dismisses suit seeking to bar Aondoakaa from Benue governorship race

By The Sun Nigeria The Sun Nigeria 16 Nov 2026 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: The Sun Nigeria

From Godwin Tsa, Abuja

A Federal High Court in Abuja on Monday dismissed a suit by four Benue State indigenes seeking to stop former Attorney-General of the Federation, Michael Aondoakaa, from contesting the 2027 governorship election in the state.

Delivering judgment, Justice Joyce Abdulmalik held that the plaintiffs lacked the locus standi to institute the action.

She upheld the preliminary objection raised by the defendants and ruled that the suit was statute-barred and was, in substance, a pre-election matter dressed up as a judgment enforcement action.

The judge said the suit was filed outside the mandatory 14-day window. She rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that they only became aware of Mr Aondoakaa’s nomination by the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) on August 30, 2026.

“The evidence before the court clearly showed that the third defendant was declared winner on June 24, 2026, and presented with a certificate of return on June 26, 2026,” she said. “This suit, having been filed outside the mandatory 14 days, is statute-barred and is dismissed in its entirety.”

On locus standi, Justice Abdulmalik held that the plaintiffs, who described themselves as registered voters in Benue, could not import themselves into matters of public interest litigation.

She also agreed with the defence that, given the nature of the reliefs sought, the suit was a pre-election matter. Since the plaintiffs were not candidates in the PDP governorship primary, she said, they had no standing to challenge its outcome.

The judge further held that the plaintiffs were not parties to Aondoakaa v. Obot, the case whose judgment they sought to enforce, and had failed to place material evidence before the court to justify the reliefs sought.

The plaintiffs, David Ejembi, Aondoaver Teryila, Joseph Okpanachi and Benedict Orkume, filed the suit, marked FHC/ABJ/CS/2152/2026, against the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC), the PDP and Mr Aondoakaa, the party’s governorship candidate.

Led by Fredricks Itula, SAN, they relied on the Supreme Court judgment in Aondoakaa v. Obot (2022) 5 NWLR (Pt. 1824) 523, which they argued bars Mr Aondoakaa from holding any public office. They contended that, under sections 235 and 287(1) of the Constitution, read with the definition of “public office of a state” in Section 318(1), INEC and the PDP were constitutionally bound to comply with that judgment.

They asked the court to:

* declare that INEC is obliged to obey the Supreme Court judgment by preventing the PDP from sponsoring Mr Aondoakaa in the 2027 poll;
* declare that the PDP is obliged to comply by withdrawing his candidature;
* issue a perpetual injunction restraining INEC from allowing the PDP to sponsor him; and
* issue a perpetual injunction restraining Mr Aondoakaa from contesting the election.

The defence

Mr Aondoakaa’s legal team, led by Prof. Anthony Ijohor, SAN, urged the court to dismiss the suit in limine as incompetent, on the following grounds:

* Defective counter-affidavit: The plaintiffs’ counter-affidavit to the objection was documentary hearsay, based on information from a lawyer in the same firm. Neither deponent had first-hand knowledge of the facts or was a party to the suit. The defence argued that the offending paragraphs should be struck out or discountenanced, citing Salini Nig. Ltd v. Lifewire Industries (2019) and Andrew v. INEC (2018).

* Disguised pre-election matter: The defence rejected the claim that the PDP was approbating and reprobating, insisting that the plaintiffs’ own label did not determine the true nature of the suit. It added that a fresh action could not be used to enforce a final judgment (AMCON v. Capital Oil & Gas, 2019), and that the suit was an abuse of process.

* No locus standi or cause of action: The suit challenged an election yet to hold and was therefore premature, hypothetical and speculative (Wabara v. Nnadede, 2009). The plaintiffs were neither aspirants in the primary nor judgment creditors in Aondoakaa v. Obot, and had shown no special interest or injury.

* Constitutional limits: Section 182 of the Constitution exhaustively lists the grounds for disqualification, so the court could not direct INEC to restrain the PDP from sponsoring a candidate. Sponsorship, the defence added, is the party’s internal affair.

* No right to enforce the judgment: Only a judgment creditor may enforce a judgment against a judgment debtor (Oviawe v. Igiebor, 2021; Access Bank v. Ikpon, 2025). A non-party would need leave of court and substitution under Order IV Rule 2 of the Judgment (Enforcement) Rules. The plaintiffs held no certificate of judgment (sections 104 and 107, Sheriffs and Civil Process Act), had no leave, and had no privity, since Aondoakaa v. Obot is a judgment in personam. The defence described them as “busybodies and meddlesome interlopers”.
