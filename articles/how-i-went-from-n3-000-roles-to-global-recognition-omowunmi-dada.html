How I went from N3,000 roles to global recognition–Omowunmi Dada

Award-winning Nollywood actress Omowunmi Dada has carved out a niche as one of the industry’s most compelling talents, with an impressive body of work spanning stage, television, and film. A graduate of Creative Arts from the University of Lagos, Dada has earned recognition for her versatility, captivating performances and commitment to telling meaningful African stories.

In a recent Appearance on Morayo Show, the actress reflects on her journey into Nollywood, the challenges of finding her feet in the industry, her international recognition and the importance of reclaiming the African woman’s narrative.

Enjoy!

How did your journey into Nollywood begin?

I studied Creative Arts at the University of Lagos, and right from my undergraduate days, I knew exactly what I wanted to do. I deliberately chose Creative Arts because I wanted to pursue acting. It wasn’t something I stumbled into or was persuaded to do. I knew what I wanted, so I went ahead and studied it.

My first stage production was Mori, in which I played Ario. One of the beautiful things about studying Creative Arts back then was that professionals were regularly invited to watch students’ performances.

During one of those productions, a man named Williams Eko saw me and expressed interest in having me feature in his play. He told me he would like me to be part of his production. That was how I moved from the academic environment into professional theatre.

While I was still in school, I began participating in professional theatre productions. Sometimes, I would tell my classmates that a particular class or test might not hold, and then head out for auditions. When I graduated in 2010, I was the best graduating student in my class. I subsequently worked with a media company for about a year, but I knew deep down that I wanted to return to acting.

How challenging was it finding your way back into the industry?

It wasn’t easy. Sometimes, auditions scheduled for 9 a.m. would find you at the venue as early as 7 a.m., only for you to discover that you were number 200 on the list. Even after waiting for hours, you might be told, “We’ll get back to you,” and never hear from them again. But I always reminded myself to give my best at every opportunity because you never know who is watching. I also believe in being audacious. If I want to meet someone or be part of a project, I ask. The worst they can say is ‘No’.

During the BlackBerry era, I reached out to a sound engineer, Valentine, whose production pictures I saw on his status. I told him I liked what he was working on and wanted to be involved. He initially said there was no substantial female role, except a walk-on character of a rape victim. I insisted he speak to the producer.

I eventually contacted the producer and asked several questions about the character because I wanted to understand her background and personality. My curiosity paid off. What was initially a one-scene role grew to three scenes, then six. The producer even developed a backstory for the character, and I eventually became the youngest sister of the film’s villain.

At the end of filming, I was offered just N3,000 because there was no budget for the role. I accepted because I was more interested in showcasing my talent and gaining the experience.

The short Nigerian film The Wages (2013) was written, produced, and directed by Walter Taylaur (under Waltbanger101 Productions. It went on to win Best Short Film at the AMVCA that year. His next call was for Married to the Game, an EbonyLife-commissioned series, where he offered me the lead female role. That was another breakthrough for me.

You recently starred at the 2026 BFI London Film Festival, where your work received international recognition. How would you describe the experience?

It was a beautiful and humbling experience. The film that took me to the BFI London Film Festival was The Secret Lives of Baba Segi’s Wives, adapted from the bestselling novel, in which I played the lead female role.

As a Theatre Arts student and film enthusiast, I had always dreamed of seeing my work screened at prestigious festivals such as Cannes, Sundance, Toronto and BFI. At BFI, we had two sold-out screenings, with audiences from diverse cultural backgrounds embracing a Yoruba-language film. Seeing people who were unfamiliar with our language and culture connect with the story was truly remarkable. Conversations about my character made the experience even more fulfilling.

At Film Africa, you spoke about the African woman reclaiming her narrative. Why is this important to you?

I grew up in a generation where women were rarely celebrated for their many achievements. Take Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, for instance. People often remember her as the first woman to drive a car, overlooking her many other remarkable contributions. We must reclaim these stories and show the world how extraordinary African women are.

As an actor, my tool for social change is the stories I tell, the films I make and the characters I portray. I want the world to see African women as strong, vibrant and resilient. We break barriers, open doors for others and keep pushing until we achieve positive results.

Your position is particularly inspiring because many women are struggling with their own challenges and sometimes feel that the odds are against them. How important is it for women to share their stories and encourage one another?

I think it is very important. Every woman has a story, and many have had to overcome different challenges to get to where they are today. Sometimes, all someone needs is to hear another woman’s experience and realise that she, too, can overcome her circumstances. When women share their journeys, they inspire others to keep going.

We must continue to tell our stories, celebrate our achievements and encourage one another. Women are doing so much, and we need to ensure that their contributions are seen, heard and appreciated.

You were also part of the team behind the Guinness World Records attempt involving The Black Book. How did that historic initiative come about?

Nora approached me with a story about women, and I immediately bought into the idea. Interestingly, women’s stories often find their way to me, so I was excited to be part of it.

As we developed the film, we began exploring a distribution strategy beyond the usual cinema, YouTube or streaming platforms. We wanted something bigger, more iconic and globally significant.

Nora suggested a public screening. Inspired by Femi Adebayo’s community cinema initiative, we decided to take it a step further. When we discovered that the Guinness World Records record for simultaneous film viewing stood at 43,000 people, we thought, why not aim for 50,000?

It was ambitious, and we had no idea how we would pull it off, but I believed it was possible. We created awareness, and Nigerians showed up. We initially targeted 50,000 people, but 63,000 turned up, with 51,000 watching the film from beginning to end.

It was historic. Seeing thousands of Nigerians gather not for football or a concert, but to watch a film was remarkable. It was a massive win for Nollywood and Nigeria.

Beyond that project, what else are you working on, and what should your fans expect from you in the near future?

There are so many beautiful projects in the pipeline, but I don’t want to reveal too much just yet. We have an animation project coming out next year, alongside several exciting films from my production company, Signet Ring Studios. I’m also involved in projects as an actress. That’s all I can share for now.

You seem to be glowing from within. What is responsible for that?

It is the Lord’s doing, and it is marvellous in my sight. Let me tell you something: is life perfect? No, it isn’t. But when you know whose child you are, you are always encouraged because, no matter what happens, you know your Father has got you.

Honestly, that is what it is for me. God has got me. I am Jesus’ baby; I have the joy of the Lord, and that is it.

I am also very passionate about education, and I want to use this opportunity to inspire women. Never give up, and dream as big as you possibly can. That space you think you cannot dominate is one you can actually conquer. Everything you need is already within you.

When I started, the opportunities available to women in the industry were largely limited to acting, costume design and makeup. Today, we have female cinematographers, directors and producers. Women drive some of the biggest box-office successes, and women are also making remarkable strides in film distribution and other areas of the industry.

It wasn’t always like this. Women had to be audacious, speak their truth, and show the world what they were capable of.

Most importantly, whatever you do, strive for excellence. Let people say, “I have worked with this woman, and she is fantastic at her job.”

Be kind to everyone, and never forget that God loves you
