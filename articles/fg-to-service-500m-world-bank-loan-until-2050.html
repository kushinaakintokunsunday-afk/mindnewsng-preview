FG to service $500m World Bank loan until 2050

By Punch Punch 2d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Federal Government will repay a $500m World Bank agricultural loan over 20 years, beginning in May 2031 and ending in November 2050, under a financing agree

The agreement obtained by Sunday PUNCH from the World Bank’s website on Friday provides that the country will make 40 semi-annual principal repayments of 2.5 per cent each, equivalent to $12.5m per instalment if the entire credit is drawn.

According to Schedule 3 of the agreement, “the Recipient shall repay the principal amount of the credit” on May 15 and November 15 each year, beginning May 15, 2031, and ending November 15, 2050.

This means the country will remain responsible for servicing the agricultural credit until 2050, although the project itself is scheduled to close on July 30, 2032.

The PUNCH earlier reported in April that the World Bank approved a $500m International Development Association credit to boost Nigeria’s agricultural sector under a new programme targeting smallholder farmers, value chains, and food security.

The funding will support the Nigeria Sustainable Agricultural Value-Chains for Growth project, also known as AGROW, which aims to improve productivity, strengthen market linkages, and create jobs across the country.

The 29-page financing agreement obtained by Sunday PUNCH covers the AGROW loan project.

It was signed by the Minister of Finance, Taiwo Oyedele, on October 29, 2026, while the World Bank Country Director, Mathew Verghis, signed on September 16, 2026.

Under Article II, the IDA agreed to provide “a credit, which is deemed as Concessional Financing” amounting to $500m to support agricultural development.

The agreement imposes a maximum commitment charge of 0.5 per cent annually on the undisbursed financing balance.

It also provides that the interest charge will be calculated using the reference rate plus a variable spread, less 250 basis points, subject to applicable ceilings and floors.

The project seeks to increase smallholder productivity and strengthen agricultural value chains in participating Nigerian states.

Of the total financing, $230m, representing 46 per cent, is allocated to performance-based matching grants for agribusiness investments.

Another $229.5m, or 45.9 per cent, will finance goods, works, consultancy services, operating costs, and training, while $40.5m is reserved for expenditure linked to performance-based conditions.

The document explains that the matching grants will support investments in agricultural aggregation, storage, processing, logistics, post-harvest handling, and market connections between farmers and buyers.

It provides for agricultural research, improved seeds, extension services, digital farmer registration, and artificial intelligence-powered weather forecasting.

The agreement also requires improvements in seed certification, fertiliser quality regulation, and responsible land-based agricultural investment.

However, participating states must satisfy several conditions before accessing the financing.

According to Annex 2, states must adopt a responsible land investment framework through an executive order and establish regulations supporting a common digital farmer registry.

They must also publish “a consolidated schedule of trade-related fees and levies for inter-state movement of goods” and reports detailing traders’ complaints and remedial actions.

States must also publish policies linking support for agricultural cooperatives to verified commercial relationships with buyers.

Failure to meet these requirements could prevent participating states from receiving project funds.

The agreement provides that no withdrawal shall be made for payments to participating states until the Federal Government presents satisfactory evidence of compliance and executes the required subsidiary agreements.

The World Bank may also withhold, reallocate, or cancel financing linked to performance targets that are not achieved.

Among the targets is the registration of farmers in a national digital registry, with basic and enhanced coverage targets of 40 per cent and 70 per cent, respectively.

The agreement allocates $16m to this condition, with payments capped at $1m per participating state.

Other performance-linked allocations include $5m each for improved seed availability and private investment in seed production, $2.5m for fertiliser quality compliance, and $12m for responsible land investments.

The agreement requires the Federal Government to establish a national steering committee and a project coordination unit within three months of the project becoming effective.

It also mandates independent verification of performance targets, environmental and social safeguards, and periodic implementation reports.

For artificial intelligence applications, the agreement requires risk assessments before deployment and prohibits uses involving mass surveillance, social scoring, and discriminatory practices.

The financing agreement sets an effectiveness deadline of 90 days after the October 29 signature date, subject to the required implementation arrangements.

Although the credit is intended to strengthen agricultural productivity and private investment, its repayment schedule creates a long-term dollar-denominated obligation extending nearly two decades beyond the project’s scheduled closing date.

An analysis of figures published by the Debt Management Office showed that Nigeria’s outstanding debt to the World Bank rose from $14.34bn in March 2023, shortly before President Bola Tinubu assumed office, to $20.73bn as of June 30, 2026.

This represents an increase of approximately $6.39bn, or 44.6 per cent, within the period.

The latest debt stock comprised $19.12bn owed to the International Development Association and $1.61bn to the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development.

Consequently, the World Bank accounted for approximately 38.02 per cent of Nigeria’s total external debt stock of $54.52bn at the end of June 2026, making it the country’s largest multilateral creditor.

Sunday PUNCH further observed that Nigeria’s external debt increased from N33.25tn ($43.16bn) in June 2023, shortly after President Bola Tinubu assumed office, to N75.20tn ($54.52bn) in June 2026, according to data from the DMO.

The figures represent an increase of N41.95tn, or 126.17 per cent, in naira terms, while the dollar-denominated debt rose by $11.36bn, or 26.32 per cent, over the three years.

The increase reflects additional external borrowing and the impact of exchange rate movements on the naira value of foreign debt, with external obligations accounting for 45.09 per cent of Nigeria’s N166.79tn total public debt as of June 2026.

Meanwhile, the World Bank’s International Debt Report 2026 has raised concerns about rising borrowing costs and debt servicing obligations among developing countries, warning that increased access to international financing does not necessarily translate into economic development.

The report stated, “Low- and middle-income countries are borrowing more, paying more, and, in many cases, leaving less for long-term development.”

It warned that rising debt servicing obligations could limit government spending on infrastructure, education, healthcare, and employment, particularly in countries already facing substantial financing needs.
