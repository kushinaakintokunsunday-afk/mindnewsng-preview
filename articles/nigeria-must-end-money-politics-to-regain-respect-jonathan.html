Nigeria must end money politics to regain respect – Jonathan

By Punch Punch 2d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Former President Goodluck Jonathan has called for an overhaul of Nigeria’s political system to end the influence of money, patronage and ethnic considerations in leadership selection.

He warned that the practice was damaging the country’s international reputation.

Jonathan said restoring the country’s global standing would require credible elections, internal party democracy, accountable leadership and a political culture that prioritises competence over personal connections.

He made the call on Saturday in Abuja while delivering the keynote address at the third edition of the Paul Alaje Colloquium, themed “The Brand Nigeria.”

“We must reform our political and leadership recruitment processes. We must strengthen internal party democracy, electoral credibility and a political culture that is more inclusive, rewards ideas, character and competence rather than money, patronage and sectional interests,” Jonathan said.

The call came as Nigeria prepares for the 2027 general elections, amid longstanding concerns over vote-buying, the influence of money in party primaries and the use of political patronage in leadership selection.

Previous elections have drawn scrutiny over voter inducements, with the Independent National Electoral Commission and anti-corruption agencies repeatedly warning that electoral malpractice undermines democratic credibility.

The former President identified the growing preference for wealth, ethnicity and political connections in public appointments as one of the major factors undermining the country’s development and international credibility.

“The growing preference for money, patronage, ethnicity and personal connections over competence and merit in leadership recruitment and public appointments,” he said.

According to him, corruption, abuse of public office, impunity, insecurity and the weakening of public institutions have contributed to Nigeria’s declining global standing.

He also criticised the increasing celebration of wealth without adequate scrutiny of its source, alongside the normalisation of cybercrime, drug trafficking, cultism and other criminal activities.

“These developments have consequences beyond our borders. They undermine investor confidence, weaken international partnerships and diminish the respect attached to the Nigerian name,” Jonathan warned.

The ex-President also lamented that Nigeria, once respected for its diplomatic influence, economic potential and contributions to African liberation, was increasingly associated with corruption, unemployment, insecurity and economic difficulties.

“Today, we must honestly acknowledge that the image of our country has suffered considerable setbacks,” he said.

Jonathan expressed concern over the relatively weak international standing of the Nigerian passport, arguing that visa restrictions and limited travel opportunities reflected the challenges confronting the country’s reputation.

“A passport is more than a travel document; it is a symbol of national identity and the confidence other countries place in the nation that issues it,” he explained.

Nevertheless, he maintained that the country’s challenges should not overshadow the achievements of millions of Nigerians who continued to distinguish themselves internationally.

“Our problem is not a shortage of human potential. It is our failure to translate that potential consistently into effective institutions, responsible leadership and national progress,” he said.

The former President recalled the country’s contributions to peacekeeping operations in Liberia and Sierra Leone, the struggle against apartheid and the achievements of its professionals, athletes and creative industries.

He referenced the 2009 national rebranding campaign championed by the late Dora Akunyili under former President Umaru Musa Yar’Adua, which adopted the slogan, “Good People, Great Nation.”

Jonathan urged Nigeria to learn from Singapore, South Korea, Malaysia and India, particularly Singapore’s emphasis on meritocracy, institutional discipline, integrity and human capital development.

He called for greater investment in education, healthcare, agriculture, manufacturing, technology and infrastructure to build a productive economy capable of generating employment.

He also advocated stronger support for Nollywood, Afrobeats, literature, fashion and sports as instruments of cultural diplomacy and economic development.

“Restoring the Nigeria brand requires more than slogans and publicity campaigns. It requires a national commitment to changing the realities that shape our reputation,” he said.

Addressing young Nigerians, Jonathan urged them to uphold integrity, reject criminality and demand accountability from political leaders while accepting responsibility for their conduct.

In his opening address, the convener of the colloquium, Paul Alaje, warned that the country could not improve its international image through publicity without addressing institutional weaknesses and economic underperformance.

“We must stop confusing potential with progress. Potential is not prosperity. Resources are not development. Population is not automatically productivity. And international recognition is not national transformation,” Alaje said.

He argued that insecurity, corruption, policy inconsistency and weak institutions increased business costs, discouraged investment and accelerated capital and talent flight.

Alaje identified healthcare, governance and security, the creative economy, artificial intelligence and investment as five critical areas for strengthening Nigeria’s international competitiveness.

He urged policymakers to prioritise productivity, institutional trust, human development and support for businesses, while ensuring economic reforms translate into employment, purchasing power and improved living standards.

“We cannot market what we have not built. We cannot communicate our way out of institutional weaknesses. We cannot advertise our way into prosperity,” he said.

Alaje also praised Jonathan’s peaceful transfer of power in 2015, describing it as an important demonstration of democratic responsibility.

He called for a consistent national development vision that would survive changes in political administrations.
