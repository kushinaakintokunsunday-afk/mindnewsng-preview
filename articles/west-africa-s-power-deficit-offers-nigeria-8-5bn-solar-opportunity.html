West Africa’s power deficit offers Nigeria $8.5bn solar opportunity

By Punch Punch 6d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG

They said the country must move beyond importing solar panels and other renewable energy technologies to developing local manufacturing, mineral processing, battery production and assembly facilities capable of serving the domestic and regional markets.

The experts also identified solar-powered agriculture, irrigation, cold storage and agro-processing as emerging investment opportunities, while calling for affordable financing, stronger product standards and technical skills development to make the energy transition inclusive.

They spoke at an event organised at the Nigerian Pavilion on the sidelines of the ongoing 31st session of the United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP31) in Türkiye. The discussion, themed ‘Just Transition: Unlocking Nigeria’s Solar Opportunities’, was part of activities marking the conference’s Ocean, Seas, Nature and Land Use thematic day on Tuesday.

Speaking during a presentation at the event, the Executive Director of the Global Initiative for Food Security and Ecosystem Preservation, Michael David, said Nigeria possessed the mineral resources and market potential to build a competitive solar industry but was missing out on the economic benefits by depending heavily on imported technologies.

He identified 12 minerals, including silica, lithium, copper, aluminium, iron ore, graphite, manganese, nickel, cobalt, tin, zinc and silver, which could support different stages of solar panel and battery production.

According to him, the resources are available in several states, including Nasarawa, Kogi, Kwara, Niger, Zamfara, Plateau, Bauchi, Ondo and Osun.

He said, “Solar energy is not just an opportunity. It is industrialisation. It is investment. It is an economic transformation opportunity for Nigeria. The opportunity goes beyond just deploying solar panels.

“It includes local manufacturing, minerals processing, battery storage, job creation, agricultural productivity and even regional exports. And so Nigeria must move from just importing solar panels to producing, manufacturing, assembling and exporting clean energy technologies.”

David disclosed that Nigeria imported about 2.9 million solar panels last year, spending more than N450bn, despite having some of the raw materials required for domestic production.

He argued that developing local manufacturing capacity would reduce import dependence, create employment and enable Nigeria to capture a larger share of the regional renewable energy market.

Making a case for the West African market, he cited figures showing that the region accounted for about 33 per cent of the 565 million people without electricity access in sub-Saharan Africa in 2023, representing approximately 185 million people.

He said the scale of the electricity deficit presented an opportunity for Nigerian manufacturers to serve neighbouring countries rather than concentrate only on the domestic market.

David added that the International Finance Corporation had identified about $8.5bn in commercially viable solar investment opportunities in Nigeria by 2030, while broader distributed renewable energy opportunities could exceed $18bn over the next decade.

He stated, “Let’s look at the entire West Africa as the big market size for the solar market. The entire sub-Saharan Africa has a population of about 565 million people. And out of this number, within West Africa, we are looking at nearly 33 per cent of that. That is a huge market.

On the government’s position, the Senior Special Assistant to the President on Climate Technologies, Olamide Fagbuji, said Nigeria was working to create an enabling policy environment to attract private investment into renewable energy.

He identified the Electricity Act, which allows states to participate in electricity generation and distribution, as an important development for expanding decentralised power supply.

Fagbuji said Nigeria’s Nationally Determined Contribution 3.0 had set ambitious renewable energy targets, including increasing the share of renewables in the energy mix by nine per cent annually towards 50 per cent by 2030.

He, however, stressed that achieving the targets would require substantial financing and stronger collaboration between government and private investors.

However, the Executive Director of Bridge That Gap Initiative, Gloria Bulus, said affordability remained the biggest obstacle preventing low-income households and underserved communities from adopting solar energy.

She explained that unlike conventional electricity payments, which consumers could make gradually, solar installations often required substantial upfront payments for panels, batteries and other components.

“For communities, it is not about the accessibility of solar panels. It is not about the solar itself. It is about the affordability of those panels and even the battery,” she said.
