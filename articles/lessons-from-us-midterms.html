Lessons from US midterms

IN keeping with pre-election projections, the Democratic Party secured a landslide in America’s November 3 midterm elections. It is proof of how genuine democracy works and sustains itself. Voters reward performance and punish failure. They vote according to their daily realities, rather than political slogans or burdensome promises. What unfolded across America was a referendum on the Donald Trump administration.

Nigeria’s political leadership should draw valuable lessons from the blue wave.

The Republicans lost control of Congress – the Senate and the House of Representatives.

The Democrats captured (flipped) Senate seats in traditional Republican strongholds such as Texas, Ohio and Kansas. Kansas had not elected a Democratic senator in almost a century. This reflected deep public dissatisfaction with the direction of President Trump. He now faces a difficult two years left in his White House tenure, which Americans dub “a lame-duck presidency.”

His approval rating crashed to an average of just 39 per cent, while 59 per cent of Americans disapproved of his performance. The rejection came even from voters in states he had comfortably won only two years earlier.

The message from the electorate was clear: lived experience matters far more than campaign promises or the size of an incumbent’s ego.

The American voters’ primary concerns were inflation, expensive food items and other daily needs, and rising energy costs. They griped about high tariffs and the war in Iran.

Trump’s immigration policy was also a factor, as many voters believed that Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents had crossed the line in Trump’s crackdown on immigrants. Some citizens who contributed significantly to sectors such as agriculture, construction, hospitality and childcare were rounded up and pushed out. There were fatalities too.

Perceptions of corruption further damaged the administration. Trump’s cryptocurrency ventures and financial dealings with foreign interests reportedly came under intense scrutiny. The president reportedly earned about $2.2 billion in 2025, with more than half attributed to cryptocurrency businesses.

American voters took note.

For Nigerians, the more than three and a half years of the Bola Tinubu administration have brought extraordinary economic hardship. To be fair, some of the administration’s reforms have produced measurable gains.

The removal of the petrol subsidies has largely eliminated persistent fuel shortages. The foreign exchange reforms have also significantly reduced uncertainty in the currency market.

Yet these gains have come at enormous cost. Petrol sells for between N1,300 and N1,400 per litre. This has dramatically increased transportation costs, food prices and the cost of virtually every essential service.

In a country that pays a minimum wage of N70,000, this matters. Inflation at around 14.05 per cent eats up incomes and pushes millions deeper into hardship.

Insecurity is pervasive. Nigeria ranks among the most terrorised countries globally, and over two million citizens remain displaced in internally displaced persons’ camps.

More than 140 million Nigerians live in multidimensional poverty. Healthcare, education, electricity, manufacturing and agriculture continue to struggle despite repeated government assurances.

Many citizens question government priorities. Projects such as the Lagos-Calabar Coastal Highway have generated considerable debate at a time when many believe scarce public resources should be directed toward hospitals, schools, security, rural roads, food production and social protection.

History documents such electoral earthquakes when the electorate used their votes to express dissatisfaction with incumbent governments.

In Venezuela’s 2015 parliamentary elections, the opposition Democratic Unity Roundtable secured a stunning two-thirds majority in the National Assembly. That upset ended 16 years of legislative dominance by President Nicolás Maduro’s ruling party. Soaring inflation, dwindling oil revenues, food shortages and widespread economic misery drove voters to the opposition.

The Taiwanese delivered a similar verdict in the 2022 local elections, with the opposition Kuomintang capturing 13 of the 22 mayoral and magistrate seats. It was the ruling Democratic Progressive Party’s worst electoral defeat. Voters were concerned about economic conditions, infrastructure and governance.

Argentina followed the same pattern in its 2021 legislative midterm elections. President Alberto Fernández’s governing coalition suffered heavy losses as inflation exceeded 50 per cent and poverty worsened.

The scandal of a birthday party at the presidential residence was probably the last straw. Citizens concluded that leaders who asked for sacrifice should themselves demonstrate discipline and accountability.

Across the democratic world, economic hardship, insecurity, corruption and poor governance carry political consequences.

For Nigeria’s political class, the challenge is especially urgent. With less than two months before the polls, there is hardly any room for dramatic policy corrections.

But beyond this election cycle, one lesson must not be lost. Leaders must never become insulated from the citizens. Lived experiences matter more than government statistics.

America’s blue wave is a reminder that democracy ultimately belongs to the people.
