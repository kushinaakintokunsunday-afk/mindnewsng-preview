On election campaigns and election manifestos

By Punch Punch 16 Nov 2026 5 min read

Governance should focus on sustainable economic development, improving people’s living conditions day by day. If in doubt, ask President Donald Trump. The global rising cost of living or declining standard of living led, invariably, to demonstrations in countries like Germany and France, while United States citizens used the Mid-Term elections in November to demonstrate their displeasure with President Trump and the Republican Party, with the loss of a majority in both the Senate and House of Representatives. With 55 seats in the Senate, the Democrats gained eight more seats lost by the Republicans. In the House, Democrats won 256 seats to the Republicans’ 169.

 According to available research records, the loss by the Republican Party has been attributed to five main causes, namely soaring cost of living, the unpopular Iran war, a disciplined message from the democrats, unpopularity of President Trump in connection to his policies generally and economic policies in particular, and shifts in the position of Hispanic and black voters as related to Trump’s immigration policy. What matters here is the soaring cost of living, a global phenomenon. The state of an economy, specifically citizens’ economic condition, has a way of helping or destroying candidates participating in elections. The rising cost of living phenomenon is the necklace being worn by every country’s leader today!

 When we talk about the macroeconomic goals every country wants to achieve, price stability is central. It seems to be the central point around which achieving other macroeconomic goals revolves. The macro goals or objectives are full employment of resources, economic growth, price stability, balance of payments equilibrium, equitable income distribution and sustainable development. We need stable prices to achieve full employment, yet we need some moderate inflation to achieve economic growth. If the law of supply is to hold, upward adjustment of prices serves to encourage higher supply. That is, the higher the price, the higher the supply.

 And higher supply means growth. Growth, encouraged by higher prices, makes external trade possible, improving the balance of payments and moving it toward equilibrium. Higher economic growth implies higher national income, for which equitable distribution is essential for peace to reign in the country. Sustainable economic growth moves the economy toward rising employment and incomes or the full-employment goal and eventually results in economic development. While moderate price rises necessarily achieve those stated macroeconomic objectives, the kind of rapid inflation being witnessed today creates a disastrous situation, particularly with respect to citizens’ welfare. The fact that this second wave of inflation came from external shocks provides a lesson in policymaking.

 We witnessed the first wave of inflation when the subsidy on fuel was removed and petroleum prices were deregulated, in addition to the massive devaluation of the naira by about 300 per cent. That was a domestic shock and did require some reforms. External shocks require policy revolution to prevent, in the future, the magnitude of the effects that we are having presently. That is why the current political campaign should centre around economic revolution rather than economic reform.

 Many African leaders are afraid of using revolution but prefer reforms, the language of the World Bank and IMF. Whereas the economic policies are necessary to take Africa out of the economic doldrum, it finds itself in a revolutionary adjustment. There is a need to overhaul the economic system of production whereby we produce to satisfy the industrial needs of advanced economies. We remain producers of raw materials in human and non-human resources. Our physical products feed industries in the Western world, and human resources are attracted by improved working environments and higher wages and salaries.

 A revolution does not mean chaotic changes like the French Revolution, but drastic, radical, or fundamental changes like the First Industrial Revolution in 18th- and 19th-century Great Britain. The revolution transformed global production, labour, and trade through mechanised factories and steam power. What we require in Nigeria and the whole of Africa is revolutionary production in agriculture, factories or industries generally, whereby we export finished products like the rest of the world. The present campaign for election in Nigeria and the manifestos of principal actors should reflect a revolutionary departure from the present situation.

 Such revolutionary changes will minimise the depth of external shocks that we are witnessing now. Politicians should tell us how many megawatts of electricity will be generated yearly and how they will do it. President Tinubu was promising a growth rate of six per cent per year for the next four years. That is not ambitious. He should be proposing a minimum growth of 10 per cent. China did take over 900 million Chinese out of poverty in three decades by growing at 10 per cent per year for more than a decade. According to the World Bank Poverty and Equity Report, an estimated 496 million Indians moved out of poverty between 2011 and 2023, driven by increased employment opportunities. This was linked to sustained growth of about 10 per cent over the decade. Specific government schemes were credited with the massive decline.

 Every presidential candidate should be specific about what to do with agriculture, education, industry, science and technology and medicine. Manifestos must be clear, just like the campaign. Every specific promise must be backed by data and a timeline. We cannot continue to run Nigeria on handouts to consumers; we must run it on jobs and enhanced productivity. A conducive production environment must be promised and fulfilled. This will include reliable electricity or power infrastructure. Without electricity, neither China nor India would have achieved the feats reported. China has long achieved 100 per cent electricity, while India is almost there, with an achievement of over 90 per cent in the last five years. The Nigerian government, like other African governments, must declare revolutionary development rather than unending reforms and transformation.
