CJN faults weak enforcement of child protection laws

By Punch Punch 2d ago 4 min read

The Chief Justice of Nigeria, Justice Kudirat Kekere-Ekun, has said the country’s failure to effectively enforce child protection laws continues to expose children to abuse, exploitation and neglect, despite the existence of legal safeguards.

She stressed that legislation alone could not guarantee children’s safety, insisting that stronger institutional commitment and coordinated enforcement were necessary to translate the provisions of the law into tangible protection.

The CJN spoke on Friday at the inaugural conference of the Nigerian Association of Family Court Judicial Officers, Magistrates and Other Practitioners, organised to commemorate the 2026 United Nations World Children’s Day in Abuja.

Represented by a Supreme Court Justice, Jummai Sanke, Kekere-Ekun said the Child Rights Act 2003 provided an important legal framework for protecting children but required effective implementation by relevant institutions.

“In Nigeria, the Child Rights Act 2003 provides an important legal framework for the protection and advancement of children’s rights. Its recognition of the best interests of the child as a primary consideration in all actions concerning children establishes a principle that must guide our laws, policies, institutions and decisions.

“However, the existence of legislation, important as it is, does not by itself guarantee the protection of children. The real measure of our commitment lies in how effectively these rights are respected, protected and enforced in the daily lives of children,” she said.

The CJN listed child abuse, neglect, sexual exploitation, trafficking, child labour, early and forced marriage, abandonment and denial of access to education and healthcare among the persistent threats to children’s welfare.

She said the judiciary had a responsibility to ensure that children appearing before courts as victims, witnesses or children in conflict with the law were treated with dignity and sensitivity.

Kekere-Ekun called for child-friendly judicial proceedings that protected children’s privacy, allowed them to participate and took account of their age, maturity and emotional circumstances.

She added that children in conflict with the law should, where appropriate, be rehabilitated and reintegrated into society rather than subjected to measures capable of permanently undermining their future.

The CJN also advocated regular training for judicial officers on trauma-informed approaches, handling child witnesses and emerging forms of exploitation, including those involving digital technology.

She urged government institutions, law enforcement agencies, social welfare services, schools, healthcare providers, families and civil society organisations to work together to strengthen child protection.

“A child who has suffered abuse should not have to navigate a fragmented system in search of help,” she said.

Meanwhile, the Attorney-General of the Federation and Minister of Justice, Lateef Fagbemi, SAN, disclosed that the Federal Government was reviewing the Child Rights Act 2003 to address emerging challenges in child protection.

Fagbemi said the review had produced a draft amendment bill scheduled for stakeholder validation on November 24, ahead of further steps towards its finalisation and enactment.

He said the country’s major challenge was not the absence of laws but the failure to implement them effectively.

“The pressing challenge before us is not the absence of laws, but the gap in their implementation. Across the country, too many children still face realities that threaten their safety and stifle their potential.

“Bridging this gap demands sustained political will, coordinated institutional action, and a readiness to adapt our systems to the evolving vulnerabilities of our children,” he said.

The minister said the Federal Ministry of Justice was also working to strengthen the child justice system through Legal Aid Guidelines and Prosecutors’ Guidelines for Handling Cases of Children in Contact with the Law.

He said the guidelines were intended to ensure quality legal representation and child-sensitive prosecution standards nationwide.

Fagbemi also called for stronger safeguards against cyberbullying, digital exploitation, harmful online content and breaches of children’s privacy.

He urged participants to ensure that the conference produced practical recommendations capable of improving court proceedings, policies and frontline child protection services.

Earlier, the President of NAFCJOMP, Justice Eberechi Suzette Nyesom-Wike, said the association was committed to advancing children’s rights and welfare through improved family court practice and a child-sensitive justice system.

She said the association, registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission in January 2026, brought together judicial officers, magistrates, prosecutors, social welfare officers, advocates, law enforcement personnel and other child protection practitioners from the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory.

Nyesom-Wike said its objectives included promoting compliance with child protection laws, advocating policies on education, healthcare and social security, and supporting the implementation of the Child Rights Act and corresponding state laws.

She added that the association, working with partners, had facilitated access to counselling, legal aid and medical support through a referral system.

The Speaker of the Children’s Parliament, Miss Progress Umoh, also made a case for children’s rights, while pupils from secondary schools across the FCT spoke on issues affecting their welfare.
