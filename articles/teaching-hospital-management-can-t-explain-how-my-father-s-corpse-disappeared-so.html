Teaching hospital management can’t explain how my father’s corpse disappeared — Son

By Punch Punch 2d ago 10 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Three months after depositing the corpse of his late father, Chief Chukwunweike Okammelu, at the Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu Teaching Hospital mortuary in Awka, Anambra State, Onyebuchi Nwankwo was preparing for the burial when he discovered that the body had gone missing. He tells AYOOLA OLASUPO about discrepancies the family uncovered in the hospital’s records and efforts made to find the missing corpse

Can you recount the circumstances surrounding how your father’s body was deposited at the hospital mortuary?

I am the first son of the late Chief Chukwunweike Okammelu. My father died on December 30, 2025. According to the information provided by the hospital, he died of a heart attack.

I was at work when my stepmother called to inform me that he had taken ill and was being taken to the hospital. I was later told that he was moved from the initial hospital to the Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu Teaching Hospital in Awka, Anambra State.

By the time I arrived at the emergency ward, my father was already declared dead. We subsequently proceeded to the hospital mortuary to deposit his corpse.

My uncle made the payment, and the cashier issued us receipts and a tally card bearing the name Clement, the mortician assigned to attend to us whenever we visited. We were also given a form to complete.

My uncle’s in-law, a doctor who had previously worked at the hospital, helped us to fill it out because we were still grieving.

After completing the necessary procedures, my father’s body was taken into the mortuary, and we left.

When did you plan to bury him?

The burial was fixed for April 9, 2026. We had printed invitation cards, sewn our family uniforms and informed my father’s maternal and paternal relatives, as well as our in-laws. We were already making preparations when we discovered that his corpse was missing.

How did you discover that the corpse had disappeared?

My sister and I discovered the disappearance on March 1, 2026. We had travelled to Enugu, where we were staying with her husband, to meet our in-laws and formally inform them about the burial. On our way back, we decided to stop at the hospital so my sister could see our father, whom she had not seen since his death.

As usual, we called Clement, the mortician, and showed him our tally card. He went into the mortuary to locate the corpse. After about 35 to 40 minutes, he came out and asked whether I could remember where my father’s body had been kept. I said yes, and he asked me to accompany him inside.

We went to the particular section and opened the body bag, but my father’s corpse was not there. We searched other body bags in that section but could not find him. We also could not locate the tally number, 331, which was supposed to be on his wrist.

We proceeded to search every section of the mortuary, including the VIP section, where corpses were kept separately for a fee, and the section where the bodies of people shot or killed by the police were kept. Despite searching everywhere, we could not find my father’s body.

I immediately alerted my family, particularly my father’s eldest sister, and informed them that we could not find his corpse.

Did you check the mortuary records to establish whether his body was transferred or released?

Yes. I asked the mortician to bring out the register used to document incoming corpses. I believed that if there was a register for receiving bodies, there should also be one for recording corpses transferred or released.

We checked the records from January 1 to March 1, but my father’s name and tally number were not on the list of corpses that had been removed.

There was, however, an earlier incident that became significant after the disappearance.

On January 19, 2026, my elder sister, aunt and I visited the mortuary. When the mortician brought out my father’s body, we noticed that the tally number on his wrist was different from the one on the card issued to us. The number had also been written on a piece of cardboard instead of the proper identification card.

I pointed this out to the mortician, who removed the wrong number. We later went to check the register. Although my father’s name, the depositor’s name and phone number were recorded, the space for the tally number was blank. We asked him to enter the correct number, and he did so immediately.

We also gave him a white wrapper known as Kilikilista, which we had chosen to make identification easier. Other corpses had different wrappers, so we believed ours would help us recognise my father’s body whenever we visited. He assured us that he would keep it safe and correct the tally number.

Did the wrong tally number and missing entry in the register raise suspicions?

No. We considered them oversights that could be corrected. I never imagined that something like this could happen in real life. We believed the mortician had simply made a mistake and would rectify it.

If we suspected that something was wrong, we would have immediately alerted the hospital management.

What explanation did the hospital give when you reported the disappearance?

After leaving the hospital that evening, I called Clement around 3am to ask whether he had found the corpse. He said he had not but suggested that it might have been misplaced or transferred elsewhere.

I challenged him because we had checked the physical transfer register and found no record of my father’s movement. He then claimed that transfers were recorded electronically on the computer system in the Reverend Sister’s office, rather than in the mortuary register.

I asked him whether, as the mortician responsible for the corpse, he did not also have a record of such a transfer. He still could not tell me where my father’s body was supposedly taken.

I returned to the hospital on March 3, but Clement was absent. We were told that he had travelled to Enugu or Ebonyi State. I went to the Reverend Sister’s office and asked whether the hospital had any record of my father’s corpse being transferred. She said there was none and that she had been trying to reach the mortician.

When I eventually spoke to Clement, he claimed he travelled to search for the corpse and had obtained permission from one Mr Ozo. I questioned why he would leave the hospital when a corpse under his care was missing.

My uncle later accompanied us to meet the chief medical director. He asked us to remain calm, assuring us that the corpse could not have disappeared and must be somewhere in the mortuary. He suggested that the chemicals used in the facility might have affected our eyes and that the hospital would bring out all the male corpses for us to identify my father.

We expected the management to treat the matter more seriously. Instead, they appeared unwilling to accept that the corpse could actually be missing. Their position was that it must be somewhere in the mortuary. We found this frustrating.

We subsequently reported the matter to the police at Zone 13, Ukpo, Anambra State.

What happened after you petitioned the police?

The matter was also taken to the State Criminal Investigation Department in Awka. We made efforts to locate Clement and get him to return to the hospital. We traced some of his relatives, and two people were eventually arrested. Clement returned after one of his relatives, who worked in a bank, was arrested.

When the mortician was questioned, he gave a different explanation. He claimed that he released my father’s corpse to one of our relatives.

Did he identify the relative he claimed to have handed the corpse to?

No. He could not identify the person. The hospital management began relying on that claim to suggest that we collected the corpse and were now pretending it was missing.

But how could a hospital employee claim to have released a corpse to a relative whose name he could not provide?

There is a procedure for releasing corpses to families. We were told by the hospital management, including the Chief Medical Director, that certain steps had to be followed before a body could be released. We did not sign any document confirming that we collected my father’s corpse because we did not see any.

The Reverend Sister also confirmed that there was no record showing that corpse number 331 was released or transferred. We therefore questioned how the mortician could claim to have released it without any documentation or identifiable recipient.

What did the police investigation establish?

The initial investigation at Zone 13 was not progressing as we expected. My uncle, who lives in Abuja, subsequently moved the matter to the Intelligence Response Team in Abuja, which began a fresh investigation.

The IRT came to Anambra State and arrested several people connected with the hospital and the mortuary. These included the mortician, the Reverend Sister, Mr Ozo and some other staff members. Even the security personnel who were on duty when the corpse allegedly disappeared were picked up for questioning.

The investigators also obtained statements from my stepmother and one of the men who helped drive my father to the hospital on the day he died.

During the investigation, I learnt that the police traced the contact of a native doctor in Abakaliki through Clement’s phone records. I do not know the full details of that aspect of the investigation.

The investigation lasted about three months, but what disappointed my uncle and the rest of us was that almost everyone arrested was eventually released, leaving only Clement.

We could not understand why the hospital was not being held accountable. We paid the institution to preserve my father’s corpse. He was in its custody, and we had not signed any document indicating that we had collected him.

The mortician was employed by the hospital. Even if he was directly responsible for whatever happened, the institution should explain how a corpse under its care could disappear without a trace.

We were dissatisfied with the way the investigation was handled and subsequently petitioned the Inspector-General of Police.

What is the current status of the investigation, and has the whereabouts of your father’s corpse been established?

Unfortunately, the police have not been able to give us any positive information about the whereabouts of the corpse. We are still waiting for answers.

We want those involved to be brought back for further investigation so they can explain what happened and, most importantly, produce our father’s body.

We also believe that everyone involved in the process should be investigated. The Reverend Sister may not have physically released the corpse, but she was involved in the mortuary’s operations. She reportedly gave Clement permission to leave the hospital despite knowing that a corpse was missing.

The security personnel who were on duty should also explain how the body could have been taken out of the hospital without clearance. Unless it was moved in a vehicle or ambulance, how did it leave the premises without anyone noticing?

We want everyone involved to come forward and tell us the truth. Our family deserves to know what happened to our father and where his corpse is. We deposited his body in the hospital’s custody, paid for its preservation and expected to retrieve it for burial. Instead, we are left searching for a corpse that should never have gone missing in the first place.
