COP31: Between climate promises and the unfinished business of development

By Punch Punch 6d ago 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

There is something almost predictable about the atmosphere surrounding international climate conferences. World leaders arrive with carefully crafted speeches,

Yet, beyond the diplomatic choreography, ordinary people whose livelihoods are threatened by climate change keep asking a simpler question: when will these promises translate into something that changes our lives?

This question has become particularly important as the 31st United Nations Climate Change Conference, COP31, enters its decisive phase here in Antalya, Turkiye. With the conference scheduled to close on November 20, the negotiations have moved into their second week, and the familiar disagreements between developed and developing countries are once again coming to the surface.

The COP31 is being hosted by Türkiye under a unique arrangement in which Australia leads the negotiations. Its central challenge is to move international climate cooperation from declarations towards implementation. So far, there has been progress, but the road to consensus remains difficult.

The negotiations leadership reported that countries had agreed on 48 items, while about 30 negotiating mandates remained outstanding as the second week began. These figures indicate movement in the negotiating process, but they should not be mistaken for 48 new, fully implemented climate commitments.

On November 16, the Presidency circulated the first draft of an overarching political decision, popularly known as the cover decision. This is an important development because it attempts to bring the conference’s various priorities together in one political framework. However, the document remains a negotiating draft, not a final agreement.

The real test of Antalya, therefore, is whether governments can overcome their differences and produce an outcome that recognises countries’ unequal capacities, vulnerabilities and development circumstances.

For developing countries, climate finance remains the most consequential issue at COP31. It is difficult to discuss adaptation, mitigation, technology transfer, or a just energy transition without confronting the question of who will pay for them.

At COP29 in Baku, countries agreed to a new climate-finance goal of at least $300bn annually for developing countries by 2035, alongside efforts to scale up finance from all sources to $1.3tn annually by the same year.

However, the developing world continues to question the adequacy, accessibility and delivery of these commitments. Many vulnerable countries argue that climate finance is either insufficient, arrives too slowly or comes in forms that deepen their debt burdens.

This is why African countries are insisting that climate finance must be predictable, accessible and substantially grant-based, particularly for adaptation.

In my mind, Nigeria must pay close attention to this debate. The country faces increasing climate-related expenditure arising from flooding, desertification, coastal erosion, agricultural disruption and extreme heat. These are not simply environmental inconveniences; they are threats to economic productivity, food security, public health and national development.

The challenge is to ensure that Nigeria’s climate-finance priorities are translated into well-designed, investment-ready projects capable of attracting international support.

Another difficult conversation concerns the future of fossil fuels. The draft political outcome includes competing formulations, including reaffirming the commitment to transition away from fossil fuels and stronger language proposing a phase-out. Countries remain divided over how far the final agreement should go.

For Africa, this discussion must be situated within the realities of energy poverty and industrial development. Nigeria, for instance, cannot discuss energy transition without considering electricity access, domestic energy security, employment, industrialisation and the economic implications of dependence on petroleum revenues.

This does not mean that Africa should abandon the global transition towards cleaner energy. Rather, it means the transition must be supported by adequate finance, technology transfer, infrastructure investment and credible arrangements to protect affected workers and communities.

A transition that leaves millions of people without affordable energy or alternative livelihoods cannot reasonably be described as just.

Perhaps the most consequential discussions for the African continent concern adaptation. While much of the global conversation has traditionally focused on reducing greenhouse-gas emissions, countries such as Nigeria are already confronting the consequences of a changing climate.

Communities are losing farms to floods, farmers are struggling with changing rainfall patterns, coastal settlements are threatened by erosion, and rising temperatures are creating additional pressures on public health and livelihoods.

The negotiations on the Global Goal on Adaptation have therefore attracted considerable attention. Developing countries are pressing for stronger adaptation finance, clearer indicators and a workable framework for measuring progress.

There is also a strong demand to triple adaptation finance by 2035, with countries debating the baseline, methodology and accountability arrangements needed to achieve this ambition.

For Nigeria, adaptation must become a practical development programme rather than another policy expression.

Our hydrological institutions, particularly the Nigeria Hydrological Services Agency, must be empowered to translate scientific information into early warning, community preparedness and bankable water-resilience projects.

The value of hydrological data is not merely in knowing how much rain has fallen or how high a river has risen. Its greatest value is in helping governments and communities anticipate danger, protect lives, safeguard infrastructure and plan their economic activities.

This is the connection between climate science and development that Nigeria must strengthen.

Another important development at COP31 is the continuing negotiation around a Just Transition Mechanism. The proposal is intended to strengthen international cooperation, technical assistance, capacity-building and knowledge-sharing to help countries navigate the social and economic implications of climate transition.

This discussion’s significance extends beyond the energy sector. For Nigeria, a just transition must include workers in the oil and gas industry, rural women, young people, informal-sector workers, farmers and communities whose economic activities depend on carbon-intensive industries.

It must also create new opportunities in renewable energy, clean cooking, sustainable agriculture, waste management, environmental restoration and green entrepreneurship.

Another issue that deserves greater prominence in Nigeria’s climate diplomacy is the relationship between climate change and public health. Extreme heat, changing disease patterns, flooding, water contamination and air pollution are already creating additional public-health challenges across the developing world.

Nigeria should, therefore, approach climate action as an integrated development agenda involving the environment, health, agriculture, water resources, energy and education.

Climate-resilient healthcare facilities, heat-action plans, disease surveillance, clean cooking, and reliable water infrastructure should become important components of the country’s adaptation priorities.

Similarly, food security must feature prominently in the Nigerian position. Climate-smart agriculture, indigenous farming knowledge, irrigation, improved agricultural forecasting and resilient food systems are essential to protecting livelihoods.

Beyond formal negotiations, another dimension of climate action deserves greater attention: culture. Having followed the Entertainment and Culture for Climate Action conversations at COP31, I am increasingly convinced that the global climate movement must do more to recognise the power of storytelling, indigenous knowledge, and cultural participation.

Climate change is not merely a scientific phenomenon. It is a human experience, shaped by how people relate to their environment, understand their traditions and organise their communities. Across Africa, indigenous communities have developed knowledge systems for protecting forests, conserving water, managing agricultural landscapes and living within ecological limits.

As COP31 approaches its final days, Nigeria must look beyond the excitement of international participation and ask what concrete national priorities should emerge from the conference. First, the country must strengthen its capacity to access international adaptation finance and develop credible climate-resilient infrastructure projects.

Second, it must integrate hydrological intelligence, early-warning systems and community preparedness into national and subnational development planning. Third, it must pursue a just energy transition that recognises the realities of energy poverty, employment and industrial development.

Fourth, it must connect climate action with public health, food security, biodiversity conservation and the protection of vulnerable communities. And thirdly, Nigeria must recognise culture, indigenous knowledge and public communication as essential instruments of climate implementation.

These priorities also provide an opportunity for stronger collaboration among universities, government institutions, civil society, development partners and the private sector. The emerging challenge is to build institutions and partnerships that can turn international commitments into measurable results. Antalya’s real success will be determined by what happens when delegates go home.
