Between boos, stones and seeking forgiveness

By Punch Punch 2d ago 6 min read

Are we to venerate President Bola Tinubu as beneficent and merciful for freeing 78 people who booed and stoned his convoy at the Area 1 mosque on the Friday before last?

The young men struck on November 13, as the President’s motorcade left Juma’at prayers at the Shehu Shagari Central Mosque in Garki. Police said several vehicles were damaged, some security personnel were injured, and 78 people were arrested.

By Monday, the President reportedly directed that the suspects be warned and released. Clemency is nobler than vengeance. But this could also be the fulfilment of a prophecy the President pronounced upon himself.

On April 16, while hosting his Renewed Hope Ambassadors at the Villa, Tinubu declared, “I took over from myself. The late Buhari is me.” If words mean anything, then anyone claiming to be Buhari, or an extension of him, should expect some of Buhari’s portion, including being stoned by the people. And 211 days later, that declaration materialised.

Were Buhari here, he might have drawn a shaken Tinubu under his comforting arm and whispered, “If they stoned me, they will stone you too. In this presidency, you shall have many tribulations…Nigerians are not easy to lead.” He has the experience to prove it.

On January 30, 2023, youths in Kano pelted Buhari’s convoy as he commissioned eight projects amid anger over fuel scarcity and the controversial new naira policy that left many Nigerians stranded. They set fires along the road and even threw pebbles at his helicopter as it hovered over them.

Buhari arrived at the Aminu Kano International Airport in the morning and was then flown by helicopter to the palace of the Emir of Kano, Aminu Bayero.

However, despite the heavy security presence mounted on the routes leading to Ahmadu Bello Way, where the President was to launch a Galaxy Backbone project, videos circulated online showed angry youths holding stones and sticks of various sizes, attacking his convoy and the helicopter believed to be conveying the President as it hovered above them.

That episode came only days after a similar outburst in his home state of Katsina, where youths stoned Buhari’s officials at the Kofar Kaura Underpass. They were chanting “Ba ma yi,” which literally means “We’re not doing,” and “Ba ma so,” meaning “We don’t want,” similar chants at the mosque that Friday before hailstones fell on Tinubu’s convoy.

Before Buhari, youths angry at the Boko Haram insurgency had stoned Goodluck Jonathan’s campaign convoy in 2015. Every president, it seems, gets his stones in one shape, form or velocity.

In August 2024, #EndBadGovernance protesters even lit a bonfire outside Buhari’s Daura home with the same chant, more than a year after he left office. Perhaps they too believed Buhari is Tinubu.

So that Friday, youths behind barriers shouted “Ba ma yi” and “Tinubu must go,” as they tore down APC posters across parts of Abuja. The President’s Senior Special Assistant on Digital/New Media Engagement, O’tega Ogra, denied that the President’s convoy was attacked, and dismissed the viral clips as misleading opposition propaganda involving “alleged paid thugs.” The aide posted short counter-footage of his principal waving at cheering worshippers from his vehicle. But the police later announced that stones actually flew and 78 arrests were made.

A day later in Jos, a section of the crowd at the APC North-Central rally booed the President’s convoy with the same chant. Video footage showed residents who lined the streets, pelting the president’s convoy with stones, shouting profanities and chanting, “Ba ma so.”

Other videos showed angry residents destroying billboards and campaign posters bearing Tinubu’s images. Residents were also filmed setting APC caps and brooms ablaze, with one woman saying she came only because she was paid N2,500. They asked for food items, not campaign caps and brooms, she said.

There at the Zaria Road Stadium, the President asked for forgiveness. Recalling his last visit, he admitted that he “was limited to the airport,” adding, “Forgive me if you didn’t see me in the hinterland.” Tinubu then promised to rebuild the ravaged communities and bring the displaced home. He also said the surveillance cameras promised to the state in April after the Palm Sunday killings had arrived in the country.

On April 2, days after gunmen killed at least 28 people in Angwan Rukuba on Palm Sunday, the President met bereaved families at the Yakubu Gowon Airport in Heipang, about a 40-minute drive from Jos. “You have no light at the airport, and I have to fly back within the next 10 minutes,” he had told them.

It was a neutral comment. And anyone could have said that to the people of Plateau. A local government chairman or a commissioner. But not a President. Understandably, his comments generated so much anger that some people rode with that anger to boo him in Jos.

You see, other presidents have faced worse than stones thrown at their convoys. President Emmanuel Macron, Tinubu’s close friend, has been slapped before and once had an egg plastered on his face.

In 2014, protesters pelted Czech President Miloš Zeman with eggs, tomatoes, and sandwiches. A year later, a woman threw a mango at former Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro that struck him in the head. The mango had a message written on it asking for housing assistance, which Maduro later granted.

Now, nothing justifies throwing stones or any objects at anyone, let alone a president. And I will never advocate violence of any sort against the person of the President. What we cannot ignore is this: when people don’t feel heard or considered, they may resort to extreme measures to get your attention.

Think mangoes to the head with a message written on them. The President’s mercy to the youths at the Garki mosque is commendable. Booing the President is a manageable way to communicate disapproval. But throwing stones or any objects is where we must draw the line. Mr President, I am sure you never saw this part coming when you danced for this job. It is part of the job nonetheless. I warned about this day over two years ago.

In my column, ‘Mr President, while the pigeons are still flying,’ written after the 2024 Armed Forces Remembrance Day, I wrote about the pigeon release ritual: “While the pigeons are still flying for him, the President must take up the challenge to ensure that the nation follows their upward path, soaring towards real prosperity for the everyday Nigerian, [not merely a swelling foreign reserve and other distant statistics].

If he rests on the symbolic gesture of the birds, assuming their flight represents the current state of Nigeria, he is missing the message. Their flight is a call to action, not a sign that all is well. The potential for progress exists, but it is far from realised. There are many grounds to cover. He must cover some of those grounds before his next encounter with the pigeons.”
