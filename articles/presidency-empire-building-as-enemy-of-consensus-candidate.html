Presidency: Empire-building as enemy of consensus candidate

By Punch Punch 18h ago 11 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

As a young secondary school boy of Ansar-Ud-Deen High School, Ikole Ekiti, Ekiti State (1978-1983), I loved History as a subject, a love the West African Examin

I was wildly fascinated by the factors responsible for the rise and fall of old kingdoms and empires in Africa and the incursion of fraudulent European exploiters and missionaries, nicely referred to as explorers. The alternate subject I could have registered for at the time was Government.

I felt Government was too simple. It didn’t offer me the stories of wars and suspense that usually excite young minds; no powerful names like Mansa Kankan Musa of the Mali Empire (reigned 1312—1337); the emergence of a powerful leader like Usman Dan Fodiyo, founder of the Sokoto Caliphate, whose great-great-grandparents migrated from Futa Toro along the Senegal River in the present-day Senegal and settled around Degel in the Gobir emirate in the North; and the Ekiti Parapo wars.

My beloved History equally taught me the origin and growth of the Yoruba Kingdom and the dispersal of the Oduduwa princes to far-flung places, including the Benin Kingdom and Dahomey (Republic of Benin). It brought to light how the Old Oyo Empire and Ilorin fell to the Fulani jihadists and warmongers in the 1830s (no be today) and the incursion of Islam to the fringes of present-day southern Nigeria.

Despite my love for History, I trailed a stubbornly-good Joseph Omolade, a classmate at AUD, to the finish line (the mock exam), where he led yours truly by half a mark (85 vs 85.5 percent). If it’s not painful, I won’t be telling you 43 years later.

Omolade’s sit-tight domination was not the only painful part in my final year at the AUD in 1983, a particularly troublesome set, hated by seniors, feared by juniors and troubled by teachers. I wasn’t really a person of particular concern in a set where you had students like Oscar Lee, who almost beat up the vice principal; Ballo, a leader of a partying gang who would normally play music while reading; and Kurry, who always chewed bitter kola to suppress other substances in his system which I don’t need to mention here.

As troublesome as he was, Oscar Lee still remembered my fine handwriting (a few actually referred to it as a printing press) over 40 years after our exit from AUD, Only the Best. What’s your own school’s motto jare?

Among our wonderful teachers, one particularly stood out, the English teacher, one Mr Ogunsan. If this man taught you English for two years and you still fumbled in WASCE, you need to consider a career in yam or akara frying, which had yet to be modernised by Senator Oluremi Tinubu then. Ogunsan’s one hour English class started at 6am with the aid of a gas lamp, after which you would need to endure the serially-monotonous threats of the vice principal on the assembly ground.

A few months before our WASCE, Ogunsan suddenly transformed into a villain, a traducer of a sort, who worked with a particularly acidic gusto to ensure the failure of about 200 young boys and girls in our set.

Some of us, the gentle ones, didn’t know our offence until it became known that our English teacher had equally been coaching one of our female classmates behind closed doors. Remember, there were no small girls in schools in those days like there are these days.

A testimony predictably came out of the private lecherous lessons as our mate became pregnant. Then the set became ostracised not just by Ogunsan but by other compromised teachers, many of whom stopped coming to class to perform their statutory duties so the innocent students could fail their final exams.

Of course, they knew we had been talking in hushed and open tones about the amorous secret affair that the Lord had brought into the open. That’s our unpardonable offence. Today, the AUD ’83 set remembers all of you as the weapons temporarily fashioned against the set’s success like yesterday. I’m happy to tell all the colluding teachers (dead and alive) that your weapons failed to prosper.

Let’s leave aside my great school and the negative legacies of some teachers who unfortunately are many in different institutions in our modern society, but it’s equally important to tell their own history as well before we return to the history of the past kingdoms.

One thing that remained constant in all these empires and their rise and fall was the inseparable link between the kingdoms and their all-powerful emperors. The kings were as powerful as their kingdoms, if not more powerful, except in the old Oyo Empire, where there was a clearly-defined governmental structure that may shame modern-day political set-up in the land.

Among the contemporary political strategies in our clime is the never-ending plan of the opposition parties to field a consensus presidential candidate to battle the All Progressives Congress flag bearer, President Bola Tinubu, in the January 16, 2027election. In my past commentary on this novel plan, I alluded to the fact that the presidential contest in any nation cannot be compared to a primary school’s inter-house sport. It appears, however, that as the reported plot by the opposition elements gathers momentum daily, the more flippant and impracticable the plan looks.

You may wish to recall that in April this year, shortly before the political parties picked their presidential candidates, the opposition camps gathered at the Government House, Ibadan, for the National Summit of All Opposition Parties and came up with the famous (really?) Ibadan Declaration meant to produce a consensus presidential candidate to battle the APC in January.

Among the over a dozen parties at this Ibadan Summit were the African Democratic Congress, Nigeria Democratic Congress, Peoples Democratic Party, Allied Peoples Movement, Social Democratic Party, Labour Party, Accord, New Nigeria People’s Party, and People’s Redemption Party. Attended by top chiefs of the parties, great speeches were equally delivered on the woes of the country and the serial offences of Tinubu at the expense of planning how a consensus presidential candidate would emerge.

The futility of the Ibadan talk show expectedly manifested just about a month after the jamboree as the various opposition parties separately went about picking their presidential candidates against the seemingly gentleman’s agreement reached in Ibadan.

I must admit that at the moment, the opposition parties have serious cause to worry about their fortunes in the January election. On the current template, I can tell you for free that none of the major parties can match the strength of the Bola Tinubu-led APC in the presidential contest if the opposition parties run separately. Serious challengers like the ADC and the NDC lack the spread, depth, financial capacity and structure to unseat the incumbent president in any national electoral contest.

I’m not asking you to agree with my amateurish postulations, but that does not stop me from telling you. The NDC presidential candidate, Mr Peter Obi, whose candidacy resonates well with the younger population, the biggest demographic sector in Nigeria, is at best a regional champion. Obi will top the chart in the five states of the South-East; he will do very well in the South-South. After the landslide victory in the South, fuel go dry.

No candidate can win the presidential election in Nigeria on regional votes. Muhammadu Buhari’s efforts in 2003, 2007 and 2011 were based on northern votes,which couldn’t get him the Presidency. You can check Obi’s votes in the last election when he ran for the same office in 2023 on the platform of the Labour Party, largely in the South-East.

While Obi has every right to contest the presidency, the ADC presidential candidate, former Vice President Atiku Abubakar, if he has any regard for the thin thread holding this nation together, shouldn’t have ventured to contest the presidential election either in 2023 or in 2027.

In the Nigerian political calendar, the Presidency is zoned into two regions – the North and the South – not six geo-political zones. After the eight years of Buhari, a Fulani man from the North, it is inconceivable that another Fulani man from the North, regardless of political affiliation, would nurse the ambition of succeeding the retired general. As how now? Military regimes ended in Nigeria in 1999.

Are we saying this man is sensitive to the political temperature of this country? Atiku’s ambition smacks of political arrogance; it is unreasonable; it is reckless; it is dangerous; it is unjustifiable, and actually a threat to the unity of Nigeria.

In case you have forgotten, Atiku’s unjustifiable presidential ambition tore apart the PDP after the 2023 general elections. I laughed most of the time whenever some political commentators attribute the collapse of the once-ruling party to the Minister of the Federal Capital Territory, Mr Nyesom Wike’s unending fight with another faction loyal to Atiku.

Against the party agreement zoning the PDP presidential ticket to the South, Atiku insisted on contesting the ticket, which he won. Wike, along with the Oyo State governor, Seyi Makinde, and three other governors who opposed Atiku’s bid, vowed to ensure the victory of a southern presidential candidate, a pledge they accomplished. Having realised that his unbridled ambition would never be achieved on the platform of a fragmented PDP, Atiku sought refuge in the ADC, where he is currently battling to realise his lifetime ambition.

One can’t, however, dismiss Atiku’s ambition and electoral value essentially based on desperation rather than equity. In spite of the ex-Vice President’s war chest and the serial intrigues he has continued to instigate in the United States (as if the US will conduct Nigeria’s elections), Atiku remains a regional champion. He will do well in about 15 states in the North but will struggle in the South-West, the South-East and the South-South.

The opposition parties, faced with the above reality, have realised the need to ‘forge a common front’ by presenting a single presidential candidate. Despite his political separation from Wike, Makinde has maintained that the South must complete its eight years of Presidency. So, Atiku’s candidacy is out of his consideration as a consensus pick. Unfortunately, the Oyo State governor, who is running in the presidential race for a personal, misdirected motive, can’t clinch the opposition’s ticket.

Obi, who is in a more stable political environment than the former vice president at the moment, won’t step down for Atiku in order not to destroy his political investments, in spite of his shortcomings and the controversies surrounding his performance as the two-term governor of Anambra State.

I can tell you with all the confidence in me that Atiku won’t step down for anyone as the presidential flag bearer on the opposition’s consensus platform. To the former number two man during Olusegun Obasanjo’s Presidency, occupying Aso Villa as President is a lifetime project which he can’t defer until 2031 when he might be adjudged to be ‘too old’.

You may not agree with my proposition that the black man is essentially an empire builder, not a nation builder, who first of all ensures that his interests are protected in every step to be taken, regardless of how such actions affect others. You may check your street, where you have mansions owned by the high and mighty, and where you have very deplorable roads leading to the community. Does that tell you anything?

It is strange that the opposition thinks it can adopt the APC-styled template in its plans to field a consensus presidential candidate about 53 days to the contest. The APC started its groundwork for a merger among different political parties, featuring many sitting governors, two years before the 2015 polls. Does this template look like the one that can be adopted by the current stock of opposition parties? I doubt this.

It appears the opposition parties may need a miracle worker not just to find a consensus presidential candidate within a month but to win the presidential election in January 2027 against an incumbent President and a party which currently parades 34 governors as supporters. At the moment, only the governors of Oyo and Bauchi states, who are both in the APM, are not supporting Tinubu’s re-election.
