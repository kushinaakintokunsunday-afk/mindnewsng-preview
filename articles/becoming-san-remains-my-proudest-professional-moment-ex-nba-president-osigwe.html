Becoming SAN remains my proudest professional moment – Ex-NBA president, Osigwe

By Punch Punch 3d ago 18 min read
MINDNEWSNG

Senior Advocate of Nigeria and former President of the Nigerian Bar Association, Afam Osigwe, speaks to PELUMI KAZEEM about his childhood, journey into the legal profession, challenges, personal interests and concerns about Nigeria’s judiciary and electoral system

What was growing up like for you?

I grew up in Nnewi North Local Government Area of Anambra State. I come from a very large family. My mother had nine sons, while my stepmother had one, so it was a big family.

I grew up largely in a trading community. My father was a trader, while my mother was a housewife who also had a shop within our family premises. It was an environment where very few people went to school, and trading was one of the major occupations.

However, despite the importance of trading in the community, our parents wanted their children to be educated. Encouraging children to go to school and discouraging them from dropping out after primary or secondary school were important values in our community. Most of the schools were built through communal efforts. The only general hospital in my hometown at the time was also built through communal effort, as was the post office. There were churches belonging to the Catholic and Anglican denominations.

That was the kind of environment in which I grew up before proceeding to Dennis Memorial Grammar School, Onitsha, for my secondary education. From there, I went on to study Law at the University of Nigeria, Nsukka.

What values did your upbringing instil in you that shaped the person you are today?

My parents instilled in us the values of hard work, diligence and honesty. In fact, my father’s business motto was, “Honesty is the best policy.” Lies were not tolerated in our home, and we were always made to take responsibility for our actions.

We were also taught not to depend on other people but to work hard, become independent and be able to provide for ourselves and our families. Excelling in whatever we did was very important.

My father drove from Nnewi to Onitsha every weekday for his trading business. I remember him always quoting a portion of the Bible which says, “A little sleep, a little slumber, and a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come on you like a thief.” His message was that if you did not want to be poor, you had to work hard and take whatever you were doing seriously.

As a student, I always remembered those words. Even when I became a lawyer and started working, the importance of hard work and making good use of my time remained at the back of my mind.

When I was leaving for Abuja in 1999 to establish myself there, one of the pieces of advice I received was never to postpone what I could do that day until another time. I was also advised not to allow my plans or ideas to become stale, but to remain focused and do things that would not bring shame to my family or me.

These are some of the values from my upbringing that have influenced the person I am today and continue to guide me as I strive to improve in whatever I do.

Was becoming a lawyer something you had always dreamed of?

I never thought I would become a lawyer. In fact, I never wanted to be one. In a manner of speaking, I would say that law happened to me by accident because, when I ran out of course options to study, it became the only other choice available to me.

As a child, my dream was to become a medical doctor. However, when I got to secondary school, I struggled with Mathematics and the sciences. By the time I completed SS1, it had become obvious that I had no future in the sciences because I found it difficult to understand some of the subjects. On the other hand, I was doing very well in my arts subjects.

I sought advice, and it became clear that medicine was no longer an option. During my SS3 year, I took what we called Trial JAMB to prepare for the actual examination, and I chose Mass Communication. I did not tell my parents or siblings about my decision.

When the result arrived at the post office, my father collected and opened it. He was surprised by the subjects I had written because they were mainly arts subjects. I explained that I had struggled with the sciences and decided to switch to the arts.

I had developed a great admiration for journalism from an early age. I started reading newspapers quite early, enjoyed listening to the news and watching newscasters, particularly the older presenters. I thought journalism was something I would love to do, having given up on my initial dream of becoming a medical doctor.

However, my father was disappointed because he had been excited about my ambition to study medicine. He told me he would not dictate the course I should study, but he knew he would not pay for me to study Mass Communication. According to him, if I wanted to study any course, I should choose a professional one that would enable me to stand on my own after graduation.

At the time, my elder brother had graduated from the University of Jos and had been called to the Bar in 1987. He had returned to Nnewi to establish his legal practice and was doing quite well. I respected him and thought it would not be a bad idea to study Law after all.

That was how I gradually shifted my attention to Law. It had never featured in my plans, but when I gained admission and started attending lectures, I realised that I could not have studied anything else. I became increasingly convinced that Law was my calling.

Looking back, I believe God directed me to where I was meant to be, and I am grateful that I eventually chose to study Law.

Looking back at your journey, what would you describe as the biggest turning point in your career?

It is difficult to identify just one turning point, but I would say that getting the opportunity to work with the late Chief T.J. Onomigbo Okpoko, SAN, was a major starting point.

Although I worked with him for less than two years, the experience broadened my understanding of the opportunities available in the legal profession and the kind of services one could provide. It also exposed me to the importance of identifying specific areas of practice, developing expertise and striving to become excellent in them.

At the time, his practice was growing, and I had the opportunity to witness different aspects of commercial transactions, litigation and cross-border transactions. These experiences widened my horizons and gave me a better understanding of what I could achieve as a lawyer.

I also encountered several people who had distinguished themselves in the profession, and I learnt from their experiences. So, I would say that the different people I met and the opportunities I had along the way contributed to the turning points in my professional journey.

You established your law firm, LAW FORTE, in 2002. What inspired you to start your own practice?

After working with the late Chief T.J. Onomigbo Okpoko for some time, I decided it was time to establish my own practice.

It was not easy initially. Clients were not readily available, and there were times when I had the capacity to work but did not have enough work coming in. There were also situations where I had work to do, but the fees people were willing to pay were inadequate.

Interestingly, I got married in 2002, and on March 3, 2003, I became a father. So, I was also dealing with the responsibility of raising a young family while trying to establish my practice.

What challenges did you face?

Some of the initial challenges were inadequate capital, attracting clients, identifying the right areas of focus and building the right team. However, God was faithful, and we continued to grow gradually.

Like every business, establishing a law firm comes with challenges, but we were able to overcome them little by little. You must continue to build your client base, find the right people to work with and create an environment that encourages growth.

You also have to keep improving yourself through training, reading and seeking the opinions of others. Sometimes, you need to form partnerships with other law firms and collaborate with them to provide services to clients.

Life is a race, and there are times when you feel exhausted. However, you must keep going if you want to cross the finish line and succeed at whatever you have set out to do.

After many years in the legal profession and serving as NBA President, what has been your most fulfilling experience?

My most fulfilling professional experience was the day I was conferred with the rank of Senior Advocate of Nigeria.

When I first arrived in Abuja, I came in on a night bus and initially squatted with friends and colleagues in Gwarinpa before I was able to get my own accommodation in Karu. When I reflect on where I was coming from and the struggles involved in building my practice, becoming a Senior Advocate remains particularly significant to me.

When I started practising law, I never imagined that I would become a Senior Advocate. Initially, I just wanted to make a living from the profession, establish a law firm, attract enough clients to sustain myself and save something for the future.

Some people might expect me to say that becoming NBA President was my most fulfilling experience, but becoming a Senior Advocate remains the most important professional moment for me. It represented recognition of the efforts and sacrifices I had made in the profession.

What did the journey teach you about yourself?

When I worked with the late Chief T.J. Onomigbo Okpoko, I saw how hard he worked to attain the rank. I also encountered other lawyers who had distinguished themselves through hard work and excellence. That made me realise it was an achievement worth pursuing.

When it eventually happened, it reminded me that God had blessed me beyond my expectations.

It reminded me that every profession rewards those who work hard and strive for excellence.

What is one thing people often misunderstand about you because of your position and achievements?

It would be difficult to assume that people will never misunderstand you. Even people who have not achieved much in life will have others who misunderstand them. Sometimes, it may be about one’s attitude, as people might consider you arrogant or have other perceptions about you.

I remember people telling me that I was overambitious and asking whether I was not too young to aspire to become NBA President. When I contested for the position in 2018, I was disqualified. I tried to explain to some people that I did not consider myself too young for the position.

I pointed out that there had been people who became heads of state in their 30s and governors in Nigeria in their 40s. So, I did not understand why aspiring to become NBA President at my age should be considered overambitious.

There will always be people who misunderstand you, just as you may not always understand other people. I do not allow such perceptions to worry me.

What matters to me is remaining faithful to my moral standards, personal beliefs and principles. I am committed to doing what is right rather than acting out of self-interest or simply trying to please people.

I also believe in working for the greater good of society and whichever profession I find myself in.

Having achieved so much in your career, what are some of the things you still hope to accomplish?

I do not think I have achieved everything I want to achieve. I want to continue becoming a better person than I was the previous day and accomplish more, although I know that, at some point, I will have to retire.

Professionally, I have attained some of the highest positions in the legal profession. I am a Senior Advocate of Nigeria and a life member of the Body of Benchers. I also had the opportunity to serve as President of the Nigerian Bar Association.

I have no interest in contesting for another elective position in the profession. However, if an opportunity for a political appointment that I believe I can credibly discharge comes my way, I may consider it. I do not intend to contest for political office, and I do not belong to any political party.

Beyond personal achievements, I want to see Nigeria become stronger, the legal profession improve and the judiciary become an institution that enjoys the confidence and respect of the people who depend on it.

Some Nigerians believe that money and other interests sometimes influence court judgments. What is your response to these concerns?

I think there may be some truth to those concerns. We operate in a political environment where judges may sometimes feel compelled to consider the implications of their decisions, particularly when the government is involved. For instance, a judge may feel that he cannot make an order or deliver a judgment that could affect the government, even without anyone approaching or inducing him.

There have also been proven cases of judges receiving money to influence decisions in cases before them. The general perception among members of the public is that money and other interests play a role in the administration of justice.

However, we must recognise that perception can sometimes be greater than reality. People may hold certain beliefs without necessarily having evidence to support them. As I have mentioned, there have been cases where the National Judicial Council sanctioned judicial officers over allegations of corruption and misconduct.

There are also situations where a judgment appears to go against established judicial reasoning, the facts or the law. When I was NBA President, I consistently emphasised that when a judge delivers a judgment that is inconsistent with established judicial precedents, the facts and the applicable law, it could be a product of corruption or incompetence.

Whichever of the two it is, such a person has no business remaining on the Bench.

In recent years, some court judgments have affected the outcomes of elections. Do you think this has reduced Nigerians’ confidence in the judiciary and the electoral process?

I think so. There is an over-judicialisation of the political process, and I have always advocated reducing the involvement of courts in determining who becomes a candidate for a political party.

In my view, political parties should be allowed to decide who they want to field as candidates, and the choice of candidates should not be justiciable. One of the things that has contributed to the negative perception of the judiciary in Nigeria is the number of political cases that end up in court.

If we get the electoral process right and reduce the number of disputes that go before election tribunals, public perception of the judiciary will improve. Similarly, reducing the involvement of courts in determining election winners could help restore confidence in the system.

I also believe that some of these judicial decisions have contributed to the feeling among Nigerians that their votes do not count. When people begin to believe that election outcomes are already determined, they may see little reason to participate in the voting process.

There are other factors contributing to this problem. Politicians sometimes prevent opposition parties from freely participating in the electoral process. In several states, you find billboards belonging mainly to the ruling party, while members of the opposition face difficulties putting up theirs. The high charges for erecting billboards may also be used to discourage opposition activities, particularly where there is no evidence that members of the ruling party pay the same amounts.

We also have issues of electoral violence, intimidation, the high cost of acquiring political power and the monetisation of the electoral process. There is also the question of whether Nigerians have sufficient confidence in INEC as an independent umpire.

Another issue is the restriction of vehicular movement on election days. I do not think it makes sense to restrict people’s movements because an election is taking place. Imagine someone whose polling unit is two or three kilometres from their home being expected to walk that distance simply because they want to vote.

People should be allowed to go about their normal activities and vote if they choose to. Voting should not mean that people’s lives must come to a standstill. In many countries, people go about their daily activities while participating in elections.

Restricting movement does not necessarily prevent electoral malpractice, and it discourages some people who may not be willing or able to walk long distances from voting. When combined with a lack of confidence in the government and the electoral process, these factors contribute to low voter turnout.

Do you think there is a need to review Nigeria’s electoral laws to reduce the number of election disputes that end up in court?

I think pre-election matters should not be justiciable. If a political party decides that the least popular person is the candidate it wants to field, it should be allowed to do so without the courts determining who its candidate should be.

I believe the provisions of the Electoral Act that make these matters justiciable should be removed. Political parties should be allowed to succeed or fail based on their decisions.

When an electoral process is seen to be fair, people are more likely to accept its outcome without going to court. I recall attending the Osun governorship election, where the outcome was generally expected, and those who lost did not challenge it.

However, when people believe that the system can be manipulated or that the outcome does not reflect their will, they will continue to seek redress in court.

Nigeria may be one of the countries where electoral disputes are heavily litigated. That does not present us in a positive light because it suggests that we have not built an electoral system that is sufficiently transparent and credible.

As a former NBA President, what role can the association play in protecting the independence of the judiciary?

The NBA, like other civil society organisations, has a responsibility to hold institutions and public office holders accountable, speak truth to power, protect the independence of the judiciary and the legal profession, and insist that the rule of law prevails.

If the association consistently carries out these responsibilities, it will play an important role in protecting judicial independence.

It must also engage in advocacy, put pressure on the government and draw attention to the poor state of infrastructure in our courts. The NBA should insist on the financial autonomy of the judiciary so that Chief Judges and heads of courts do not have to approach the executive to beg for funding.

There is also a need to protect the appointment process for judicial officers from political interference and ensure that the right people are appointed. Those appointed as judges must equally maintain their independence and avoid surrendering themselves to political authorities.

Judges must deliver decisions that inspire confidence, not only by resolving disputes between parties but also by ensuring that justice is served.

When this happens, people will have greater confidence in the judiciary. Despite the interdependence of the arms of government, the judiciary must remain independent and be able to make decisions without fear or favour.

Away from work and professional responsibilities, how would you describe yourself?

When I am away from work, I enjoy reading and sightseeing. I also used to do photography, although I have not practised it in recent years.

I keep animals, particularly dogs, because I love them. I have a few dogs at home, and I enjoy feeding and grooming them and spending time with them.

What do you enjoy doing in your free time?

I enjoy being around nature. I do not drink or smoke, and I am not particularly a club-going person. I enjoy the company of friends and having intellectual discussions with them or my colleagues.

Sometimes, I simply lie in bed doing nothing or sit at home and watch a movie.

I am not particularly a music person, although I listen to music occasionally. Interestingly, I do not like working in a completely quiet environment. When I am working at home or in the office, I prefer having some background noise, either from the television or music playing on my laptop. I enjoy reading and watching movies more.
