I sought AI’s guidance when existing theories didn’t fit – 89-year-old Nwagwu

By Punch Punch 1d ago 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Eighty-nine-year-old Professor Mark Nwagwu has disclosed how he turned to artificial intelligence when existing social theories failed to provide a suitable fra

Nwagwu, who recently earned another PhD from the University of Ibadan, said AI suggested that he could develop a speculative theory after he struggled to find an existing social theory that adequately explained his research.

The professor, who began the doctoral programme at the age of 86, disclosed this in an interview with The PUNCH.

He said, “I went to social theories, one by one, but not one of them suited my work. Eventually, I went to AI to ask, ‘I don’t have a theory for my work, what do I do?’ It said I could develop a speculative theory. It’s in my thesis.”

According to him, AI also contributed to some of the justifications for his research, particularly as he explored how his findings could be applied to society.

“If you read my thesis, there are four or five justifications for this. Why did I do this? A lot of those justifications come from AI,” he said.

Nwagwu, however, maintained that the Cultural Stem Cell Theory was an original contribution arising from his research, explaining that the artificial intelligence system had not previously encountered the particular work.

He said the theory, which brings together biology, sociology and the humanities, was developed from his study of Nsibidi, an indigenous African system of communication through gestures, signs, movements and other non-verbal expressions.

The professor explained that his research examined how the interaction between different elements could produce new forms of understanding and transformation, drawing an analogy from the biological functions of stem cells.

He said, “The important point in the stem cell theory is communication, interaction and change. You are no longer what you once were because you are now interacting and you produce something innovative.”

Nwagwu explained that his interest in Nsibidi developed following a conversation with former Nigeria’s Ambassador to Brazil, Bolivia and Paraguay, Christopher Okeke, who challenged him to consider the importance of understanding one’s cultural origins.

He said Okeke’s observation that people needed to understand where they were coming from to know where they were going inspired him to undertake research into Nsibidi.

Although he initially intended to write a book on the subject, he decided to pursue a doctoral degree because he considered Nsibidi a specialised social discipline that required proper academic investigation.

He subsequently approached Professor Sola Olorunyomi of the University of Ibadan to supervise the research, while Okeke supported the project with a monthly stipend.

Nwagwu said the research eventually produced the Cultural Stem Cell Theory, which he hoped to apply to communities to promote interaction, understanding and unity across cultural and social boundaries.

He added that his examiner described the thesis as innovative and multidisciplinary, recommending that the postgraduate college recognise the work for bringing different academic disciplines together and proposing a theory with potential applications in society.

He described the decision to undertake the doctorate as unexpected, saying, “I did not set out to do a PhD. Fortunately, I have done it. And a theory has come out of it: Cultural Stem Cell Theory.”

The professor explained that Nsibidi was a non-verbal system of communication involving gestures, eye movements, dances and other forms of expression through which people conveyed meanings without spoken words.

“Nsibidi is the heart of a culture in which the human mind comes out not in words but in gestures, in eye movements, in dances, in every possible way of expression except words,” he said.

He argued that the interaction between Nsibidi and biological concepts provided the foundation for his theory, which draws on the ability of stem cells to develop into different forms depending on their environment.

“The stem cell is that part of embryology, that part of development that can grow into anything depending on where it finds its location,” he said.

According to him, the theory is built around communication, interaction and transformation, with the potential to explain how different individuals and communities can interact and produce new forms of social understanding.

“The important point in the stem cell theory is communication, interaction and change. You are no longer what you once were because you are now interacting and you produce something innovative,” he said.

On concerns about the growing influence of artificial intelligence, Nwagwu said knowledge could be dangerous depending on how it was used, comparing AI’s potential benefits and risks with the development of nuclear weapons and drones.

He argued that the same knowledge that produced weapons could also be deployed for beneficial purposes, adding that the challenge was ensuring that technological advancements were properly guided.

“Knowledge is dangerous. In itself, it’s wonderful. If we use it, that’s what we are using,” he said.

The professor, however, acknowledged that AI had made complex tasks easier, particularly in analysis, but questioned whether it could ultimately be regarded as the highest expression of wisdom.

He said, “AI has made complex things simple. How it can analyse, et cetera. We can ask the question, where are human beings going? Is AI the ultimate scope of wisdom? Is AI wisdom? You can say, well, we do not know until we put it to use properly.”

Nwagwu also warned that the technology could influence language and moral values, stressing the need to guide its applications.

On the commercialisation of research findings, he supported efforts to translate academic discoveries into products and services but cautioned against allowing the pursuit of money, wealth and professional recognition to undermine the broader benefits of research.

Citing the development of the COVID-19 vaccine and the discovery of insulin, he argued that researchers should be willing to make useful discoveries available for the benefit of humanity rather than prioritising personal financial gains.

Turning to the Nigerian education sector, the professor identified declining discipline as one of its major challenges, arguing that the country’s education system had deteriorated from the standards he experienced in the 1950s and 1960s.

He attributed much of the decline to the military’s intervention in governance following the 1966 coup, saying the period marked a turning point in the country’s institutions, including its schools.

According to him, discipline and academic standards were once central to educational institutions, with students who failed examinations facing the possibility of being withdrawn.

He lamented the deterioration of infrastructure and standards in public schools and universities, adding that the increasing preference for foreign education reflected declining confidence in Nigeria’s educational system.

Nwagwu called for a restoration of discipline and order in schools, arguing that academic qualifications alone were insufficient to produce responsible leaders.

Despite his latest academic achievement, the professor said he did not set out to pursue another PhD, describing the decision to study Nsibidi as a development that unfolded through divine direction.

He said he began the programme at 86 and was delighted to have completed the research and produced what he considered a significant contribution to knowledge.

Nwagwu also said he was not interested in ranking his achievements, stressing that cooperation, interaction and the ability to contribute positively to society were more important than personal recognition.

He said he hoped to take the Cultural Stem Cell Theory beyond academic research by applying its principles to different communities to encourage mutual understanding and social transformation.

“My cultural stem cell theory is big and will take me places. But those places will be the work of the Holy Spirit. How I do it will be the work of the Holy Spirit,” he said.
