Atiku, Obi, Tinubu: The race for Trump’s blessing, by Emmanuel Aziken

By Vanguard Vanguard 3d ago 5 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Vanguard

Despite his party’s bruising defeat in the US midterm elections a few weeks ago, President Donald Trump remains a powerful attraction in the political calculati

The scramble for American recognition was particularly conspicuous this week, with President Bola Tinubu, former Vice-President Atiku Abubakar and former Anambra State Governor Peter Obi engaging American political, media and policy circles in a determined effort to shape their international profiles.

The loudest signal came from the Tinubu camp, which released photographs of the President with American political strategist and media personality, Diana London. The encounter was initially presented as an interview but subsequently assumed a different character following criticism from Atiku’s camp, with London clarifying that it was intended to provide material for a documentary.

London had earlier taken to social media to praise the security partnership between Nigeria and the United States under Tinubu and Trump, describing the relationship as productive and capable of unlocking significant economic and trade opportunities. She also described Nigeria as America’s greatest ally in Africa.

But what appeared to be a publicity opportunity for the Presidency quickly became a political controversy when Atiku questioned the circumstances surrounding the engagement and demanded the release of the complete recording.

In a statement issued through his Director of Strategic Communication, Phrank Shaibu, the former Vice-President questioned whether the engagement was connected to a $4.5 million lobbying arrangement involving the Nigerian government and American firm DCI Group AZ.

Atiku cited US Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) filings identifying London as a consultant to the firm, reportedly engaged by the Nigerian government to enhance its image in America.

Although he acknowledged that the filings did not establish that Tinubu’s interview formed part of the services covered by the lobbying contract, he argued that the relationship raised questions requiring clarification.

He also questioned a large screen reportedly positioned within the President’s line of sight during the recording, suggesting that it might have contained prepared prompts. He challenged Tinubu to submit himself to a live, unscripted interview with Nigerian journalists, including Rufai Oseni, Ojy Okpe, Seun Okinbaloye and Reuben Abati.

London subsequently clarified the nature of her engagement with Tinubu, insisting that she had travelled to Nigeria to interview the President for portions of a documentary rather than for a conventional media assignment.

In a statement posted on her X account on Wednesday, November 18, 2026, she categorically denied receiving $4.5 million from the Nigerian government or anyone else in Nigeria.

“I unequivocally deny the claim that Nigeria’s government nor anyone else in Nigeria paid me $4.5 million. I went to Nigeria to interview President Tinubu @officialABAT for parts of a documentary and see Nigeria for myself,” she wrote.

She also dismissed the allegations surrounding her visit as a smear campaign.

Her clarification, however, has not entirely extinguished the controversy. While London denied personally receiving the alleged $4.5 million, FARA filings reportedly identify her as a consultant to DCI Group. Reports have also indicated that London Global Strategies, her company, received payments from the lobbying firm under the arrangement.

The distinction between payments to an individual and payments to a company is important. But it does not eliminate the need for transparency, particularly where a government-funded lobbying engagement and a presidential documentary interview appear in the same frame.

Yet, Atiku’s own relationship with Washington’s lobbying industry adds another dimension to the controversy.

In April 2026, reports based on US government filings revealed that he had engaged Washington-based lobbying firm Von Batten-Montague-York under a 12-month contract valued at $1.2 million.

The agreement provided for strategic advisory services, congressional and executive branch engagement, and efforts to strengthen his reputation among American policymakers. It also identified counterbalancing the Nigerian government’s lobbying narratives and advancing understanding of Atiku’s leadership posture and policy vision as objectives.

OBI’S WASHINGTON OUTREACH

Obi was equally active in the Washington theatre.

The former Anambra governor arrived in the American capital on Monday, November 16, for meetings with political figures, including members of the US House of Representatives and Senate.

He subsequently released photographs of his engagements, listing Congressman Marlin Stutzman, Congresswoman Beth Van Duyne, former Congressman Todd Tiahrt and other influential personalities among those he met.

According to Obi, the discussions focused on Nigeria’s development, security, economic prosperity, citizens’ welfare and democratic future. He also disclosed that he granted an interview to American television commentator Glenn Beck.

However, photographs and favourable remarks from some American personalities generated controversy, with suggestions in some quarters that Obi had secured congressional endorsement for his presidential ambition.

Karl Von Batten, the US-based representative of Atiku’s lobbying firm, challenged that interpretation, insisting that the gathering was a private engagement and did not constitute an endorsement by the US Congress.

Obi was quick to dismiss any suggestion that he had sought or secured an endorsement from the US government, Congress or individual American lawmakers.

He maintained that his visit was intended to discuss Nigeria’s development and democratic challenges.

THE WASHINGTON FACTOR

Taken together, the activities of Tinubu, Atiku and Obi reveal an emerging dimension of the 2027 presidential contest: the determination of leading political figures to shape perceptions of their leadership credentials beyond Nigeria’s borders.

For Tinubu, the priority appears to be projecting his administration as a strategic partner of the Trump government. Atiku is seeking to counter the government’s international narrative and present an alternative leadership proposition to American policymakers. Obi, meanwhile, is using political and media engagements to project his development-oriented message.

There is an irony in three presidential hopefuls competing for American attention while millions of Nigerians remain preoccupied with insecurity, unemployment, poverty, inflation and the rising cost of living.

Ultimately, the measure of presidential leadership will not be the warmth of its reception in Washington but its capacity to earn the confidence of Nigerians.
