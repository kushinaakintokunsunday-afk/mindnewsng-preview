Manufacturers, frozen food dealers groan as blackouts persist

By Punch Punch 3d ago 8 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

Manufacturers, cold-room operators and frozen food dealers are counting losses as persistent power outages force businesses to rely on generators, raising opera

Findings by Saturday PUNCH show that prolonged blackouts in parts of the country have compelled many small and medium-scale enterprises to spend heavily on diesel and petrol to keep running.

Frozen food operators who spoke with Saturday PUNCH also lamented that inadequate electricity had caused the spoilage of goods worth millions of naira.

According to data obtained from nationalgrid.ng on Thursday evening, the country generated 257.2MW, representing an 89.1 per cent drop.

Businesses count losses

An official of a beverage manufacturing company in the Agbara industrial area of Ogun State, Femi Adeagbo, told Saturday PUNCH on condition of anonymity that persistent electricity disruptions had cut the company’s daily production capacity by about 40 per cent while sharply raising operating costs.

He said the Ibadan Electricity Distribution Company promised companies in the industrial zone between 20 and 22 hours of electricity daily, but they currently received less than 10 hours of supply.

He lamented that the situation had forced many companies to rely on gas-powered systems and diesel generators to keep production lines running.

He added that many companies along the axis were struggling to meet production targets because of unreliable electricity supply.

He explained that the disruptions had reduced his company’s daily output from an expected 10,000 cases of beverages to about 6,000 cases.

“It has had a major impact on our operations. No company can survive under the current state of Nigeria’s power system unless urgent steps are taken to address the challenges,” he said.

He disclosed that the company spent about N130m monthly on energy, while diesel expenditure alone stood at between N16m and N20m monthly for weekend operations.

“When we operate on gas from Monday to Friday and switch to diesel during the weekends, we spend between N8m and N10m on diesel weekly. That amounts to approximately N16m to N20m monthly on diesel for weekend operations alone.

“We have not even factored in what we would spend if we had to rely on diesel during the weekdays. The cost would certainly be much higher,” he added.

He said the added energy costs, equipment maintenance and lower production output had squeezed expected profit margins.

He added that the company was considering further diversification into solar energy to reduce reliance on the national grid, noting that some major manufacturers, including Nigerian Breweries and Seven-Up, were also exploring alternative energy sources.

A cold-room manager at Olomore Market, Abeokuta, Ogun State, Odedina Olaide, told Saturday PUNCH that operators had struggled to stay afloat since September when electricity supply worsened.

She said her business spent at least N218,400 daily on diesel to buy about 120 litres at N1,820 per litre.

She disclosed that the cold-room had spent more than N10m on diesel over the past two months, yet generators could not match the freezing achieved with regular electricity.

“The amount we spend on diesel daily is enormous, not to talk of the monthly cost. We buy at least 120 litres of diesel every day at the rate of N1,820 per litre,” she said.

“In the last two months, we have spent more than N10m on diesel to power our cold-room.

“Even if we run the generator for five hours, the frozen items will not be as properly frozen as they would be with one hour of electricity supply.”

She said the situation had also caused the loss of frozen food worth millions of naira, adding that some customers had returned goods because they could not preserve them properly with their refrigeration facilities.

She disclosed that the cold-room discarded frozen food worth N750,000 in September and another N550,000 in October because of inadequate power supply.

According to her, generators have failed to prevent further losses because they lack the capacity to freeze all items properly, while frozen products defrost faster when generators are switched off.

A frozen food dealer in Osogbo, Osun State, Bakare Ayodeji, also lamented that the situation had exposed businesses to heavy financial risks, especially when power failed for several consecutive hours.

“We cannot afford to leave our generators off because the goods in our cold-rooms will begin to defrost. At the same time, running generators continuously is becoming too expensive,” he said.

“We spend money buying fuel, servicing generators and repairing equipment. Even with all these expenses, we sometimes lose goods because the generators cannot maintain the required temperature. It is frustrating because we are spending more but making less profit.”

An operator of a poultry processing business in Ibadan, Oyo State, Somide Koya, said the cost of alternative power had become a major threat to the survival of small enterprises.

“We are practically working to buy fuel. Whatever profit we make goes back into keeping the business running. We cannot increase our prices every week because customers will stop patronising us,” he said.

“Unlike big companies, we don’t have enough capital to sustain this kind of expenditure. Some small businesses are already borrowing money to keep their operations running. If electricity supply does not improve, many operators may not survive.”

Another frozen food dealer, Badmos Suliat, decried the irregular power supply, saying it had hurt sales and discouraged bulk buying.

“Before now, customers could buy frozen chicken and fish in large quantities because they were confident that we could preserve them. But now, some of them prefer to buy small quantities because they are not sure we can keep the products frozen,” she said.

“Sometimes, we have to sell goods quickly at reduced prices when we notice that they are beginning to defrost. If we fail to do that, we risk losing the entire stock. It is a serious challenge because frozen food is not something you can keep without electricity.”

Power deficit threatens businesses

The Director-General of the Nigerian Association of Chambers of Commerce, Industry, Mines and Agriculture, Olusola Obadimu, warned that Nigeria would not achieve industrialisation with its current power generation capacity.

He said electricity generation of between 3,000 and 4,000 megawatts was grossly inadequate for a population of over 200 million people.

According to him, the country required at least 150,000 megawatts to industrialise, noting that South Africa, with a smaller population, generated significantly more electricity but still faced shortages.

“If we want to truly industrialise the country, we should be talking in the range of 150,000 megawatts. But because we are a domestic usage economy, people are just trying to solve their problems with whatever solar facility they can afford,” he said.

“It is a basic challenge for industry. Manufacturers are trying their best to cope, but at what price? They have to pass it on to the consumer. All these things have an inflationary effect on consumption.”

He said the country needed fundamental reforms across power generation, transmission and distribution, stressing that generated power must be effectively transmitted and distributed to consumers.

The immediate past President of the Nigerian Economic Society, Prof Adeola Adenikinju, said prolonged power outages had sharply raised operating costs for businesses, especially those relying on electricity for production and preservation.

He explained that grid electricity remained the cheapest option for most businesses, but poor supply had forced operators onto costlier alternatives such as diesel generators and solar systems.

According to him, the extra expenses would eventually push up prices of goods and services while cutting business profitability.

“If they are not careful, some of them may have to run at a loss. The prolonged outage must have impacted significantly on the bottom line of most of these businesses,” he said.

He estimated that businesses nationwide could be losing hundreds of millions, if not billions, of naira daily to poor electricity supply and generator operating costs.

He warned that small businesses already hit by inflation, high interest rates and weak consumer purchasing power were especially vulnerable.

“Some of them are already struggling to survive. When you layer power on it, they are just struggling to survive. They must be bleeding by now, particularly small-scale businesses that don’t have alternatives,” he said.

DisCos blame low tariffs

The Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer of the Association of Nigerian Electricity Distributors, Sunday Oduntan, said Nigeria needed more investment in power generation and distribution to tackle persistent supply shortfalls.

He said a country with an estimated population of 237 million people could not keep relying on power generation of about 5,500 megawatts.

Oduntan blamed part of the problem on energy theft and weak willingness by some consumers to pay for electricity, arguing that the sector needed sufficient revenue to sustain operations.

“No economy can grow in the dark. No economy can industrialise in the dark. Electricity is very essential to industrialisation.

“We just need to invest more, do more and bring in more foreign investors. Investors will not come if you cannot assure them of cost recovery,” he said.

The DisCos spokesperson noted that power sector improvements would take time, adding that the government and stakeholders needed to address challenges across generation, transmission and distribution.

He also urged state governments to use the amended Electricity Act 2023, which allows them to generate, transmit and distribute electricity within their jurisdictions.

According to him, only a few states, including Lagos, Abia and Enugu, have taken major steps towards developing their electricity markets.

Oduntan also defended cost-reflective electricity pricing, arguing that any gap between production cost and approved selling price must be borne by someone.

He said without adequate funding and cost recovery, electricity distribution companies would struggle to deliver reliable service.
