EXPLAINER: Why Nigerians are pushing back against ‘lazy youths’ narrative

By Punch Punch 22h ago 3 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

When Enioluwa Adeoluwa stood before a crowd of young Nigerians at the Emergence of Stars Youth Conference in Port Harcourt on April 1, 2025, he probably thought

Instead, his words would return more than a year later to trigger a heated debate about hard work, privilege, and the struggles of Nigerian youths.

Enioluwa passionately said, “Young people, we do not like to work anymore because we think that things should come easily for us. We need to understand that we must work; we cannot give up easily. You are 20, and you are already saying you are tired of life. Have you even seen life? What has life shown you?”

He added, “You have to have mentors. For example, Tyler Perry is my mentor. If you read every night, you are going to finish with a first-class. It is very easy. Life is not difficult; we overcomplicate it. You need to put in the work that will bring you a certain result.”

Although the speech was delivered in 2025, a clip from the event resurfaced on social media on November 17, 2026. Within hours, many Nigerians were criticising the influencer, arguing that his comments overlooked the economic realities facing young people.

Why Nigerians are angry about the label?

To understand the anger, it is important to look beyond Enioluwa’s comments.

For many Nigerians, being called lazy is particularly insulting because they believe they are already working harder than their circumstances allow.

Across the country, graduates are taking jobs unrelated to their qualifications, running side businesses, learning new skills and combining multiple jobs just to make ends meet. Yet, even with these efforts, many struggle to afford food, rent, transportation and other basic needs.

Low salaries are another problem. A young person can work full-time and still depend on side hustles to survive. Others work long hours, sometimes in jobs that have little connection to their professional training, because waiting for an opportunity in their chosen fields is not financially possible.
An X user, @MkpoikankeF, shared his experience of working two jobs at 26 but earning a combined ₦170,000 monthly. He explained that his mother’s medication cost ₦90,000, leaving him with little after transportation and feeding expenses.

Another user, @favwunmi, questioned what was left for a worker earning ₦60,000 monthly after waking up at 4am to go to work, paying bills and borrowing against the next salary.

A pharmacist, @ClemsAndy, also wrote that despite working at least eight hours a day, six days a week, since his induction in February 2024, he could not boast of owning a car.

These experiences raise an important question: how can a generation that is constantly finding ways to survive be described as unwilling to work?

There is also the frustration with bad leadership and poor public infrastructure. Nigerians have continued to contend with unreliable electricity, poor roads, rising food prices, limited job opportunities and an economy that makes it difficult for many businesses to grow.

When government fails to create an environment where hard work can translate into a decent standard of living, young people are left to find their own ways to survive.

For many, the problem is not a lack of willingness to work but the limited rewards for their efforts.
