Government neglect and poverty fueling deforestation, climate heat in Nigeria, Official warns

By VeryNigerian VeryNigerian 6d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: VeryNigerian

By Oladosu Adebola Oluwaseun ​Speaking on behalf of the Directorate of Agriculture and Natural Resources, Kwali Area Council, Abuja, a representative, Bala Bako

By Oladosu Adebola Oluwaseun

​Speaking on behalf of the Directorate of Agriculture and Natural Resources, Kwali Area Council, Abuja, a representative, Bala Bako, has raised the alarm over the rapid rate of deforestation in Nigeria, attributing the crisis to government negligence, poor policy implementation, and widespread poverty among rural farmers.

​Although noting that his colleague from the Environment Department formerly part of the Agriculture and Natural Resources Department had already addressed key issues, Bako emphasized the severe consequences of indiscriminate tree felling.

​”Deforestation is the act of cutting down trees sporadically without following professional standards,” he explained.

“Professionally, when you cut down one tree, you are supposed to replace it with five, six, or ten others to prevent desertification.”

​He noted the vital biological cycle of trees, explaining that they absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen while providing essential shade on sunny days.

However, he lamented that human destruction is dismantling this natural balance while the government fails to enforce its own environmental laws.

​”Today, the government of this country is not serious. We are very good at making laws, but we fail to implement them,” he said.

​Bako recalled past initiatives, such as the introduction of charcoal stoves, noting that while the program was mocked by many, providing affordable alternatives is the only way to keep rural populations out of the forests. “Even if you deploy soldiers to the forests, they cannot stop deforestation in this country without viable alternatives.”

​Citing his home village in Plateau State as a stark example, he described how a forest once so dense that visitors needed a flashlight during the day has now been completely cleared.

According to him, logging has become a primary source of income, with over 20 heavy-duty trucks (locally known as 911s) hauling timber out of the community daily toward areas like Gwagwalada and Gwari in the Federal Capital Territory.

​He also criticized the government for leaving forest guards unequipped, noting that many reserved areas around Abuja have been abandoned and degraded due to a lack of political will.

​Addressing the economic reality of rural dwellers, Bako pointed out that local farmers cannot afford cooking gas and rely on firewood to survive. He accused politicians of being insensitive to the plight of the common man, noting that leaders readily brave insecure areas—even those threatened by Boko Haram to campaign for votes, only to abandon citizens once elected.
​”How do you even expect a subsistence farmer who cannot feed his family to stop cutting down trees when you have provided no substitute?” he asked. “You cannot tell people to stop an activity without providing an alternative.”

​He further linked current extreme heat waves to both deforestation and poor agricultural practices, noting that the overuse of chemical fertilizers damages the ozone layer while the loss of tree cover exposes people to direct sunlight. He highlighted recent training on RIDA (Regenerative Agriculture), which promotes organic farming methods and the planting of cover crops to conserve soil and provide natural shade.

​To reverse the trend, Bako stressed the need for strict enforcement of policies that penalize and imprison individuals caught illegally felling trees or wasting water resources. He also called on authorities to promote water harvesting by constructing ponds to collect rainwater for dry-season farming and soil conditioning, alongside the adoption of smart drip irrigation systems to prevent water evaporation.

Furthermore, he emphasized that the government must ensure adequate funding by releasing budgeted funds to support agricultural officers and educate local farmers, rather than holding empty workshops without financial backing.

​Expressing frustration over unpaid entitlements and unimplemented budgets from January to December, Bako stated that he only agreed to the interview out of personal respect for the organizers.

​”Our entitlements are not being given; you have to shout and agitate just to get what is due,” he concluded. “We have the expertise, and we are ready to work, but the government must get serious, release the necessary resources, and stop deceiving the people.”
