COP31 faces collapse threat as latest draft fails to mention fossil fuels

By Vanguard Vanguard 2d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Vanguard

The COP31 presidency proposed an eleventh-hour deal Sunday that again fails to mention fossil fuels — an omission that deepened a tense standoff at the UN clima

The latest offering by summit co-hosts Turkey and Australia published deep into overtime leaves unresolved tensions over planet-heating fossil fuels but retains a much-sought goal to electrify the global economy.

The proposal needs consensus approval from nearly 200 nations at a closing session Sunday but they have struggled to bridge differences during two weeks of grinding negotiations.

The European Union and other nations have pushed for strong language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, a call that has faced resistance from oil-rich Saudi Arabia.

An EU official told AFP the latest proposal was a “backwards” step, warning there was a “very real possibility” that COP31 ends with no deal.

“I am more pessimistic today than I was yesterday,” the official said on condition of anonymity.

“The situation is difficult, and the outcome is uncertain. The oil-producing countries are putting up a fierce fight,” a German official said.

A Saudi official declined to comment when approached by AFP.

Fossil fuels are a flashpoint at the climate talks and the summit comes against a difficult backdrop of soaring energy prices and geopolitical tensions stemming from the war in Iran.

A draft deal unveiled earlier this week called for a faster phase out of fossil fuels but a second, pared-down version released Thursday stripped away all mentions of coal, oil and gas.

Threats of a walkout prompted Turkey and Australia to convene a hurried meeting between the European Union and Saudi Arabia in a bid to broker a compromise.

The latest text introduces a reference to a previous global deal in 2023 to shift the world away from fossil fuels — but stops short of naming them explicitly.

And it retains a contentious push to expand carbon capture and storage, a technology championed by the fossil fuel industry but criticised as costly and ineffective in cutting emissions.

“We are very disappointed with how the text looks,” the environment minister of Antigua and Barbuda, Michael Joseph, told AFP after leaving the COP31 presidency’s offices.

“I can’t honestly say that I’m okay with it passing” in its current state, Joseph said, adding that the draft was “quite silent” on fossil fuels.

– Goal to electrify –

The draft also calls for global efforts to boost the share of electricity in total final energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035 and the expansion of “clean energy”.

The summit had been under pressure to show progress in tackling soaring greenhouse gas emissions in a year of climate-linked disasters from Europe to Nepal and Indonesia.

Facing blowback over fossil fuels, Turkey and Australia pointed to “electrification” as a way to cut heat-trapping emissions and bolster energy security.

This works by replacing technologies that burn fossil fuels directly — like diesel cars and gas heating — with electric alternatives.

But some nations had demanded an explicitly “clean” target to ensure the electrification push was powered by renewable energy and not fossil fuels.

Around one-third of global electricity is produced using coal and another fifth using fossil gas.

Attempts by Saudi Arabia, India and Kenya meanwhile to delay the next set of reports by the UN’s Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) have been another major source of friction at the talks.

The draft text “highlights the importance” of these reports for an upcoming stocktake of global carbon-cutting efforts in 2028.

Dozens of countries across Europe, Africa and the Pacific want these assessments by the global scientific authority on climate change published in time for that crucial stocktake.

Countries have also disagreed at COP31 over how to respond to the looming breach of the 1.5C warming target set under the Paris climate accord.

The latest draft expressed “deep concern” that global warming would cross 1.5C above pre-industrial levels in the next few years.

The talks also jammed over long-running complaints that rich nations are shirking their obligations to help developing nations confront climate change.

The removal of a $120 billion target for climate adaptation angered developing nations.
