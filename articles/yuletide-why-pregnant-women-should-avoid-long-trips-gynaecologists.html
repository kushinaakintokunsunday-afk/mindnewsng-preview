Yuletide: Why pregnant women should avoid long trips – Gynaecologists

By Punch Punch 1d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

They clarified that although travelling itself would not trigger labour, pregnant women should consider the length of the journey, the prolonged sitting it may involve, the condition of the means of transportation, and the availability of skilled medical personnel at their destination.

The gynaecologists warned that going into labour while travelling, especially on a long-distance journey or aboard an aircraft, could create medical challenges because appropriate care might not be readily available.

Away from the festive season, they also advised women who intend to travel to their parents’ or relatives’ homes for support during childbirth and after delivery to make the journey early, preferably before 35 weeks of gestation, and arrange to continue their antenatal care at a health facility at their destination.

Yuletide in Nigeria comes with a lot of visits and travels to the homes of loved ones, as well as trips to exciting places and for religious activities.

Also, for many expectant mothers in Nigeria, travelling to their parents’ or relatives’ homes ahead of delivery is a way of securing family support during childbirth and the demanding early weeks of caring for a newborn.

Some rely on their mothers or mothers-in-law for assistance with the baby and other household responsibilities, especially where they have other young children.

PUNCH Healthwise had earlier reported that maternal health experts advised pregnant women to register for antenatal care at health facilities close to their homes and avoid long-distance journeys as their EDD approached.

The experts warned that labour could progress rapidly, while traffic congestion, transport difficulties and delays in accessing emergency care could leave women delivering on the road or in other places without appropriate medical assistance.

Providing expert insight into the matter, a Professor of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Oluwarotimi Akinola, said there was no evidence to suggest that travelling alone triggered labour, but cautioned pregnant women against taking unnecessary risks when they were approaching their delivery dates.

The gynaecologist said the concern was the possibility of labour beginning during a journey, particularly when the woman was travelling for several hours without easy access to medical care.

He said, “I honestly cannot say travel on its own will trigger labour. Nobody can absolutely say that because you are travelling, it will trigger labour. But what we know is that at some gestational age, labour might start.”

The professor said pregnant women planning to travel by air should be cautious, noting that a long flight could become difficult if labour began while they were on board.

“For example, if you are taking a plane and you are going to spend six to eight hours on that plane, I would want to be sure that you are not close to labour.

“Because when you go into labour on the plane, it has territorial political implications and all that. Apart from the medical challenges, it constitutes a risk for the people on the plane,” he said.

He, however, maintained that the journey itself was not necessarily responsible for the onset of labour, adding that the concern was the possibility of labour beginning when appropriate medical care might not be readily available.

For women travelling within the country, especially those going to stay with their parents or in-laws ahead of delivery, Akinola advised that the journey should be made early enough to allow them to transfer their antenatal care appropriately so that the health workers at their destination could understand their medical history and know how to manage them if complications arose.

“The advice is that they do so in good time, so that they can transfer their care appropriately. So that the people who are going to take care of them will know them and know what to do when anything happens,” he added.

Akinola, a former President of the Society of Gynaecology and Obstetrics of Nigeria, advised women not to wait until the final weeks of pregnancy before making such journeys, stressing the importance of following the guidance of their antenatal care providers.

“Don’t wait until the last minute and start travelling,” he said.

Akinola advised pregnant women to take antenatal care seriously because it provides guidance on preparing for childbirth and recognising situations that require medical attention.

Also, a Professor of Obstetrics and Gynaecology at the Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Osun State, Ernest Orji, advised pregnant women against travelling late in pregnancy, especially over long distances by road.

He added that prolonged sitting during long journeys, bad roads and the possibility of witnessing or being involved in accidents were concerns for heavily pregnant women.

He said, “What I normally tell people is that you should not travel after 35 weeks. If you want to travel, travel early. Many commercial bus drivers are careless, and some of the roads are bad. The trauma of seeing or having an accident is not good for a woman who is heavily pregnant.

“Also, a pregnant woman is not supposed to be sitting down for long; it’s not good for the baby too.”

Orji said women travelling to another town to deliver should also ensure that the health workers at their destination have access to their antenatal records and previous test results.

He said this would help the receiving medical team understand the woman’s condition, the care she had received and any precautions that might be necessary during delivery.

“We advise them to get their records and results from the hospital where they are coming from so that the new hospital knows what their state is, what has been done for them, and precautions they need to take,” he said.

Orji also urged pregnant women and their families to make practical arrangements for delivery before labour began, rather than waiting until an emergency arose.

He advised them to identify a suitable health facility, preferably one with qualified medical personnel, and ensure that they had the necessary supplies for delivery.

The obstetrician further urged families to arrange reliable transportation that would be available at any time of the day or night, including ensuring that vehicles had enough fuel.
