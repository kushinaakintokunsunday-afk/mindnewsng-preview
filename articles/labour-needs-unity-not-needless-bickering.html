Labour needs unity, not needless bickering

IN an era when Nigeria’s economic crisis is squeezing workers to the bone, the last thing Organised Labour needs is a needless turf war. Consequently, negotiations for a new minimum wage appear to have hit a bad patch because of divisions between the Trade Union Congress and the Nigeria Labour Congress, as reported by Daily Trust. This is both unfortunate and demoralising for workers whose welfare the two labour centres exist to protect.

The well-advertised feuding has raised concerns, particularly among civil servants, that the disunity could weaken labour’s bargaining power in negotiations with the Federal Government and the Organised Private Sector for an upward review of the current N70,000 monthly National Minimum Wage.

The immediate trigger was the TUC’s rejection of the Federal Government’s proposed N70,000 monthly transportation allowance for public servants for six months, beginning in November. The NLC promptly pushed back, insisting that the TUC had no authority to speak for the entire labour movement on an issue it was not party to negotiating.

On October 18, the Joint National Public Service Negotiating Council sided with the NLC, declaring that accepting the allowance was appropriate. Hours later, however, three unions – the Senior Staff Association of Statutory Corporations and Government-Owned Companies, the Senior Staff Association of Universities, Teaching Hospitals, Research Institutes and Associated Institutions, and the Nigerian Union of Allied Health Professionals – threw their weight behind the TUC.

This is a needless spectacle. What should be a coordinated campaign for better wages has degenerated into a contest over who controls the labour movement. Whatever the underlying institutional rivalries, workers should not be made to pay for the egos or ambitions of union leaders.

The Federal Government has maintained that the current minimum wage will be due for review in June 2027, relying on the amended National Minimum Wage Act, 2019, which provides for a three-year review cycle.

On October 12, the Minister of Labour and Employment, Muhammad Dingyadi, described calls for another review barely two years after the current wage took effect as “premature and potentially disruptive” to industrial relations.

The NLC strongly disagreed. It had earlier issued a two-week ultimatum demanding that the government reduce petrol prices to the levels prevailing when the current minimum wage was signed into law in 2024, commence negotiations for a new wage before the end of October and honour outstanding labour agreements. The ultimatum was subsequently extended to October 27.

The NLC argued that preparations for a new wage review should begin immediately and that the timetable in the original 2019 legislation remained relevant despite the 2024 amendment.

Following a conciliation meeting with the NLC on October 27, the Federal Government approved a N70,000 monthly transport allowance for public servants for six months, beginning in November. The concession was intended to cushion workers against soaring living costs and formed part of efforts to resolve renewed labour agitation over welfare and outstanding agreements.

The TUC rejected the arrangement, dismissing it as another set of “haphazard measures” to soften the impact of petrol subsidy removal. It instead demanded immediate salary increases, arguing that temporary allowances cannot substitute for a meaningful improvement in workers’ basic pay.

There is merit in the argument that a transport allowance, however welcome, is no substitute for a living wage. But rejecting an immediate concession without presenting a coordinated alternative risks leaving workers worse off. Labour must be pragmatic without surrendering its greater demands.

The disagreement has descended into personal exchanges. TUC President Festus Osifo accused his NLC counterpart, Joe Ajaero, of short-changing workers during negotiations with the Federal Government following the removal of the fuel subsidy.

He alleged that Ajaero rushed into negotiations and was prepared to accept a watered-down two-point proposal despite the TUC having submitted a 10-point demand to the government.

Osifo maintained that the TUC had explained the rationale behind its demands, only for the NLC to proceed without consulting it. Ajaero, in response, insisted that the NLC was negotiating independently, making the TUC’s opposition to the outcome unwarranted.

Both positions raise legitimate questions about consultation, representation and the respective mandates of the two labour centres. But none justifies the public acrimony.

If the NLC has a mandate to negotiate for its affiliates, it should exercise that mandate transparently. Equally, the TUC should be free to articulate its members’ grievances without turning every disagreement into a confrontation over legitimacy.

The TUC’s claim that it has more civil service workers than the NLC, and the NLC’s insistence that it can negotiate independently, are distractions from the real issue.

Nigerian workers are not interested in a statistical contest over union membership or a battle for institutional supremacy. They want wages that can sustain them in an economy where the cost of food, transport, housing and healthcare has become increasingly unbearable.

The rivalry between the two labour centres is not new. The TUC emerged in 2005 as a separate centre representing senior workers, and the institutional competition has persisted. But history is no excuse for allowing old animosities to undermine present-day negotiations.

The NLC secured the six-month transport allowance through its engagement with the government, while the TUC has pushed for a new minimum wage of N300,000.

Yet the TUC’s argument that the NLC cannot negotiate for its members does not resolve the wider question of how organised labour can present a united front on national wage policy. Nor does the NLC’s right to negotiate independently absolve it of the responsibility to build consensus where the interests of workers overlap.

The consequences of labour disunity are not theoretical. Previous disagreements have weakened industrial action and bargaining efforts, including the failed or fractured strikes of 2015, 2016 and 2023. The 2023 N35,000 wage award proved difficult to enforce in several states, exposing the limits of agreements that lack effective coordination and sustained pressure.

At a time when workers’ purchasing power has been devastated by inflation and the removal of fuel subsidy, labour cannot afford to negotiate with government from a position of division. A fragmented labour movement is an easy opponent for a government skilled at exploiting differences and delaying difficult concessions.

The OPS, too, will have little incentive to make meaningful compromises when workers cannot speak with a coherent voice.

The Petroleum and Natural Gas Senior Staff Association has rightly urged both labour centres to adopt a memorandum of understanding to regulate joint negotiations and prevent recurring disputes.

This proposal deserves serious consideration. Such an agreement should clearly define representation, consultation procedures, negotiation mandates and mechanisms for resolving disagreements before they spill into public confrontation.

Meanwhile, labour should accept the six-month transport allowance as an interim relief, without mistaking it for a victory or allowing it to derail the campaign for a substantially improved minimum wage.

The government should understand that a temporary allowance cannot compensate indefinitely for the erosion of workers’ real incomes. Preparations for a credible wage review should begin with transparent consultations and a realistic assessment of the cost of living.

Osifo and Ajaero should rise above personal rivalries and remember that their positions are positions of public trust. They are not competing for political office or fighting for personal prestige. Their responsibility is to defend workers whose salaries are being swallowed by the relentless rise in living costs.

Organised Labour was built on solidarity, collective action and the strength of numbers. Without these, its threats become hollow, its negotiations weak and its victories short-lived. Workers need better wages, not bigger egos. The NLC and TUC must stop fighting each other and start fighting for the people they claim to represent.
