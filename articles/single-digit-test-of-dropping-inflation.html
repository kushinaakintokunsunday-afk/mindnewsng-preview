Single-digit: Test of dropping inflation

By Punch Punch 1d ago 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

NIGERIA’S inflation rate fell to 14.06 per cent in October, but this is no cause for celebration. Food, transport, rent, electricity and other essentials remain

Finance Minister Taiwo Oyedele has rightly clarified that falling inflation does not mean falling prices; it means prices are rising more slowly. But this explanation must not become an alibi. Families whose food bills have doubled are not comforted by a slower pace of increase. What matters is whether incomes can buy more.

The latest National Bureau of Statistics figures indicate headline inflation eased from 14.63 per cent in September to 14.06 per cent in October, and from 18.97 per cent a year earlier. Yet month-on-month inflation accelerated to 0.43 per cent from 0.06 per cent.

Food inflation remained 19.13 per cent year-on-year. Food prices fell 0.75 per cent in October, but that was a smaller monthly fall than September’s 1.55 per cent. The average price level continued to rise. Therefore, relief remains fragile.

The retreat over the past 12 months has been anything but steady. Annual inflation fell from 18.97 per cent in October 2025 to 17.33 per cent in November and 15.15 per cent in December. It hovered around 15 per cent in January and February 2026, then rose to 15.38 per cent in March, 15.69 per cent in April and 15.93 per cent in May, before easing to 14.06 per cent in October. This is progress, not a stable retreat.

The 2025 CPI rebasing reduced food’s weight from more than half to about 28 per cent; the IMF warns that this affects comparisons across the rebasing period. Therefore, the headline figure cannot capture the cost-of-living crisis.

Market prices paint a far more accurate picture of Nigerians’ lived realities. NBS data put the average national price of a kilogramme of local rice at N487.47 in October 2022, N819.42 in October 2023, N1,944.64 in October 2024 and N1,913.78 in October 2025, nearly four times the 2022 price.

A measure of brown beans rose from N564.69 in October 2022 to N2,798.50 in 2024, before falling to N1,760.53 in 2025, still more than three times the 2022 price. Boneless beef climbed from N,266.24 to N6,850.51 over the same period.

The NBS food price reports, October 2024 report and October 2025 figures document this disturbing trajectory. It shows that a fall from a peak does not restore purchasing power.

Nigeria must aim lower if the cost-of-living crisis is to abate. CBN Governor Olayemi Cardoso described single-digit inflation as the Bank’s medium-term goal in July 2026.

The United States Federal Reserve and European Central Bank both target 2.0 per cent inflation.

Nigeria’s situation and vulnerabilities differ, but a country seeking investment, deeper credit and productive growth cannot normalise inflation above 10 per cent. Single digits must become a credible, sustained objective.

The NESG chairman, Olaniyi Yusuf, made the economic case that inflation below 10 per cent would create room for interest rates to fall, improve access to credit for businesses and households, and support expansion and consumer spending. He warned against premature celebration and identified insecurity and logistics as barriers to moving goods. The NESG chairman’s observations are apt.

The CBN’s Monetary Policy Rate remained 23 per cent after its September decision. At this cost, many manufacturers, farmers and small businesses cannot borrow affordably to expand. High rates may restrain demand, but used alone against supply-driven inflation, they can suffocate the investment needed to bring prices down.

Inflation is not exclusively monetary or the result of traders’ greed. Petrol subsidy removal and sharp naira adjustments since 2023 raised the cost of transport, imported machinery, fertiliser, packaging and other inputs. The IMF identifies food supply shocks, exchange-rate depreciation, deficit financing and supply constraints as major drivers.

About 30 per cent of food consumed in Nigeria is imported; the 2022/23 drought and 2024 floods damaged domestic production. Transport accounts for roughly a quarter of retail food prices. Poor roads, storage gaps, expensive diesel and unreliable electricity compound costs between farm and market. Insecurity keeps farmers from their land and makes moving produce riskier and more costly.

External forces also matter. Global food, fuel and fertiliser prices, shipping costs and geopolitical disruptions feed into an economy dependent on imported food and production inputs.

But external shocks do not explain why local prices stay high after global prices ease. Domestic productivity deficits, fragmented markets and inconsistent policies magnify the effects.

Fiscal indiscipline and deficit financing can inject liquidity and undermine monetary restraint; unpredictable levies and abrupt trade restrictions add risk premiums.

The IMF says inflation has become less persistent and more responsive to monetary policy, but durable disinflation still requires credibility, fiscal discipline and coordination between monetary and fiscal authorities.

Market conduct also comes into play. Oyedele rightly observed that expectations can become self-fulfilling as traders anticipating further cost increases may mark up prices in advance. But the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission has warned that its surveillance detected conspiracy, price gouging, hoarding and other unfair practices in parts of the food chain.

True, not every high price is profiteering; sellers face real costs. But these must not cover collusion, artificial scarcity or opportunistic mark-ups.

Prices often rise quickly when costs rise, but fall reluctantly when costs recede. Competition enforcement, transparent price information and action against hoarding are essential rather than blunt price controls.

Still, a disinflation programme must go beyond monetary tightening and liquidity mop-ups.

The CBN withdrew over N90 trillion in the first 11 months of 2026, more than double the N40.90 trillion mopped up in all of 2025, but inflation eased by just 1.09 percentage points from 15.15 per cent in December 2025 to 14.06 per cent in October 2026.

The CBN must defend price stability, maintain a transparent foreign-exchange market, and ease rates only as inflation falls sustainably. Government must avoid monetary financing, restrain wasteful recurrent spending and prevent election-driven liquidity surges.

Food production must rise through secure farming communities, rural roads, irrigation, affordable inputs, storage and processing. Farmers must be able to plant, harvest and transport without paying a ransom to insecurity.

Nigeria must cut the energy and logistics costs embedded in every product. Reliable power, competitive gas, efficient ports, highways and rail freight can cut production and distribution costs.

Predictable tax and trade policies, local manufacturing and agro-processing, and stronger non-oil exports would reduce import dependence and earn foreign exchange.

Government policies such as targeted cash transfers, school feeding and other support, if well-governed, should cushion the poorest households, but broad, fiscally costly subsidies are no substitute for reform.

Genuine macroeconomic improvements are undeniable. The World Bank reports that the economy grew by 4.2 per cent in the first half of 2026, up from 3.9 per cent a year earlier, while external reserves reached $51.9 billion at the end of July. Yet the Bank also warns that growth remains insufficient to create enough productive jobs or materially reduce poverty.

Thus, reform cannot be judged only by reserves, exchange-rate stability or a lower CPI reading. It must be judged by whether workers can afford decent meals, businesses can move goods, factories can obtain reliable power, and families can meet rent, school and medical bills without sinking deeper into debt.

The NESG is right that single-digit inflation is necessary to consolidate recovery and unlock affordable credit. But the destination must be more than a number on an NBS chart.

The government must make food affordable, energy dependable, and transport cheaper; protect production, not just financial indicators; and ensure lower inflation translates into real relief.

Nigerians have endured years of rising prices. They cannot be asked to applaud because the pain is increasing more slowly. Elegant explanations of disinflation are not a testament to economic policy success. What counts is whether ordinary citizens can afford a decent life.
