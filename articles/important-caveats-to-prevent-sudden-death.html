Important caveats to prevent sudden death

Stress and overwork rarely act in isolation. We saw in last week’s essay how hypertensive heart disease and related cardiovascular conditions lead to sudden, unexpected natural deaths. Stress accelerates the development of those conditions.

This week, we will examine how these factors subtly change key structures within our blood vessels over time until they cause the catastrophic events which result.

Not every stressed or overworked person dies suddenly; individual factors like genetics, existing hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, alcohol use, and access to proper healthcare matter greatly.

Reducing the risk involves addressing both the physiological effects of stress and the underlying cardiovascular vulnerabilities.

These can be obtained by regular blood-pressure and metabolic screening; blood-pressure control if you are hypertensive; prioritising sleep and rest. Other measures are stress-management techniques, including simple ones such as structured downtime, physical activity, or social support.

Avoidance of continuous overtime without recovery periods where possible is vital. Lastly, seek care for persistent symptoms rather than “pushing through” or invoking your faith.

In short, stress and overwork are widely recognised as important but modifiable contributors that raise blood pressure and can trigger fatal events in men who already carry high rates of undiagnosed hypertension and related risks.

Addressing economic and occupational pressures at a societal level, alongside individual health behaviour, must be part of the broader response. Therefore, you must conduct regular check-ups even when feeling well.

Eating healthier diets with less salt and processed food, and early detection of hypertension and related risks, are the most important preventive measures any individual can take.

While data quality on exact national rates is limited, due mostly to unreliable record-keeping, the figures available rely on hospital and autopsy series, surveys, and expert observation. The consistent finding across sources is that preventable cardiovascular events driven by hypertension and lifestyle factors are the core issue for sudden deaths in this demographic.

Alcohol is a recognised contributing risk factor for the sudden deaths discussed among men under 50 in Nigeria, primarily through its effects on blood pressure, heart rhythm, and overall cardiovascular health.

It is not usually the single dominant cause; hypertensive heart disease remains the leading finding in local autopsy series, but it amplifies existing risks, especially when combined with stress, overwork, poor diet, and undiagnosed hypertension.

Cardiologists discussing the rise in sudden “slump and die” cases routinely list excessive alcohol consumption among the lifestyle drivers of hypertension, alongside stress, smoking, obesity, and sedentary habits. Advice for prevention almost always includes limiting or avoiding excess alcohol.

Local studies support the blood-pressure link. For example, research on heavy consumers of traditional palm-wine liquor (“ozu”) found elevated blood-pressure parameters, particularly in middle-aged men. This is what alcohol does; excessive or regular heavy drinking is directly linked to hypertension.

It can cause both acute increases through sympathetic activation and chronic elevation. Reducing intake often lowers blood pressure in heavy drinkers. Nigerian clinicians note that a substantial proportion of patients with hypertension, heart failure, or serious heart disease in outpatient clinics report significant alcohol use together with smoking.

Binge drinking can provoke irregular heart rhythms including atrial fibrillation – “holiday heart syndrome” – and, less commonly, ventricular arrhythmias that can lead to cardiac arrest.

This risk is higher in people who already have structural heart disease or hypertension. Long-term heavy use can contribute to alcoholic cardiomyopathy, causing weakening and enlargement of the heart muscle, which increases the chance of heart failure and sudden cardiac death in the long term. It also interacts with other lifestyle factors like obesity, poorer sleep, and reduced exercise.

Alcohol can worsen metabolic problems, promote inflammation, and, in some cases of adulterated local spirits like methanol contamination, cause acute toxic deaths, though that is distinct from typical chronic effects.

Prevalence varies by region, age, and setting, but harmful patterns like heavy episodic drinking exist among adult men, even as some younger cohorts appear to drink less overall in recent surveys.

The volume consumed matters. Light or occasional drinking has weaker or inconsistent links to sudden cardiac death in some global studies, but heavy or binge patterns are clearly harmful for blood pressure and arrhythmia risk.

In a population with already high rates of undiagnosed hypertension, even moderate intake can be more problematic. Alcohol rarely acts alone. Its contribution is greatest when layered on top of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, chronic stress, and limited medical care.

Limiting alcohol, ideally avoiding excess or binge drinking, is one of the straightforward lifestyle measures recommended alongside blood-pressure screening and control, better sleep, stress management, and reduced salt and processed food consumption.

For men already under high economic or work pressure, substituting alcohol with healthier coping strategies can reduce one layer of cardiovascular strain.

In summary, alcohol contributes meaningfully by elevating blood pressure and raising the chance of acute rhythm disturbances or progressive heart damage, particularly in the high-risk environment of undiagnosed hypertension and lifestyle pressures common among Nigerian men under 50.

Reducing intake is a practical step that supports the broader prevention advice from physicians. Smoking, including cigarettes and even vaping, is another contributing risk factor for sudden deaths among men under 50 in Nigeria.

It accelerates cardiovascular damage and raises the likelihood of acute events in people who already have high rates of undiagnosed or poorly controlled hypertension. Smoking increases that risk by damaging blood vessels and promoting atherosclerosis. Nicotine and other toxins cause endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and plaque build-up in arteries.

This contributes to coronary artery disease, reduced blood flow to the heart, and higher chances of heart attack or cardiac arrest. This combination raises blood pressure and heart rate, causing temporary spikes in blood pressure.

So, chronic use supports the development or worsening of hypertension. It also promotes clotting and arrhythmias. Smoking makes blood more likely to clot and can trigger irregular heart rhythms that may lead to cardiac arrest.

These mechanisms are the dominant findings in Nigerian autopsies, and hypertensive heart disease and other risks such as stress, alcohol, obesity, and diabetes are the most likely combination of circumstances to cause sudden death in young Nigerian men.

Smoking is rarely the sole cause in the local pattern of sudden unexpected natural deaths, where hypertensive heart disease predominates over classic coronary atherosclerosis (unlike in many high-income countries).

Its impact is greatest when layered onto existing hypertension, diabetes, or other lifestyle pressures common in the demographic. Even so, it is one of the most actionable modifiable risks. Quitting reduces cardiovascular risk over time, with benefits beginning relatively soon after cessation.

So, avoiding or quitting smoking cigarettes, shisha, or vapes is a standard recommendation from doctors, alongside blood-pressure screening and control, limiting alcohol, managing stress, better diet, and regular activity.

For men under high work or economic pressure, eliminating smoking removes one major accelerator of the cardiovascular pathway that leads to many of these sudden deaths.

In short, smoking meaningfully heightens the risk of the hypertension-related and arrhythmic events that drive sudden deaths in this group. It is a preventable contributor that sits alongside the stress, overwork, and alcohol factors already discussed.
