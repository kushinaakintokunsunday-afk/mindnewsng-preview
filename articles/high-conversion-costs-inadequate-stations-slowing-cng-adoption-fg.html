High conversion costs, inadequate stations slowing CNG adoption – FG

By Punch Punch 16 Nov 2026 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Presidential Initiative on Compressed Natural Gas and Electric Vehicles has said Nigeria’s transition to alternative transport energy will depend more on re

The initiative said Nigerians would embrace Compressed Natural Gas and electric vehicles only when they could conveniently access refuelling and charging facilities, afford vehicle conversions and enjoy measurable reductions in transportation costs.

The agency projected that Nigeria’s CNG-powered vehicle population would nearly double to 273,000 by February 2027, but identified reliable gas supply and infrastructure expansion as critical to achieving the target.

The initiative, which put the country’s existing CNG vehicle population at about 143,000 as of the third quarter of 2026, is targeting an additional 130,000 conversions within five months, from September 2026 to February 2027.

The Executive Chairman of Pi-CNG and EV, Ismaeel Ahmed, stated this on Monday at the Federal Capital Territory Regional Town Hall and Stakeholder Dialogue in Abuja, where he acknowledged persistent concerns over conversion costs, access to refuelling stations, safety, gas supply and the affordability of alternative transport.

He said the government must move beyond announcing investments and infrastructure projects to ensuring that the benefits of the programme reached commercial drivers, commuters, transport operators and ordinary Nigerians.

“One of the things we have learnt in the course of implementing this programme is that people are not necessarily opposed to change. Most times, they simply want to know that the alternative being presented to them will work, that it will be available when they need it, and that it will make economic sense.

“A commercial driver who has used petrol all his working life will not change simply because government says CNG is cheaper. He wants to know where he will refuel, how long the conversion will take, whether the vehicle will remain reliable, whether the savings are real, and whether there will still be a station available when he needs one,” he said.

The chairman explained that the initiative had learnt from the practical difficulties of introducing alternative fuels into a transport system that had depended on petrol and diesel for decades.

He said the programme’s next phase must address the gap between government-reported progress and the experiences of Nigerians expected to adopt the technologies.

“At the same time, we know that progress on paper is not always the same as progress experienced by the average Nigerian, and that distinction is important.

“I often say that energy is a habit, because people organise their lives around what they know they can depend on. Nigerians have spent decades building their mobility around petrol and diesel because those were the fuels available to them. If we want people to adopt CNG and electric vehicles, then we have to make the alternative easy enough, available enough and reliable enough for new habits to form,” he added.

He identified availability, affordability and acceptability as the three critical conditions for widespread adoption, stressing that the initiative could not measure success solely by the number of conversion centres established or investments attracted.

“It is not enough to have a station somewhere in a state if the people who need it cannot conveniently reach it. It is not enough to have conversion centres if the process is still difficult for the ordinary vehicle owner to access. It is not enough to speak about lower operating costs if passengers are not yet seeing any difference in what they pay on the routes they use every day.

“This is why, within Pi-CNG and EV, we continue to emphasise availability, affordability and acceptability. These three things have to move together because the transition will only work when people can find the infrastructure, afford the service and trust the technology,” he said.

The chairman said the programme had attracted more than $`2.5bn in private-sector investment commitments, with increased participation in gas infrastructure, vehicle conversion and alternative mobility solutions.

He, however, maintained that the government was not expected to take over the operation of refuelling stations but to create an enabling environment for private investors.

“The important thing is that the private sector is the one that is going to make this sustainable. Government is not in the business of running businesses. It’s not our job to open a gas station. Yes, that’s not the job of government. The job of government is to create an environment for the private sector to invest in it,” he said.

He cited the growing involvement of private investors in the sector as evidence that the market was beginning to develop, adding that the initiative was working to accelerate infrastructure deployment across the country.

According to him, Nigeria’s estimated 350 trillion cubic feet of natural gas reserves provide an opportunity to reduce dependence on petrol and diesel, particularly in the transportation sector.

He argued that Nigeria should take greater advantage of its gas resources, noting that other countries had successfully deployed natural gas for vehicle transportation.

The chairman also stressed that the transition would require substantial investment and sustained political commitment, noting that countries with established alternative energy systems had spent considerable resources building the necessary infrastructure.

He said the Federal Government’s objective was to reduce transportation costs and give Nigerians more energy choices, rather than force consumers to abandon existing fuels without providing workable alternatives.

Meanwhile, the Head of Gas Supply Integration, Tari Mayor-Bright, said the programme was moving from its foundational stage towards expanding infrastructure and consolidating private-sector participation.

In her presentation, Mayor-Bright disclosed that the number of CNG stations in Nigeria had increased to 113 as of November 12, 2026, from 106 recorded on November 6.

She said the South-West had the highest number, with 41 stations, followed by North-Central with 33, South-South with 17, North-West with 15, North-East with four and South-East with three.

Of the 113 stations, 25 were located in the Federal Capital Territory, she said.

Mayor-Bright put investment in the CNG programme at approximately `$2.6bn, describing the growing participation of investors and operators as evidence that the market was developing.

She said the initiative was also working to expand the network of refuelling facilities, conversion centres and other supporting infrastructure across the country’s six geopolitical zones.

Mayor-Bright explained that the expansion required more than establishing facilities, stressing the importance of reliable operations, safety standards, regulatory compliance and customer confidence.

She identified dependable infrastructure, effective regulation, compliance and stronger market participation as essential to sustaining the programme.

The official also called for complementary development of CNG and electric mobility, arguing that both technologies could help reduce transportation costs rather than compete against each other.

She noted that electric vehicle adoption would require adequate charging infrastructure and reliable electricity, while encouraging private-sector investment in alternative power sources to support charging facilities.

The town hall, themed CNG and EV in Nigeria: Your Questions, Our Answers, was organised as part of a six-zone engagement programme designed to bring government officials, investors, operators, beneficiaries and members of the public together to address concerns surrounding alternative mobility.

The engagement came against the backdrop of the removal of the petrol subsidy in May 2023, which triggered a sharp increase in transportation costs and intensified calls for cheaper alternatives.

The Pi-CNG programme was introduced to promote the use of Nigeria’s domestic gas resources as an alternative to petrol and diesel, while its expanded electric mobility mandate seeks to encourage the adoption of electric vehicles and supporting infrastructure.

The chairman said the initiative was committed to addressing public concerns and ensuring that the transition delivered tangible economic benefits.

“We also have to remain conscious of who ultimately benefits from all of this. I have said repeatedly that the last mile must smile, because public policy cannot be judged only by the amount of infrastructure announced or the level of investment attracted.

“The real test is whether the benefit gets to the driver, the commuter, the small business owner, the technician, the transport operator and the ordinary Nigerian whose daily expenses are affected by the cost of movement.

“That last mile is where people will judge us, and rightly so,” he said.

He urged stakeholders to use the engagement to raise concerns about infrastructure gaps, conversion costs, safety and service quality, assuring them that the government was open to criticism and feedback.

He added that the next phase of the programme would focus not only on expanding infrastructure but also on improving the experience of Nigerians using alternative energy.
