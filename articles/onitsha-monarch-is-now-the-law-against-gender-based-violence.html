Onitsha monarch is now the law against gender-based violence

By Punch Punch 5d ago 4 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Obi of Onitsha has put the weight of a 700-year-old throne behind one message: no custom justifies violence against a woman. Now GBV is no longer a private

Nigeria does not have a GBV law problem. It has a culture problem.

We have the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act. We have state laws. We have campaigns. Yet a woman beaten in her home is still told to “go and settle it as family”. A girl abused is still told to keep quiet to protect the family name. The law speaks, but culture whispers louder, and culture usually wins.

That is why what is happening in Onitsha matters.

The Obi of Onitsha, Igwe Nnaemeka Alfred Achebe, has decided that the throne will no longer be neutral. He has put the full weight of a 700-year-old monarchy behind one simple message: there is no custom, no tradition, and no proverb that justifies violence against a woman.

In Onitsha today, GBV is no longer a private matter. It is a public matter.

It did not start as a GBV project.

In 2014, the Obi-in-Council set up the Onitsha Advancement Foundation to push education, skills, and the preservation of Onitsha values. But a serious institution cannot preserve values while ignoring violence in the home. So, OnAF became the engine.

First, the Obi changed what ‘royal power’ means. He did not just condemn GBV in a speech. He got traditional rulers across Anambra to sign a collective royal mandate, a public commitment to protect women and vulnerable people. That is no small thing in a culture where a ruler is expected to protect custom, not question it.

Then he did something even rarer for a palace: he asked for data.

OnAF carried out participatory research across all the villages in Onitsha. 841 people spoke. They were asked what they know about GBV, what forms it takes, why it happens, what it does to women and girls, and why survivors do not report.

The answer was uncomfortable but useful. People knew what GBV was. They just did not trust the system to protect them if they spoke.

So, Onitsha built a system they would trust.

That research produced two tools: a data-driven GBV awareness curriculum for people who live and work in Onitsha, and an Onitsha GBV Prevention Strategy, with monitoring built in to track progress toward zero tolerance.

Then came the law, Onitsha style.

The Obi-in-Council moved to formally incorporate a GBV prevention and response framework into Onitsha’s customary guidelines. Alongside it, the Council is developing a compendium of rules to penalise domestic abuse, verbal and emotional violence, and discriminatory inheritance practices.

Those rules will guide the Ime Obi Traditional Court.

This is the genius of the model. The formal court is far, expensive, slow and intimidating. The Ime Obi court is near, trusted, fast and transparent. A survivor does not need a lawyer and transport fare to Lagos or Awka to be heard. She can be heard at home, by a court her community respects.

And to make sure it is not just the palace talking to itself, OnAF has been holding strategic dialogues with Ndi Diopka, the kindred head. When the kindred heads buy in, the message reaches every compound, every umunna meeting, and every family dispute.

That is how you change culture. Not by press release. By palace, by data, by court, and by kindred.

On October 16, 2026, the Obi took it one step further. He launched the OnAF GBV Centre, Africa’s first palace-connected GBV centre. It is not just a building. It is a statement: survivors do not have to run away from the community to get help. Help is now inside the community, with the blessing of the King.

According to the OnAF Programme Coordinator and Onitsha GBV Project Lead, Dr Rita Melifonwu, the centre will serve as a one-stop shop for all GBV work in the kingdom, with confidential counselling, crisis intervention and support, supported by the Ford Foundation. The Obi described it as a safe environment for the Rapid Response Team to work with stakeholders and support survivors.

This is the lesson for Nigeria.

We have spent years trying to fight GBV by fighting culture. Onitsha is showing us a smarter way: fight GBV by reclaiming culture.

When a 700-year-old stool says culture must serve people, not punish them, the silence breaks. And when the silence breaks in Onitsha, it will be harder for it to hold anywhere else.

The Bottom Line is that laws can punish a man after he beats his wife. Only a changed culture can stop him before he raises his hand. In Onitsha, the King has chosen to change the culture.
