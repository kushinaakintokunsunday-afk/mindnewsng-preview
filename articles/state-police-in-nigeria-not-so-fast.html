State police in Nigeria: Not so fast!

By Punch Punch 2d ago 6 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

 According to a press statement by the Chief of Staff to the President and Chairman of the Presidential Working Group on State Police, Femi Gbajabiamila, the bill was formally transmitted to the President on Wednesday, November 18, 2026, by Basheer Lado and Wasiu Olanrewaju-Smart, his special advisers on Senate and House of Representatives matters, respectively.  President Tinubu would review the bill in consultation with Lateef Fagbemi, Attorney-General of the Federation and the Minister of Justice, before appending his signature.

 I had thought the President would sign the bill by last Friday, but he’s yet to do so as at the time of writing this piece. Meanwhile, I had the privilege of discussing the passage of this constitution alteration bill, the very first under President Tinubu, last Wednesday on Radio Now 95.3 Lagos. I did say on the programme that it is not yet Uhuru for state police, as there are still several hurdles to cross.

 Many people, particularly opposition candidates and political parties, believe the state police is being rushed to be used as a political weapon against them during the forthcoming 2027 general elections, holding on January 16 and February 6. I have consistently disabused their mind that no state will be ready with a functional police system until long after the elections.

 For instance, even if President Tinubu signs the constitution alteration bill into law, a National Policing Bill is still needed to flesh out the guardrails and modus operandi for state police operations vis-à-vis the existing federal police.  The committee overseeing it is headed by the Chief of Staff to the President, Femi Gbajabiamila. In his press statement last Wednesday, he gave an update on the work of his committee when he said: “Meanwhile, the Presidential Working Group on State Police, which I chair, with the support of the Policy Advisory Committee, has undertaken extensive stakeholder engagement and nationwide consultations towards developing a draft National Policing Bill.” He said the draft bill had been submitted to the Federal Ministry of Justice for legislative drafting and validation and that the working group would review the validated version upon receipt and present it, alongside its report, to the President for consideration.

 The key highlights of the National Policing Bill drafted by Femi Gbajabiamila’s committee include: Establishment of a Dual-Policing Structure: The bill aims to create a dual policing system comprising the Federal Police Service and 36 State Police Services; Minimum Standards and Certification: It will set national minimum standards for recruitment, training, oversight, and funding, ensuring credible and accountable policing; Human Rights Safeguards: The bill will include provisions to protect citizens’ rights and ensure that state police do not become instruments of political intimidation.

How early will this National Policing Bill be passed and assented to by the President when the National Assembly is struggling to form a quorum at the peak of this campaign season?

 I reliably learnt that about 62 extant Acts of Parliament relating to Nigeria’s policing system must also be amended before state police are on full legal footing. Furthermore, each governor has to sponsor an executive bill establishing the state police for their respective states. Among other things, the bill will establish a State Police Service Commission, an autonomous body responsible for the recruitment, training, promotion and discipline of the officers and men of the state police. At the takeoff of the state police, some federal police personnel may transfer to their respective states or go on secondment to serve as foundational staff pending the actual takeoff of the state police. For instance, not all state police recruits will start as constables.

 Another sticky point is that the law establishing state police does not permit state governments to procure arms and ammunition. They must apply to the Office of the National Security Adviser to procure arms and ammunition for their respective state police. Even applications to establish state police must be routed through the National Security Adviser or another federal body or committee, which will certify that the state has met the minimum threshold and safety standards for state police operations. Fear has been expressed in some quarters that the NSA, being a political appointee, may turn down applications from opposition state governments. This is, however, speculative.

 The big elephant in the room is the funding for state police. I learnt that 80 per cent of operational costs must be ready at inception before approval for establishment can be given. Many have wondered how state police will be funded and whether each of the 36 states has enough resources to fund them adequately. Questions are being asked if governors will still be collecting security votes when they have state police, or if they will have to set up Police Equipment Trust Funds, as some states have done. Another proposition is saying that the federal government must give take-off grants or bailouts to states wanting to establish state police.

 Sincerely, establishing state police is not a tea party or a walk in the park. It’s going to be capital intensive. States will have to establish their respective training institutions, build police stations and perhaps barracks. The state will also have to build armouries and stores. Automatic weapons are not cheap, as they are not manufactured in Nigeria and have to be procured abroad. States will also need forensic laboratories and to deploy technology to fight crime at the state level. Enugu State has procured drones and established a command and control centre, while the Oyo State government has bought two jet fighters to help combat insecurity in the state. Given the artificial nature of interstate boundaries, it’s important to specify in law how to treat transborder crimes and jurisdictional overlaps between federal and state police.

 Interestingly, former President Muhammadu Buhari signed a constitutional alteration devolving prisons, now known as Correctional Centres, from the exclusive legislative list to the concurrent legislative list, as the State Police bill has just done. However, to the best of my knowledge, no state government has established its correctional services. Meanwhile, most of the crimes being committed are likely to fall under state police jurisdiction. Will the Federal Government continue to bear the burden of awaiting trials and convicts of state crimes under the new dispensation? Food for thought!
