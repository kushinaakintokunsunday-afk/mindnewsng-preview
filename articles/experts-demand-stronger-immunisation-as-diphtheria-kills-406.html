Experts demand stronger immunisation as diphtheria kills 406

By Punch Punch 2d ago 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

As diphtheria claimed 406 lives in seven months, seasoned epidemiologists have called for the introduction of booster doses of diphtheria vaccines and expanded immunisation campaigns for children aged one to 14 years to curb the outbreak of the disease in Nigeria.

The experts said Nigeria needed to strengthen routine immunisation, improve vaccine coverage and ensure adequate supplies of diphtheria antitoxin to prevent more infections and deaths.

They made the recommendations in separate interviews with PUNCH Healthwise while reacting to fatalities so far from diphtheria complications in 2026.

Reports show that there have been increased diphtheria cases over the past three to four years, with major surges recorded in 2024 and 2025.

More than 1,260 people have died from diphtheria in Nigeria since the disease outbreak started in late 2022, while suspected infections have surpassed 41,000, with the country recording more than 24,000 confirmed cases, according to Médecins Sans Frontières.

The MSF, known as Doctors Without Borders, also disclosed that between January and August 9, 2026, Nigeria recorded 12,977 suspected infections, 10,476 confirmed cases and 406 deaths.

Speaking with PUNCH Healthwise, the public health experts identified incomplete childhood immunisation, vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, poor access to healthcare and delayed diagnosis as factors contributing to the sustained resurgence of the vaccine-preventable disease.

The epidemiologists urged the government to extend vaccination campaigns beyond children under five, noting that a significant proportion of confirmed diphtheria cases occurred among children aged between two and 14 years.

A Public Health Physician and Epidemiologist at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital, Idia-Araba, Mushin, Prof Adebayo Onajole, said the availability of vaccines had not translated into adequate protection because many children were still missing routine immunisation.

Onajole said vaccination remained important in reducing the severity of the infection, adding that poor immunisation coverage, vaccine stock-outs and misinformation are some of the factors responsible for the continued spread of the disease.

“Why are we having an increase in diphtheria confirmed cases and deaths is straightforward. One, we have a failure of immunisation. That is, immunisation is not getting to the population, most especially children under five. Because diphtheria is more common among children under five,” he said.

The don urged the government at all levels to strengthen public awareness campaigns and ensure that healthcare workers reached communities that were difficult to access, particularly rural settlements and internally displaced persons’ camps.

Onajole also stressed the importance of maintaining the cold chain system to preserve vaccine potency.

“Health facilities also need to take care of the storage of their vaccines to increase their potency. Nowadays, electricity is not readily available and if that persists, storage is going to be a problem, and that means that the cold chain system might be hampered or affected.

“The government also needs to encourage healthcare providers to search for or go to difficult-to-reach terrains and immunise these populations that may need this support. Most of these populations are in rural areas and among the internally displaced population,” he said.

Furthermore, Onajole called for increased government funding and greater contributions from individuals and organisations to support immunisation and other preventive healthcare interventions.

“The government and the general population both need to support immunisation activities. When I say government and the general population, the government will support through funding. The general population may also need to fund indirectly.

“Rather than doing all these parties or owanbes, the people should support health interventions too, like Rotary has been doing with immunisation and supporting health activities; this is what I will call the hidden resources in health.

“Rather than spend so much money on activities that may not improve or protect life, people who can afford to donate or support these activities that can protect life or prolong life should channel funds into it,”

Similarly, a Consultant Epidemiologist and Professor of Community Medicine, Prof. Kabir Durowade, identified low routine immunisation coverage and incomplete vaccination as major drivers of diphtheria transmission in the country.

He said many children received the first dose of the pentavalent vaccine but failed to complete the recommended three-dose schedule.

The epidemiologist said available data indicated that only about half of children aged between one and two years were fully immunised against diphtheria, a situation he described as a major factor driving the outbreak.

He said, “People start the vaccine, take the first dose, and don’t complete all the doses. So, we have what is called dropouts. We have high dropouts. That is one major issue driving what we are beginning to see in Nigeria.”

He further identified inadequate supplies of diphtheria antitoxin as another major challenge, explaining that the medication was required to neutralise the toxin produced by the bacteria in patients with severe infections.

“We don’t have enough stockpiles of diphtheria antitoxin. Our stocks are very low, but it’s not peculiar to Nigeria alone. There are global shortages across the world,” he said.

On what should be done differently to curb outbreaks, he recommended increasing routine immunisation coverage to above 90 per cent to achieve herd immunity and reduce transmission within communities.

“We need to ensure that we improve our vaccination coverage to substantially above 90 per cent. When we do that, we’re going to have what is called herd immunity.

“Herd immunity means immunity of the entire community. When people are immunised, and an infection comes, the community can withstand the infection. It won’t spread because people are immunised against it. So, when we reach that level and scale up our coverage, we’re also going to drive up herd immunity in our locality,” the don said

Durowade, who is also the Dean of the Faculty of Clinical Sciences at the Federal University, Oye-Ekiti, also called for the procurement and strategic positioning of diphtheria antitoxin stockpiles to ensure that patients with severe infections could receive prompt treatment.

The epidemiologist stressed that mass vaccination campaigns should cover children between one and 14 years.

He said, “Now, we shouldn’t just limit mass campaigns to children under five because this outbreak affects children between one and 14 years old. We must extend the mass campaign to cover this age group, between one and 14, not just children under five.”

Furthermore, the don advocated the introduction of booster doses at school entry and adolescence to strengthen immunity beyond the three routine infant doses.

He said, “Again, I must add that there should also be booster doses. Apart from the three doses we give our infants, there should be a booster dose somewhere, maybe at school entry and adolescence stage, that can strengthen children’s immunity.”

Durowade also called for the training of primary healthcare workers to recognise the clinical signs of diphtheria, including the formation of membranes in the throat and enlarged lymph nodes.

He urged healthcare workers to recognise the condition and refer suspected cases to appropriately equipped hospitals without delay.

Durowade warned that delayed presentation or referral could allow the bacterial toxin to damage vital organs, including the heart, kidneys and nervous system.

The don added that public awareness campaigns should be intensified to address vaccine misinformation and hesitancy, with community and religious leaders enlisted to encourage acceptance of immunisation.

The World Health Organisation states that diphtheria vaccines are highly effective but recommends a three-dose primary series followed by three booster doses in childhood and adolescence to produce and sustain long-term immunity.

WHO also notes that booster doses strengthen and extend protection when immunity from the primary series weakens over time.
