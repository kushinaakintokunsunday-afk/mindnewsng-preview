Strong economy vital to Nigeria’s foreign policy – FG

By Punch Punch 17 Nov 2026 3 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

The Minister of State for Foreign Affairs, Sola Enikanolaiye, stated this on Tuesday while declaring open the sixth edition of the Foreign Policy School at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Tafawa Balewa House, Abuja.

The three-day programme, organised by the Nigerian Institute of International Affairs and the Peace-Building Development Consult, is themed “Foreign Policy as a Tool for National Security and Economic Growth” and runs from November 16 to 18, 2026.

Enikanolaiye said the President Bola Tinubu-led administration was repositioning Nigeria’s foreign policy through reforms within the ministry, with improved diplomatic operations and a performance-driven approach to the country’s foreign missions.

He described the administration’s foreign policy as bold, pragmatic and citizen-centred, anchored on the reformulated 4Ds of Demography, Development, Diaspora and Democracy, with Nigeria and its citizens at the centre.

The minister, however, clarified that the renewed emphasis on domestic priorities did not diminish Africa’s position in Nigeria’s foreign policy, arguing that the country must first build a strong and resilient economy to sustain its leadership role on the continent.

He said the ongoing economic reforms under Tinubu were intended to provide the domestic foundation required for effective diplomatic engagement.

Recalling that the ministry adopted the motto, “Nigeria First”, in 2007, Enikanolaiye said the present administration was working to translate the principle into practical outcomes.

He explained that efforts were underway to reform the Ministry’s internal operations and reposition Nigerian missions abroad as economic hubs and centres for trade and technology facilitation, while improving consular services for Nigerians.

“We are moving beyond the mantra of diplomacy for diplomacy’s sake to a purposeful engagement calibrated to serve our people.”

To strengthen accountability and improve service delivery, the minister unveiled three instruments for assessing the performance of Nigeria’s foreign missions.

They include the Stations Charter, which assigns specific priorities to each mission; the Strategic Career Performance Evaluation and Review, an annual performance assessment for heads of mission; and the Global Best Practices Project, designed to enable Nigerian missions to identify international practices that could inform national policy.

He said the initiatives represented a shift from measuring diplomatic success by activities to assessing tangible results.

“Together, they mark a decisive shift from diplomacy as activity to diplomacy as measurable delivery,” he said.

On Nigeria’s international relations, Enikanolaiye said the country remained a confident, assertive and responsible global actor, committed to strategic autonomy, economic diplomacy and citizen diplomacy.

He added that Nigeria would continue to strengthen partnerships across the Atlantic, the Global South, the United Nations, the African Union and the Commonwealth, guided by reciprocity, mutual respect and respect for national sovereignty.

Addressing security challenges in West Africa, the minister identified insecurity in the Sahel, unconstitutional changes of government, the proliferation of small arms and humanitarian crises as major threats confronting the Economic Community of West African States.

He said Nigeria had no territorial ambitions in the region and remained committed to good neighbourliness, dialogue and mediation, including engagements with member states of the Alliance of Sahel States, to restore constitutional order and rebuild confidence.

“For Nigeria, it is zero tolerance for unconstitutional change of government; this being key to the defence and protection of our democracy.”

Enikanolaiye called for stronger preventive diplomacy, effective early-warning mechanisms and frank but respectful dialogue among African countries, noting that preventing conflicts was less costly than managing their consequences.

He described the Foreign Policy School as a strategic investment in the intellectual capacity of Nigeria’s diplomatic service, urging participants to see themselves as custodians of the country’s national interest.
