Senate orders probe of attacks on Tinubu, Obi, Atiku

From Adesuwa Tsan, Abuja

Alarmed by the wave of attacks on political leaders and their supporters ahead of the 2027 general elections, the Senate, yesterday, ordered an investigation into reported assaults on President Bola Tinubu and opposition presidential candidates, Peter Obi and Atiku Abubakar, as lawmakers raised concerns that politicians themselves were sponsoring the violence threatening the electoral process.

The Senate also warned that candidates found culpable in electoral violence through the activities of their agents could face disqualification, with Senate President Godswill Akpabio drawing attention to provisions of the Electoral Act 2026 prescribing sanctions for political thuggery and intimidation.

The resolutions followed a motion of urgent public importance sponsored by Senator Abdul Ningi, which highlighted attacks on political leaders, campaign convoys and supporters across the country, including the reported stoning of Tinubu’s motorcade in Abuja on November 13, attacks involving Nigerian Democratic Congress (NDC) presidential candidate Peter Obi and African Democratic Congress (ADC) presidential candidate Atiku Abubakar, and violence at political gatherings.

In a particularly troubling contribution, Senator Samaila Kawu accused politicians of being behind much of the violence, alleging that some supplied weapons and drugs to young people to attack opponents and disrupt political activities.

“We are the sponsors of all what is going on,” he declared, urging security agencies to move beyond arresting perpetrators to identifying and prosecuting those financing and encouraging political thuggery.

The Senate consequently directed its Committees on National Security and Intelligence, Public Affairs and Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) to engage relevant security and electoral institutions and report back within one week on measures to prevent further attacks and safeguard the elections.

It also urged the Federal Government to direct the Inspector-General of Police and other security agencies to strengthen protection for political leaders, candidates, supporters and campaign gatherings nationwide, while establishing effective early-warning and rapid-response mechanisms to contain electoral violence.

Moving the motion, Ningi expressed concern over what he described as an alarming escalation of political intolerance, intimidation and violence, warning that the trend could undermine public confidence in the electoral process and discourage citizens from exercising their democratic rights.

He specifically drew attention to the reported attack on Tinubu’s motorcade shortly after the President observed Jumat prayers at the Shehu Shagari Central Mosque, Garki, Abuja, on November 13.

According to the motion, the convoy was attacked with stones and other dangerous objects as it departed the mosque, resulting in damage to vehicles and injuries to security operatives.

The Senate also recalled the reported attack on Obi and other political stakeholders in Benue State, the disruption of a procession involving supporters of the NDC and Obidient Movement in Asaba, Delta State, on November 7, which reportedly left one person dead and five others injured, and the attack on Atiku’s convoy in Sokoto State.

Contributing to the debate, Senator Mohammed Ndume warned that the National Assembly must take a firm stand against political violence, irrespective of the party affiliation or political standing of those targeted. “What we need to do as a Senate is to stand up solely against any form of violence.”

Ndume described the reported attack on Tinubu as a national embarrassment, arguing that an assault on the President diminished the country’s democratic institutions.

“What happened last Friday to Mr President is not an embarrassment to Mr President, it is an embarrassment to Nigerians,” he declared.

Questioning the justification for attacking political leaders because of their positions, he asked: “What are the youths saying, that who God chose as president is wrong?”

He commended Tinubu’s response to the incident, contrasting it with what he suggested might have happened under military rule.

“The President is a democrat. If it were military, those 88 people that were arrested that day would have been dead,” he said.

Ndume urged the police to prohibit the carrying of knives, sticks and other objects capable of being used as weapons at political rallies, while advocating mobile courts for the speedy trial of suspects implicated in electoral violence.

He also warned against political slogans calling for the removal of leaders, arguing that inflammatory rhetoric could encourage intolerance and confrontation among supporters.

“Tinubu must go, Atiku must go, Obi must go. So if everybody goes, who will remain?” he asked.

Kawu, while condemning the attack on the President, said the problem of political violence could not be effectively addressed without confronting the politicians who allegedly recruited, armed and financed those responsible.

He alleged that some political actors supplied weapons and drugs to youths to unleash violence on opponents, citing an incident involving a National Assembly candidate who was allegedly seen carrying weapons without facing any known consequences.

Kawu, however, cautioned security agencies against excessive force, referring to an alleged incident during an All Progressives Congress (APC) rally in Sokoto State, where security operatives reportedly shot at supporters wearing party insignia as they entered the venue.

He stressed that the responsibility to prevent electoral violence must extend to both political actors and security personnel, whose conduct could either strengthen or undermine confidence in the electoral process.

Senate Minority Leader Abba Moro warned that persistent attacks on candidates and supporters could ultimately disenfranchise voters and weaken democratic participation.

He urged security agencies to rise to their responsibilities by ensuring that political campaigns and other electoral activities were conducted peacefully, without intimidation or harassment.

Senator Babangida Hussaini, in his contribution, recalled recommendations of the Justice Mohammed Uwais-led Electoral Reform Committee, particularly proposals for the establishment of an electoral offences commission and specialised courts to deal with electoral violence.

He lamented that the failure to implement far-reaching measures against electoral offences had allowed political thuggery and intimidation to persist.

“We have begun to see the results of what we have not done,” he said.

Hussaini also alleged that an ADC candidate in his state openly brandished a pistol without any known action being taken against him.

“We have paid lip service with this matter and we have to be serious about it,” he declared.

Senator Sampson Ekong called for a level playing field for candidates across political parties, stressing that the right to campaign freely and peacefully must be protected regardless of political affiliation.

He urged the Federal Ministry of Information and the National Orientation Agency to embark on intensive public enlightenment campaigns to discourage electoral violence and promote political tolerance.

In his closing remarks, Akpabio warned that the consequences of political violence could extend beyond the arrest and prosecution of thugs to the disqualification of candidates implicated in such activities.

He called for the full implementation of the Electoral Act 2026, particularly Sections 121 to 128, which he said contained provisions prescribing sanctions for electoral violence and misconduct.

According to him, candidates whose agents incited violence could face disqualification where INEC established a prima facie case against them.

He added that candidates who carried weapons or encouraged armed thugs to surround them could also face disqualification under the law.

Akpabio warned parents and guardians to caution their children against participating in attacks on political leaders, saying some actions could attract serious criminal charges.

“We must warn our children because there are certain things you can do and it will turn out to be treasonable felony,” he said.

Referring to the reported attack on Tinubu’s motorcade, the Senate President said those involved could have faced charges of treasonable felony, warning that a confrontation with security operatives could have resulted in fatalities.

“If the security agents had attacked, such a child may not get back home,” he said.

The Senate subsequently mandated its three committees to engage relevant security and electoral institutions and submit their findings and recommendations within one week, as concerns mount over the safety of political leaders, their supporters and the integrity of the electoral process ahead of the 2027 elections.
