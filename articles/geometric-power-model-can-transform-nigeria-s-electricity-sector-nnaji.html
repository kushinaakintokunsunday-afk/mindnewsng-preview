Geometric Power model can transform Nigeria’s electricity sector — Nnaji

By Punch Punch 3d ago 11 min read

Geometric Power Group’s Managing Director, Agatha Nnaji, discusses how the Aba Integrated Power Project is improving electricity supply, plans to expand generation capacity, financing challenges and lessons for Nigeria’s power sector in this chat with SAMI TUNJI

How has the Aba Integrated Power Project impacted Nigeria’s electricity sector, particularly in Aba?

I would say that the Aba Integrated Power Project is a model that should make a difference in the Nigerian power sector because we believe it is a workable model that would enable people to get power at an affordable rate, provided it is implemented properly by whoever the sponsors are.

What we have been able to achieve in Aba is to prove that it is possible for Nigerians to have an affordable and sustainable power supply on a 24/7 basis.

We have a vision as Geometric Power. Our goal here is to provide power that will enable the people to grow economically, socially and environmentally.

From an environmental perspective, we make sure that we do not have noise pollution. We also want to ensure that, regardless of their status, whether they are wealthy or not, traders or students, people have access to electricity all the time. It is then up to them to determine how much electricity they want to use.

Because it is available all the time, people’s lifestyles have been positively impacted. As a young person, you can go home, find electricity available and do your homework. You do not have to light candles, which create health and environmental challenges and risks in family homes and businesses.

Before our arrival, a survey was conducted to assess the potential environmental impact that this project would have on Aba. We took note of the number of generators in the city. By the time the power plant was built, those generators had been displaced, thereby protecting the environment and the health of people who were inhaling fumes from generators, especially in marketplaces and other public places.

Economically, consider what is happening with the cost of diesel. A couple of weeks ago, we experienced gas supply challenges and, for a few days, had issues with power supply. We informed our customers, but when the disruption occurred and they had to buy diesel for those three or four days, their reaction was, “What has happened?”

Once people get used to a 24/7 power supply and experience an interruption, everything seems to have gone wrong. People get used to comfort and are able to plan around it.

How have manufacturers and businesses in Aba benefited from the improved power supply?

Businesses in Aba have been very happy and supportive. We actually made them our priority by ensuring that the power supply to them is reliable.

That meant that some of the equipment and infrastructure we inherited from EEDC and NEPA, which were about 60 years old in some cases, had to be replaced to ensure that areas where we had factories and industries received reliable power.

When we were redesigning the network, we made sure that we included brand-new substations in strategic locations around Aba so that those industries would be able to receive reliable, stable and good-quality power supply through their own substations.

If anything goes wrong in one location, we can loop them through another. That is how the network has been redesigned.

We examined the four or five substations we inherited, which were very old and dilapidated, and rehabilitated them completely, almost as if we were building brand-new substations.

The idea is that our people in Aba should not have to worry about the infrastructure that has been established for the next 50 or 100 years.

Securing funding for a project of this magnitude can be challenging. How instrumental were financial institutions in its completion?

Afreximbank provided the funding that enabled us to complete and commission the project.

Politics and human error got in the way, and the project was delayed when we were just about to commission it. For seven years, we maintained facilities that were already in place. We had staff who had to be paid to continue maintaining those facilities.

When the issues were eventually resolved and we determined the amount needed to complete the project, we were very happy that Afreximbank came in because it saw the vision and understood the impact it would have on the people we were going to serve.

When it saw Aba’s potential as an industrial city in Africa, I believe that made it more willing to find a way to make the project work.

I must also commend Diamond Bank and its late founder, Pascal Dozie, because he saw the vision. At a time when banks were mostly giving short-term loans to traders to import containers, turn over the money and repay their loans, he saw the value of a strategic project like this and the impact it would have on customers in Aba, who are largely traders.

He bought into that long-term vision and provided the initial funds that got us started. We subsequently faced challenges that made the project take much longer to complete, and Afreximbank came to the rescue.

At the time we approached Afreximbank, we needed $50m, and it provided the funding.

Typically, a bank or financier would run away when approached with a project that had the kind of challenges we faced.

There was not much we could offer to give them comfort other than the fact that our boss was a very determined investor. It was not just about investing to make money. The determination to see the project through was largely driven by the desire to make a sustainable impact that would touch lives.

Afreximbank eventually brought in technical advisers and financial experts. We examined the figures and went into the city to speak with customers, especially industrialists, to understand their level of need, commitment and willingness to pay.

We engaged an independent organisation to conduct that survey, and I think the outcome reinforced its belief that this project would make the kind of impact it wanted to make in Africa.

Geometric Power demonstrates the potential of private investment in electricity. What accounts for the shortcomings of Nigeria’s power sector privatisation?

It is a pity that the privatisation ended the way it did. If it had gone according to the original plan, and if we had selected investors, especially at the DisCo level, who had the technical know-how and financial muscle to invest in infrastructure, things could have been different.

For the DisCos, it was not simply about how much you could pay for the asset. It was about how much you could reduce aggregate technical, commercial and collection losses.

If you are able to reduce those losses, it means you would have made substantial investments in infrastructure.

The only way you can achieve that is by building substations, as we have done, and constructing new power lines at the appropriate voltage levels.

If we had stuck to that approach, we would not be in the mess we are in today.

But the answer remains that it is doable. There are things that need to unwind themselves, and that is already happening, although it is taking a lot of time.

How severely have recent gas supply disruptions affected your operations?

Our gas suppliers are dealing with a major challenge because they inherited gas infrastructure that was built in the 1960s.

Imagine having a Volkswagen that was built in the 1960s. Where would you get the spare parts? They have to struggle to find spare parts whenever anything goes wrong. They need to make huge investments, and the government probably needs to do something to help them upgrade the existing infrastructure.

Recently, something went wrong with one of their pieces of equipment. They checked for the part in Nigeria, but it was not available. They mobilised, found it in the United States and flew it in at enormous cost. They took it to the site, tested it, fixed it and immediately started supplying gas to us.

We also have an arrangement with NDPHC. Whenever we have such an emergency, we buy emergency power from NDPHC for that interim period so that we can maintain supply to our customers.

In light of the gas supply challenges, are you exploring renewable energy sources to diversify your power generation?

Yes. Part of our plan is to supply reliable power through gas-fired as well as steam-fired power.

We are also in discussions with people promoting alternative sources of power, including waste and solar energy. One or two people have talked about water, although we do not have a water source close by to test it.

In a nutshell, we are open to innovative ideas that can help us supplement the power supply from here.

What measures should the government take to ease gas supply constraints and attract greater investment in the power sector?

The gas infrastructure is ageing. Some of the infrastructure around here was installed decades ago. That is what suppliers are struggling with because spare parts are not easily available.

We need to figure out how we can engage and support gas providers to produce more gas. A lot of operators focus on extracting oil, but if they can produce more gas, it will help organisations that want to invest in power, as well as industries that want to use gas to drive their machines.

Having installed 141MW, what are Geometric Power’s plans for the next phase of expansion?

It is not just the South-East that will benefit from what we are doing. The power we are supplying here has displaced what used to be supplied to Aba from the national grid, and that power can go to other parts of the country. The more power we provide as we expand our geographical footprint, the more the rest of the country benefits.

At the moment, we have installed 141 megawatts. We have three LM6000 GE turbines, each generating 47 megawatts. We have also built the foundation for the fourth turbine, which will take us to 188 megawatts.

To put it in perspective, Aba currently consumes an average of about 50 megawatts. At one time, consumption was as high as 90 megawatts when we had not metered customers because people were doing all sorts of things.

Since we metered our customers, consumption has changed. People who used to leave their lights on when going to work now switch them off because they know that when they return in the evening, they can turn them back on and nothing in their refrigerators or freezers would have spoilt.

We have also provided electricity to some underserved communities in Rivers State that had been without power for years. We had to liaise with Port Harcourt DisCo because they are its customers and then approach the regulator for permission to serve them.

Going forward, when we designed the power plant, we planned the entire value chain, from gas supply to the power plant, substations, distribution transformers and end users.

Our 12-inch gas pipeline, which runs about 27 kilometres from Owaza to here, was designed to provide enough gas for up to 500 megawatts within this footprint.

So, in addition to the next phase, which would take us to 188 megawatts, if demand grows sufficiently, we can install more turbines and eventually increase capacity to 500 megawatts. That means we can continue to grow the business, and Discos in the South-East and South-South can get power from us more easily.

How practical would it be to replicate the Geometric Power model across other parts of Nigeria?

We have been the guinea pig for the power sector. Anybody else coming to replicate this model, by God’s grace, would never have to go through one-tenth of the challenges we have experienced.

Can the model work? Yes. I would say we should start with cities and locations that are commercially viable, especially places we want to industrialise. Cities such as Kaduna, Kano and Onitsha, as well as industrial corridors in Enugu and Aba, are examples.

If private investors can work with a DisCo, agree on the terms and raise the money, it can work. The money can only be raised if you have willing and capable buyers who can pay for the power and, of course, you have your source of fuel.

It could take up to two years to get a project from financial close to commissioning, but the devil is in the details of the negotiations.
