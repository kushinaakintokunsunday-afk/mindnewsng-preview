Arsenal can win back-to-back titles – African China

Veteran Nigerian artiste, Chinagorom Onuoha, popularly known as African China, reflects on the business evolution of Nigerian music, his favourite club, love for Osimhen and more in this interview with PETER AKINBO

Growing up, what sport did you enjoy?

Growing up, like most young kids across Nigeria, I enjoyed and still enjoy football. I love playing football. I also represented my secondary school in a relay race once, but it obviously was not my calling, as I loved music a lot.

Did your parents ever discourage you from pursuing a career in sports?

They did not need to discourage me because I did not take up any sport as a career.

Were you passionate about football growing up?

I was back then, but not any more, even if I still play once in a while. I became a football lover in the late 1980s, watching Nigerian national teams.

What club do you support?

I support Arsenal.

What do you think of Arsenal’s season so far?

They have been doing well, but they lose games their fans least expect, just like the game against Brighton. I am happy now that we won that league last season, though, and I believe Arsenal stands a good chance of winning the EPL this season again if they can maintain a good league form. I see Liverpool and Manchester City losing more grounds.

If you could spend a day with any sportsperson, who would it be?

Currently, Victor Osimhen.

Who do you think is the Greatest Footballer of all Time?

That is a tough question, but if I am to pick one, I will take Pele.

If any of your children show an interest in sports, would you encourage them?

Of course, yes, it is the right thing to do.

Who do you think is the best African player of all time?

I know some will argue that I might be choosing him because he is Nigerian. Some will say he did not win much during his time playing, but I choose Austin ‘Jay-Jay’ Okocha any time, any day. To me, he is the most naturally skilled footballer I have ever seen.

You became known for songs that spoke directly about Nigerian crises and everyday realities. Looking back at what inspired those songs then, what was the goal?

Those songs were meant to call the attention of Nigerians to stop and reflect—it was more like a wake-up call. In *Crisis*, if you remember, I sang, *”Make we no let this country to fall inside well.” Back then, many people were just dancing to the rhythm without paying close attention to the deeper warning in the message.

What kind of songs do you find yourself listening to today?

Funny enough, I do not really follow many of the newer tracks on my own playlist. Many artists today rely heavily on street slang and lamba, and once that slang fades out, the song fades with it. Conscious music, on the other hand, grows with the listener because it is evergreen. There are still a few young artists who send me their songs, and I am blown away by their talent. Some tracks have great rhythm, though I personally still lean toward music with stronger lyrical substance.

Looking at the new generation of Nigerian artists, what is something they understand about the industry better than the older generation did?

They understand the business side of music, and I really applaud them for that. They took the business from point A to a much safer, more lucrative place. While technology and time naturally improved production, they really stepped up the business structure. When we started, we did it purely for the love of the craft—there was barely any money, just the joy of hearing people call your name on the street.

How much has the public perception of being a musician changed since your early days?

Tremendously. Back then, there was heavy stigma around being a musician; sensible families did not even want their children marrying one because society saw artistes as people without real jobs. Today, entertainment has become one of the highest-paying industries in the country, creating jobs for lawyers, personal assistants, managers, and stage managers, and musicians are respected. Even when we critique lyrics, we still applaud how far they have taken the business.

Looking back at your catalogue, which song gives you the most nostalgia?

Because I am the writer, all my songs give me nostalgia. For the fans, it is usually Crisis. That was my first song and the one that brought African China out—hearing that intro and feeling the crowd’s energy always brings back memories. And then Mr President, with the cock crowing; it was meant to be a wake-up call, just like a rooster crowing at dawn.
