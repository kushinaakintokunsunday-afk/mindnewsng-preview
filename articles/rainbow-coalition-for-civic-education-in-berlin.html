Rainbow coalition for civic education in Berlin

By Punch Punch 16 Nov 2026 7 min read
MINDNEWSNG
Photo: Punch

“We pay a price when we deprive children of the exposure to the values, principles, and education they need to make them good citizens.” ~ Justice Sandra Day O’Connor

 It was a rainbow coalition of sorts! From the North, East, West and South of the world, delegates from 23 countries, including myself, gathered in the German capital, Berlin, to commemorate the 2026 International Civic Education Summit. From November 1–5, the Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung, in collaboration with the Heinrich-Böll-Stiftung and Arolsen Archives, brought together educators, researchers, practitioners, and civil society actors from around the world to explore how participation can be strengthened through civic education. Through the exchange of experiences and innovative approaches, participants examined how democratic engagement can be fostered in diverse political, social, and cultural contexts.

 Delegates from Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America, North America, and the Middle East engaged in bilateral meetings, institutional visits, and discussions with Berlin-based partners from educational institutions, public authorities, foundations, museums, memorial sites, and civil society organisations. The programme focused on how to promote mutual learning, highlight successful practices, and strengthen international networks dedicated to democratic engagement and civic learning.

 Before taking a deep dive into the programmes of the event, what exactly is civic education and why is it very important to democratic consolidation? An independent international expert and researcher on environment and development, Jennifer Rietbergen-McCracken wrote in a paper on CIVICUS Global Alliance online that “Civic education (also known as citizen education or democracy education) can be broadly defined as the provision of information and learning experiences to equip and empower citizens to participate in democratic processes. Civic education can take many forms, including classroom-based learning, informal training, experiential learning, and mass media campaigns. Civic education can be targeted at children or adults, in developed or developing countries and at the local, national or international level. As such, civic education is an approach that employs a range of different methods, and is often used in combination with other participatory governance tools.”

 According to her, the overall goal of civic education is to promote civic engagement and support democratic and participatory governance. Civic education has been used to address a wide variety of political and governance issues (e.g. corruption, civic apathy or post-conflict reconciliation) as well as important social issues (e.g. domestic violence, drug abuse, and HIV/AIDS).

 Whenever I facilitate training on civic education and voter education, I use the analogy: “All accidents are incidents, but not all incidents are accidents.”

” That is to say, “All voter education is civic education, but not all civic education is voter education.

” This is because voter education is just a subset of civic education. In Civics for Life by Sandra Day O’Connor, the author establishes the nexus between civic education and democratic consolidation. It says, “The success of our democracy and society depends on civic education. It equips us with the knowledge, skills, and values necessary to become informed citizens actively participating in our democratic process. Civic education helps us understand our rights and responsibilities as citizens, promotes critical thinking, problem-solving, and responsible action.”

 Another soundbite from Civics for Life says, “The success of a democracy relies heavily on its citizens having the necessary knowledge and skills to participate in the political process. The ability of citizens to make informed decisions, including electing their representatives and understanding the laws that govern them, is the foundation for any functioning democracy. Without an informed citizenry, a democracy cannot endure.”. I couldn’t agree more with that submission.

 Now, back to Berlin, one of the outstanding presentations for me was at the Bundestag, the German Parliament Building, where we met with a Member of Parliament, CDU/CSU parliamentary group, Member of the Internal Affairs Committee, David Gregosz, who, along with the Head of the Domestic Affairs Department, Heinrich Böll Foundation, Bastian Hermisson, walked us through a presentation on “Polarisation and Populism – Democracy under Pressure”. It was very insightful and participatory as we learnt about Germany’s political development.   I also recall the memorable visit to the headquarters of the Heinrich Böll Foundation, where we learnt about the HBS work in the field of civic education and democracy strengthening, with a particular focus on political participation. This session also offers insight into the Foundation’s work on memory culture and its international programming.

 My key takeaway from the engagement at HBS was learning about cultural memory: the constructed understanding of the past passed from one generation to the next through text, oral traditions, monuments, rites, and other symbols. Prof. Gernot Wolfram of the Department of Cultural Management and Cultural Studies at Macromedia University, Berlin, made a presentation on “Innovative Solutions – Participation in Times of AI and Societal Polarisation”. From this presentation, I learnt about the interactive “Wahl-O-Mat,” which informs voters about party policies and helps them decide which party is right for them.

 The actual International Civic Education Summit 2026 was held on Wednesday, November 4, at the KAS Academy. On this day, all 23 delegates had the opportunity to present their projects. I participated in the panel with the theme: “Seats at the Table: Empowering Participation in Democratic Institutions.” The moderator was Shelly Kupferberg. Other members of the panel included: Melanie Walker of Canadian International Council, Canada; Adam Kądziela of Department of Public Policy Coordination, Chancellery of the Prime Minister of Poland; and Beatriz Kipnis of Center for Studies and Debates at Fundação Fernando Henrique Cardoso, Brazil.

  I presented my anti-corruption work, which was done with funding support from the MacArthur Foundation, which culminated in the publication of my third book titled “Nigeria: Corruption and Opacity in Governance”. I also referenced my engagement as a resource person to train the new generation of youth leaders by HBS under the Nigeria Green Academy Program from the 2023 edition to date, as well as the hosting of a television talk show, Development Focus with Jide Ojo on ITV, Abuja, from January 2021 to December 2022. Lastly, I spoke about my ubiquitous media advocacy initiatives, which I am using to mobilize Nigerians to participate in the country’s democratic activities.

  I learnt a lot from the keynote presentation by Christiana Bukalo titled “Who Gets to Participate? Citizenship, Statelessness and the Preconditions of Democracy”.  I learnt that there are approximately 30 million stateless people around the world. You may ask what statelessness is. According to UNHCR, the international legal definition of a stateless person is “a person who is not considered as a national by any State under the operation of its law”. In simple terms, this means that a stateless person does not have the nationality of any country. Some people are born stateless, but others become stateless.

 Statelessness often has a severe and lifelong impact on those it affects. The millions of people around the world who are denied a nationality often fight for the same basic human rights that most of us take for granted. Often, they are excluded from cradle to grave – being denied a legal identity when they are born, access to education, health care, marriage and job opportunities during their lifetime and even the dignity of an official burial and a death certificate when they die. Many pass on statelessness to their children, who then pass it on to the next generation. South African Grammy Award winner, Miriam Makeba (Mama Africa), was said to be stateless for about 30 years when she was exiled by the apartheid government of South Africa.
